Send photons one at a time at a polarizing beam splitter and turn it: in an ideal splitter 100% pass at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, none at 90°; every photon leaves whole by one exit or the other; and the two exits together account for every photon at every angle. Two questions hide inside yours. How do we *know* the power is 2? That is what this experiment measures. Why could it not have been 1, 3 or anything else? That is what the models answer, and the short version is: once amplitudes are the shadows of an arrow that can be turned, the square is the only rule under which the two exits always add up to all the photons. The rest of this answer makes both halves precise and says which parts are measurement, which are mathematics, which are postulates and which are still argued over.

**The set-up.** A source of single photons: a crystal that emits photons in pairs, so that catching one of a pair announces that its partner is on its way; with rare exceptions (two pairs emitted at once) there is then exactly one photon in the apparatus. A polarizer fixed at 0° prepares each photon's polarization. Then a polarizing beam splitter: a cube that sends light polarized along its axis out of one face (call that exit "pass") and light polarized at right angles to its axis out of another face ("reflect"). The splitter can be turned about the beam by an angle θ; in practice one usually turns the photon's polarization instead, with a wave plate in front of a fixed splitter, which gives the same counts. A detector at each exit. Idealizations: a splitter that absorbs nothing and sorts perfectly, detectors that miss nothing, a perfectly prepared polarization. Real splitters leak a little of each polarization into the wrong exit and real detectors miss some photons; the numbers below are the ideal ones, which real single-photon runs match to within a few per cent.

**What is seen.**

(1) Each photon is caught at one exit or the other. The two detectors essentially never click together: far less often than a wave sharing its energy between the two exits would require, and in the ideal case never. (Grangier, Roger and Aspect, 1986, with single photons at a half-silvered mirror, the non-polarizing cousin of our splitter; repeated in undergraduate laboratories since, e.g. Thorn et al., 2004.)

(2) The fraction of photons taking the "pass" exit depends on the angle: 100% at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, 0% at 90°, and in between it changes smoothly. Turning the splitter a little changes the fraction a little. (Measured with heralded photons whose polarization is set by their partner's polarizer, e.g. Dehlinger and Mitchell, 2002.)

(3) Turn the splitter and the counts shift from one exit to the other, but their total does not change: 75 + 25, 50 + 50, 25 + 75. In the ideal splitter every photon is accounted for at every angle; a real one loses a small fraction, and the total stays constant to within that fraction.

(4) With a bright beam instead of single photons, the same fractions describe how the *energy* divides between the exits. Malus measured this in 1809, long before photons were known.

(5) The pieces can be put back together. Lead the two exits along paths of equal length (equal to within a fraction of a wavelength) into a second splitter facing the other way, and the photon comes out polarized at 0°, as it went in: a test polarizer at 0° then passes it every time (ideal set-up). Lengthen one path by half a wavelength and the photon comes out polarized at 60° instead, and the 0° test polarizer passes only a quarter of the photons.

The same "two exits always total 100%, with fractions that change smoothly" holds for an interferometer's two exits as the path difference is changed, and the same law, with half the angle in it, is what spin-½ particles obey when sent through two Stern–Gerlach-type analysers at an angle (75% at 60°, 50% at 90°; why the angle is halved belongs to a different story). The 2 is not a quirk of light.

**What this rules out.** *Light as a classical wave.* A wave carries energy in proportion to the square of its height, and a wave polarized at 0° meeting a splitter at 30° divides into a part of relative height 0.87 along the pass axis and 0.50 along the reflect axis, so its energy divides 75/25. The classical wave gets (2), (3), (4) and (5) right, and already contains a square. It is killed by (1): it would have to send part of every photon's energy out of each exit, and the detectors say each photon is whole. *A particle carrying a hidden pointer.* Suppose each photon carries a definite polarization angle of its own, the splitter sends it out of whichever exit's axis is nearer to that angle, and leaves the angle alone. The 0° polarizer then lets through photons whose pointers lie within 45° of 0°, spread over that range; a splitter at θ passes those within 45° of θ; the overlap of the two ranges shrinks in a straight line: 67% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 33% at 60°. Killed by (2): the measured 75% and 25% are not on that line. (A pointer rule can be tuned to fit any single-photon fractions, but then it merely records them; for *pairs* of photons, Bell tests rule out every rule in which each photon's exit depends only on its own pointer and its own splitter. That is a separate story.) What survives is a model in which the thing the splitter handles is an arrow, the exits are two directions at right angles, and the two pieces are the arrow's shadows on them: in (5) the pieces re-add to the original arrow, and reversing the sign of one of them (the half-wavelength delay) re-adds to a different arrow, exactly as the two components of an arrow would.

**Model 1: states as arrows, chances as squared shadows.** This is standard quantum mechanics in its state-vector form, applied to a system with two outcomes. *Intuition.* The photon's polarization is an arrow of length 1 lying in the plane across the beam. A splitter at angle θ offers two directions at right angles, pass and reflect. The amplitude for each exit is the arrow's shadow on that direction: the length you get by dropping a perpendicular from the arrow's tip onto the line, counted as negative if it lands on the far side of the centre. For a 0° arrow and a splitter at 30° the shadows are 0.87 on pass and 0.50 on reflect; at 45°, 0.71 and 0.71; at 60°, 0.50 and 0.87. The chance of each exit is the square of its shadow: 0.75 and 0.25; 0.50 and 0.50; 0.25 and 0.75. After leaving an exit, the photon's arrow lies along that exit's direction, which is why three polarizers at 0°, 45° and 90° pass a quarter of the photons (0.5 × 0.5) while 0° and 90° alone pass none. For light polarized in a circle or an ellipse, and for the electron amplitudes of the two-slit answer, the components are clock hands rather than plain positive-or-negative numbers; "squared size" then means the squared length of the hand, and everything below goes through unchanged. *Why this model.* One picture gives every number in (2) to (5): the fractions, the constant total, the re-adding, the 60° after a sign reversal. It is the two-slit answer's "combine first, square after" with the two slits' amplitudes replaced by two shadows. *Pros.* The arrow idea, with more directions, is the state-vector form of the whole theory: spin, energy levels, qubits. *Cons.* As a textbook presents it, the square is a postulate: Born's rule. In Born's 1926 paper the text has the probability given by the amplitude itself, and a footnote added in proof says that more careful consideration shows it is proportional to the square. The rule was put in because it fits, which is exactly the gap you noticed. The next three accounts fill it, at rising strength.

**Why 2, account 1: Pythagoras.** *(Status: established mathematics, given that the pieces are the shadows of an arrow of length 1.)* Draw the arrow and its two shadows: they form a right triangle whose longest side is the arrow. Pythagoras says the squares of the two short sides add to the square of the long side, which is 1, at every angle: 0.75 + 0.25 = 1 at 30°, 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 at 45°. That is observation (3): all the photons, at every angle. Now try the plain size instead. At 30°, 0.87 + 0.50 = 1.37; at 45°, 0.71 + 0.71 = 1.41: 141% of the photons, when the two exits are the only places a photon can go. Cubes: at 30°, 0.649 + 0.125 = 0.774; at 45°, 0.35 + 0.35 = 0.71: 29% of the photons unaccounted for, though (3) says they are all there. Fourth power: 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5 at 45°. Of all the plain powers, only the second keeps the total at exactly 1 for every angle; any smaller power overshoots at every angle between 0° and 90°, any larger one undershoots, worst at 45°.

*Why this account.* It turns "why 2" into geometry: two outcomes that exclude each other and exhaust the possibilities must have chances adding to 1, and squared shadows are what add to 1. *Pros.* Nothing but a right triangle. *Cons.* "Only" here means only among plain powers. With just two exits, a determined sceptic can write down a rule that is not a power and still totals 1: take the squared shadow and add a small wiggle to each exit's chance, with the wiggle chosen so that the one for the pass exit always cancels the one for the reflect exit. Measurements like (2) push any such wiggle below their error bars but cannot make it exactly zero. Closing that gap takes the next two accounts.

**Why 2, account 2: the arrow can be turned.** *(Status: established mathematics, given one rule carried over from the two-slit answer: amplitudes add.)* Two facts. First, amplitudes add. In the two-slit answer the two slits' amplitudes were combined before the chance was taken; here the two shadows re-add to the arrow in (5). The precise form of the rule: whatever a splitter, a wave plate or a slit does to an arrow that is the sum of two others, it does to each part and then adds the results. Second, the arrow can be turned smoothly, by as small an angle as you like, with the two exits still totalling 100% afterwards. Keep the splitter fixed and put a tube of sugar water in the beam: it turns the polarization by an angle that grows with the length of the tube, and the counts at the two exits change smoothly as the tube is lengthened while, in the ideal lossless case, their total stays put (observation (3), with the photon turned instead of the splitter). Now a theorem. Suppose the chance of an exit is the p-th power of the size of its component, for some power p, and changes act on sums part by part. Then for every p other than 2, the only changes that keep the chances totalling 100% for every possible arrow are swapping the labels of the directions and reversing signs (for clock-hand components, turning each hand on the spot): jumps, not turns. For p = 2 the chance-keeping changes are exactly the turns and mirror flips (for clock-hand components, their generalization, the unitary changes), and you can turn by as little as you like. One exception: p = 1 with components that are never negative. Then the components are the chances themselves, nothing ever cancels, and the dark stripes of the two-slit answer, where opening a slit removed hits, rule that world out. *Why this account.* It explains not just "not 1, not 3" but "not 1.9, not 2.1": a world whose amplitudes add, cancel and can be turned a little at a time has to use the square. *Pros.* It ties the power to the two features that make quantum mechanics quantum, cancelling and continuous change. *Cons.* It rests on "changes act on sums part by part". Drop that and alternatives can be cooked up, for instance by taking the square rule and renaming each amplitude as its own square root, which makes it look like a fourth-power rule; but renamed amplitudes no longer add, and the two-slit stripes need amplitudes that do.

*Sources:* Aaronson, *Quantum Computing since Democritus* (2013), ch. 9 "Quantum" (why the 2-norm: only p = 1 and p = 2 allow non-trivial continuous changes).

**Why 2, account 3: Gleason's theorem.** *(Status: established mathematics; its one physical assumption is a postulate, but a weaker one than the square rule itself.)* Go to three or more exits. A photon's polarization alone offers only two, but a photon that could be on either of two paths, each with two polarizations, offers four, and a spin-1 atom in a Stern–Gerlach magnet offers three. The model: outcomes are directions in the arrow's space; outcomes that exclude each other are directions at right angles; a measurement is a complete set of such directions; one of them must happen, so their chances total 100%. The assumption: the chance of a given direction depends only on that direction and on the arrow, not on which other directions you have grouped it with, so two measurements that share a direction give that direction the same chance. Gleason proved in 1957 that, for arrow spaces with three or more mutually perpendicular directions, the only chance rules satisfying this are the squared shadow of the arrow and, for an arrow you know only partly, averages of squared shadows; adding the observed fact that a photon prepared along a direction passes that direction every time picks out the squared shadow itself. This is not "only among powers": any rule at all, however contrived, is excluded. The wiggle of account 1 dies as soon as a third direction can be swapped in and the chances have to stay consistent across every regrouping. For a system with only two exits Gleason's theorem does not apply; Busch showed in 2003 that the conclusion returns if the assumption is extended to measurements that do not sort perfectly, like a real leaky splitter. *Why this account.* It is the most general: once outcomes are directions and chances are consistent across all the ways of measuring, the square is not a free choice. *Pros.* Forces the power from consistency alone. *Cons.* Critics note that the assumption does most of the work: "depends only on its own direction" is itself a postulate, and Bell pointed out in 1966 that experiment does not force it, since the whole arrangement of the apparatus might matter. The theorem also takes the arrow-and-directions structure as given rather than explaining it, and says nothing about why there are chances at all or what counts as a measurement.

*Sources:* Gleason, *J. Math. Mech.* 6, 885 (1957); Busch, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 91, 120403 (2003); Bell, *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 38, 447 (1966), on the assumption behind Gleason's theorem.

**Where the interpretations stand.** All of them keep the square and agree on every number above; they differ on what the chance is a chance *of*, and on whether the square is a postulate, a derived fact or a stable condition. Everything in this section is contested.

*Copenhagen-style.* The square is a postulate; Gleason's theorem shows it is the only consistent one given the structure, and there the explanation stops. Pro: minimal. Con: critics object that a postulate about measurement outcomes leaves "measurement" undefined.

*Many-worlds.* The wave never collapses: both exits happen, in different branches, and "chance" has to be reconstructed. Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) argue that an agent who knows the branches and bets consistently must bet on them by their squared sizes; Zurek (2003) derives the square from a symmetry of entangled states (if swapping the two branches on the photon's side can be undone by a swap on its partner's side, leaving the joint state unchanged, the two branches must be equally likely, and unequal cases are built from equal pieces). Critics object that these derivations assume what they need, such as that branches should be weighed at all or weighed by size, and that in a theory where every outcome happens it is unclear what a chance is; supporters reply that the assumptions are requirements of rationality or symmetry, not the rule itself.

*Pilot-wave.* Particles always have positions and are steered by the wave (worked out for electrons and atoms; the version for light is less settled). A mathematical fact: if positions are spread according to the squared size of the wave at one moment, the steering keeps them spread that way for ever, while a spread according to the plain size or the cube would not in general stay that way. Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì (1992) argue that for a typical starting condition of the universe the square holds anyway, and in computer runs (Valentini and Westman, 2005) other spreads usually relax towards it, as a gas relaxes to a uniform temperature, though not for every wave. So here the square is an equilibrium rather than a postulate. Other powers are possible in principle as "non-equilibrium" starting conditions; Valentini has proposed looking for relics of them from the early universe, and none have been found so far. Critics debate whether "typical" explains or merely restates.

*Objective collapse.* The wave is real and localizes itself at random, and in the collapse law the chance that the wave settles at a place is its squared size there, so the square is built in. There is a reason it has to be: Gisin showed in 1989 that, given the standard description of entangled pairs, a random collapse weighted by anything other than the squared size would let two people sharing entangled particles signal to each other faster than light. So in these theories the square is fixed by the ban on such signalling. Pro: a reason that comes from relativity rather than from a postulate about measurement. Con: new constants of nature, and the small departures from standard quantum mechanics that these theories predict have been searched for and not found so far, which has ruled out some versions and narrowed the room for the rest.

**How the accounts relate.** The three "why 2" accounts are one geometric story told with rising rigour. Pythagoras shows that, among plain powers, only the square keeps two exits adding up to all the photons; the turning argument shows that a world where amplitudes add, cancel and change smoothly has no other option, non-integer powers included; Gleason's theorem shows that, with three or more directions and chances that stay consistent across every way of grouping them, no rule other than the squared shadow survives at all. Each is established mathematics; what each assumes is the arrow-and-directions structure itself, which is where the postulates sit. The classical wave carries the same square as energy, and Born borrowed the square from that analogy; the wave function and the sum over paths of the two-slit answer are the arrow idea with many directions instead of two, and inherit the square unchanged. The interpretations agree on every count in (1) to (5); they differ on why there are arrows at all and what the chances are chances of. No experiment has told the first three apart, while objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose small departures are still being searched for. So the honest status of your "why exactly 2" is this: measured to a few per cent, forced by consistency once states are turnable arrows, and still argued over at the level of what the arrows are.
