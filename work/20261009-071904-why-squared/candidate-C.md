There is an experiment built for exactly your question: cut three slits instead of two, open them one at a time, two at a time and all three, and the square rule makes a prediction that the plain size, the cube and every other power get wrong.

First, what the two-slit stripes do and don't settle. Answer A's rule was: each slit sends an amplitude to each spot on the screen (a number that can be positive or negative; in the full theory a small clock hand); add the two first; the chance of a dot is proportional to the squared size of the total. The stripes force the "add first" part: chances can't cancel, so something that can cancel must be combined before the chance is worked out. They do not, by themselves, force the "squared" part. If the chance were the plain size of the total (what mathematicians call the modulus), or its cube, you would still get stripes, bright where the two hands agree and dark where they cancel; only the shape of the stripes would differ. Reading the power off the shape is possible, but it needs a detailed model of the slits. Three slits give a yes-or-no test that needs no such model.

**The experiment.** A plate with three narrow parallel slits, call them A, B and C, equal in width and equally spaced. In front of it a second plate, a mask with openings, which can be moved to leave open any chosen slits. Eight settings: all three blocked, A alone, B alone, C alone, the pairs AB, BC and AC, and all three. Light goes through: a faint laser beam in one series of runs and, in another, single photons made one at a time (each announced by a partner photon, so the counting is only consulted when one photon is known to be on its way). Single-photon detectors count arrivals at a chosen spot on the far side, and the counting is repeated at several spots. The all-blocked setting measures the background, stray light and detector noise, which is subtracted from the other seven. Done this way by Sinha, Couteau, Jennewein, Laflamme and Weihs in 2010.

**What is seen** (established results). (1) Each detection is one click. The pair settings show stripes across the spots, the familiar two-slit pattern; the three-slit setting shows a different striped pattern. (2) Take the three pair counts added together, subtract the three single counts added together, and compare with the three-slit count at the same spot. Call the difference the leftover (physicists call it the three-way, or third-order, interference term). At every spot tested the leftover was zero within the measurement error, and the error bars put any real leftover below about one hundredth of the two-way interference, that is, of the strength of the stripes themselves. (3) The same holds for the faint laser and for the single photons. Since then the same null result has been found with photons in a five-path interferometer (2017, with a tighter bound) and with large molecules (2017, within that experiment's precision).

One piece of fine print, so that nothing here is overstated. The prediction "leftover exactly zero" rests on an idealization: that the wave coming through slit A at the screen is the same whether or not B and C are open. For real slits it is not quite the same: an open neighbour changes the edge conditions slightly, and a wave can also take looped routes, in through A, back out through B, in again through C. Standard quantum mechanics, square rule included, therefore predicts a tiny leftover for real slits, smaller than the 2010 experiment could have seen (it was calculated with the sum over paths in 2014); an experiment designed to make the looped routes much stronger measured such a leftover in 2016, of the size the theory gives for that set-up. So the exact statement is: with independent slit waves the square rule says the leftover is exactly zero, and with real slit waves it says the leftover is tiny; both halves have been checked. The tiny leftover is the square rule applied more carefully, not a departure from it.

*Sources:* Sinha, Couteau, Jennewein, Laflamme & Weihs, *Science* 329, 418 (2010); Kauten et al., *New J. Phys.* 19, 033017 (2017); Cotter et al., *Sci. Adv.* 3, e1602478 (2017); Sawant, Samuel, Sinha, Sinha & Sinha, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 113, 120406 (2014); Magaña-Loaiza et al., *Nat. Commun.* 7, 13987 (2016).

**What the classical pictures say.** Tiny balls, each through one slit: chances just add, so no stripes in any setting, and "three-slit count = pairs added minus singles added" holds trivially, because both sides are then just the three single counts. The three-slit test does not rule balls out; the stripes in the pair settings do, as in answer A. A classical wave, such as light treated as a wave: its energy at a spot goes as the square of the wave height, so it passes the add-and-subtract test too, and the faint laser beam in the 2010 runs is, to a good approximation, exactly such a wave. What rules the classical wave out is whole arrivals: a single photon sent at a half-silvered mirror is registered on one side or the other, and the two detectors click together far less often than any wave, which would have to split, could manage (Grangier, Roger and Aspect, 1986); the single-photon runs of the three-slit test use photons of this kind, one at a time; for electrons, the single whole dots of answer A say the same. So the three-slit test is not about particle-versus-wave. It is about the power. The rules it was built to catch were sorted by Sorkin in 1994 according to what kind of interference they contain: classical chances have none; the square rule has two-way interference and nothing beyond; the plain size, the cube, the fourth power, and mixtures such as "the square plus a dash of the fourth power" all contain genuine three-way interference, and the three-slit test is designed to see it.

*Sources:* Sorkin, *Mod. Phys. Lett. A* 9, 3119 (1994); Grangier, Roger & Aspect, *Europhys. Lett.* 1, 173 (1986).

**Model 1: amplitudes with the square rule.** This is answer A's wave function (the state-vector formulation uses the same rule): each slit sends a clock hand to each spot; add the hands tip to tail; the chance is proportional to the squared length of the total. Here is the intuition for why the square has no three-way term. For hands that all point the same way, with lengths a, b and c, the squared total is the area of a square with side a + b + c. Cut it into three rows and three columns (figure below): on the diagonal sit three squares with sides a, b and c, one per slit; off the diagonal sit six rectangles, a by b twice, a by c twice, b by c twice, each involving exactly two slits. No piece involves all three. The pair AB on its own gives the 2-by-2 block in one corner: the a-square, the b-square and the two a-by-b rectangles. So adding the three pair blocks counts each diagonal square twice and each rectangle once; subtracting the three diagonal squares once leaves exactly the full grid, the three-slit chance. That is the add-and-subtract rule, and it holds at every spot, at any screen distance and for any slit shape, as long as the slit waves are independent (the idealization above). When the hands point in different directions the same bookkeeping holds: each rectangle is scaled by how well its two hands align, from the full area when they point the same way, through zero at right angles, to minus the full area when they point opposite ways, and there is still no piece that involves three slits.

Now the arithmetic at two spots on the far screen, in relative units in which each slit alone sends a hand of length 1 (the slow fall-off of each slit's own band is the same for all three slits at a given spot, so it does not affect any comparison below). At the centre of the pattern all three hands point the same way: the three-slit chance is 3² = 9; the pairs give 2² + 2² + 2² = 12; the singles 1 + 1 + 1 = 3; and 12 − 3 = 9, no leftover. Half a stripe out, at the dark line of the pair AB, the hands from A and C point opposite to the hand from B (−1, +1, −1): the three-slit chance is (−1)² = 1; the pairs give 0 + 0 + 2² = 4 (AB and BC cancel, A and C agree); the singles 3; and 4 − 3 = 1, no leftover. Now the other rules. Plain size, at the centre: 3 against 6 − 3 = 3, it passes; half a stripe out: 1 against (0 + 0 + 2) − 3 = −1, it fails, and the "prediction" is even negative. Cube, at the centre: 27 against 24 − 3 = 21, fails; half a stripe out: 1 against 8 − 3 = 5, fails. Fourth power, at the centre: 81 against 48 − 3 = 45, fails. The plain size passes wherever all the hands line up, because there it is just the lengths added; away from those spots it generally fails, as it does half a stripe out. The cube and every higher power fail even at the centre. Only the square agrees at both spots, and the figure shows that it agrees everywhere.

Why this model: two-way interference present (the pair stripes) and three-way interference absent (zero leftover) is the signature of the square and of no other plain power, and the measurement shows exactly that signature. Pros: it turns "why 2?" from a matter of taste into a measurement with a yes-or-no answer that does not depend on the slit shapes, the wavelength or the distance to the screen; and "pairwise only" is also what keeps calculations manageable, since every interference effect, however many alternatives there are, is a sum of conversations between pairs. Cons: the test shows that the world has the square's signature, but not why; in the textbooks the square is still a postulate, the Born rule (Born's 1926 paper first said the chance is proportional to the amplitude, and a footnote added in proof corrected it to the square); and the clean zero needs the independent-slit-waves idealization, the real prediction being "tiny".

*Sources:* Feynman, Leighton & Sands, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. III (2010), ch. 3 "Probability Amplitudes", §3-1 "The laws for combining amplitudes"; Born, *Z. Phys.* 37, 863 (1926).

**Model 2: the sum over paths.** The same statement in Feynman's language: a hand for every route from source to spot, added tip to tail, squared length at the end. Grouping every route through A into one hand, every route through B into a second and every route through C into a third is exact, and the grid picture then applies to the three group hands. Why this model: it is the same theory as model 1 in different mathematics, so it predicts the same zero; you reach for it when it is easier to think with. Pro: it is the natural language for the fine print above, because the looped routes (in through A, back out through B, in through C) are simply more routes in the sum, and that is how the tiny real leftover was calculated in 2014. Con: for the power itself it adds nothing; it inherits the square as a rule.

*Sources:* Feynman, *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985), ch. 2 "Photons: Particles of Light"; Sawant et al. (2014), above.

**Model 3: what the mathematics says** (established mathematics, not interpretation). The three-slit null, if it held exactly for every possible combination of three hands (the experiment checks a whole family of combinations, spot by spot across the pattern), pins the rule down almost completely. A chance rule that has no three-way term for any combination, has no wild jumps, gives zero chance for a zero hand and is never negative must be quadratic: built only from squares and rectangles of the hand's two components (its across part and its up-down part). If, in addition, the chance depends only on how long the hand is and not on which way it points, the rule is a constant times the squared length. That is the square, with no room left for anything else.

Gleason's theorem (1957) reaches the same word, quadratic, from the blackboard, without any slits. In the state-vector formulation the whole situation of the photon or electron is one arrow of length 1 in a space with a separate perpendicular direction for each outcome the detector could register (a click at spot 1, a click at spot 2, and so on). The amplitude for an outcome is the arrow's shadow on that outcome's direction, and the Born rule says the chance is the squared shadow. Pythagoras shows why the square fits: the squared shadows along perpendicular directions add up to the squared length of the arrow, 1, for every arrow, so the chances always add to 100% whichever way the arrow points. Gleason showed the converse: if chances over any complete set of perpendicular directions add to 100%, if each outcome's chance depends only on the arrow and that outcome's own direction (not on which other outcomes are being told apart alongside it), and if there are at least three outcomes, then the chance must be the squared shadow (more precisely, a quadratic formula that reduces to the squared shadow for a definite arrow and also covers mixtures). Not the plain shadow, not its cube: nothing else is consistent. Busch extended this in 2003 to two-outcome systems when generalized measurements are allowed.

Why this model: it says the exponent is not a dial that could have been set elsewhere; given that states are arrows and outcomes are directions, 2 is forced, and the lab's "no three-way term" and the blackboard's "squared shadow" are two sightings of the same structure. Pros: it reduces the Born rule to weaker assumptions and makes the square look inevitable rather than chosen. Cons: "depends only on its own direction" (non-contextuality) is itself an assumption, not a theorem; the picture of arrows and perpendicular directions is taken for granted, so the theorem explains the exponent but not why the world is described by arrows at all; and it does not say why there are chances rather than certainties.

*Sources:* Gleason, *J. Math. Mech.* 6, 885 (1957); Busch, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 91, 120403 (2003).

**Where the interpretations stand.** All four below predict the same patterns and the same zero leftover for independent slit waves; they differ on what the square is, and every position here is contested, none settled.

*Copenhagen-style.* The square is a postulate, as in the textbooks: the theory is a recipe for chances, and the recipe contains the square because it works. Pro: honest and minimal. Con: critics object that it leaves the most striking rule in physics unexplained.

*Many-worlds.* Every outcome happens, each in its own branch, and the square must then be derived as the right way to weight branches. Everett gave an argument of this kind in 1957; Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) derive the square from rules of rational betting plus the symmetries of the branching; Zurek (2003–2005) derives it from a symmetry of entangled states. Pro, if the derivations hold: the square is explained rather than posited. Critics object that the derivations assume things equivalent to what they prove, or that "chance" has no clear meaning when every outcome occurs; supporters reply that the assumptions are weaker than the Born rule.

*Pilot-wave.* The particle always has a position, steered by the wave, and chance comes from not knowing the starting point. The square appears as an equilibrium: a cloud of possible positions spread out as the squared size of the wave is carried by the steering rule into a cloud spread out as the squared size of the later wave, for ever; a cloud spread as the plain size or the cube is, in general, carried into something else. Pro: the square gets a familiar meaning, like the settled distribution of a gas. Why the actual world's cloud is the squared one is argued either from typicality (for the overwhelming majority of possible starting conditions of the universe it comes out that way; Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì, 1992) or from relaxation towards equilibrium (Valentini), which would allow departures from the square in the early universe; proposed signatures of such departures have been looked for without a detection so far. Critics call the typicality argument contested.

*Objective collapse.* The wave really localizes at random, and the square is written into the collapse law itself (Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber, 1986): the localizations land with chances given by squared sizes. Why that weighting and not another: Gisin showed (1989–1990) that if the collapse picked outcomes using any other weighting of the amplitudes, entangled pairs could be used to send signals faster than light; so within these theories the square is forced by no-signalling. Pro: a physical reason for the exponent. Con: the theories add new constants of nature, and their predicted departures from standard quantum mechanics have been searched for and not found so far.

A* 71, 052105 (2005); Dürr, Goldstein & Zanghì, *J. Stat. Phys.* 67, 843 (1992); Valentini & Westman, *Proc. R. Soc. A* 461, 253 (2005); Ghirardi, Rimini & Weber, *Phys. Rev. D* 34, 470 (1986); Gisin, *Helv. Phys. Acta* 62, 363 (1989) and *Phys. Lett. A* 143, 1 (1990); Maudlin, *Philosophy of Physics: Quantum Theory* (2019).

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2 are one theory in two sets of clothes, with the same square, and the three-slit test checks both at once. Model 3 is mathematics about that theory: it shows that "pairwise only" and "squared shadow" are the same statement, and that within the arrow picture no other power is even consistent. The interpretations keep every number and the zero leftover; they disagree only about the status of the square: a postulate, a derived fact, an equilibrium, or a weighting forced by no-signalling in a slightly changed law. What stays open is why chances have this quadratic, arrow-and-shadow structure at all, and whether a sharper three-slit test could ever find a genuine leftover. If one did, every model above would be in trouble at once, which is the best reason the experiment keeps being repeated.
