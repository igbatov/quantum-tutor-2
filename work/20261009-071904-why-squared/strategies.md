# Strategies: why exactly the *square* of the amplitude?

Learner: conceptual level, numerate (asked "not modulus, not 3rd power"), wants experiment-first, zero false simplifications, words and pictures, simple arithmetic at most. Follow-up to the two-slit answer A ("what-is-qm-v2"), which asserted "combine first, square after" without saying why the power is 2.

What a correct answer must separate:
- **Established experiment:** polarizer pass-fractions (cos² law) for single photons; the shape, peak height and bookkeeping of two-slit stripes; the three-slit (Sorkin) null test of pairwise-only interference.
- **Established mathematics, given the standard structure:** Pythagoras (squares of components add to the fixed squared length at every angle); the Schrödinger equation conserves the total of squared sizes and no other power; Gleason's theorem (1957; Busch 2003 for two-outcome systems with generalized measurements): if each outcome's chance depends only on that outcome's own direction, the rule must be the square; "only the 2-norm and the 1-norm admit non-trivial continuous norm-preserving linear maps" (why a continuously turnable state forces 2 rather than any other power).
- **Postulate:** in textbook quantum mechanics the Born rule is a postulate (Born 1926, the footnote "more careful consideration shows that the probability is proportional to the square").
- **Contested derivations:** many-worlds (Deutsch–Wallace decision theory, Zurek's envariance), pilot-wave (quantum equilibrium / typicality; Valentini's non-equilibrium), objective collapse (the square is built into the collapse law; Gisin's no-signalling argument says any other choice lets you signal faster than light).

Trap to avoid in every candidate: for a two-way split alone, "the square is the only rule that always sums to 100%" is true only *among plain powers*; contrived non-power rules also sum to 100% for two outcomes. Say "among plain powers", or lean on Gleason (three or more outcomes) or on turning-plus-mixing. Also: the Sorkin test needs the idealization "each slit's wave is unchanged by whether the others are open"; standard quantum mechanics itself predicts a tiny leftover from looped paths (seen by Magaña-Loaiza et al. 2016), so "exactly zero" must be qualified.

## Candidate plans

1. **Polarizers at several angles, single photons (Malus's law), Pythagoras.** Pass-fractions 100/75/50/25/0 % at 0/30/45/60/90°, and the two exits of a polarizing beam splitter account for every photon at every angle. Amplitude = shadow (component) of a unit arrow on the exit's direction; squares of the two shadows add to 1 at every angle (Pythagoras); plain sizes add to 1.41 at 45°, cubes to 0.71. Then Gleason for ≥3 outcomes. Fits perfectly: two outcomes, arithmetic only, a picture the learner can redraw; new experiment for this learner. **Fit 5.**

2. **Count the electrons: two-slit shape and bookkeeping.** Builds on answer A's own figures: the stripes are rounded at the dark lines (not V-shaped cusps), the central stripe is 4× the one-slit value there, and (ideal set-up) both slits open catch exactly twice as many electrons as one. Only the square moves electrons around without creating or losing any (average of the square over a stripe is exactly half its peak); plain size would lose about a third of them, cube would invent extra. Then: the Schrödinger equation conserves the total of squares and no other power (a spreading cloud). Continuity with A; conservation is the most physical "why". **Fit 5.**

3. **Three slits: Sorkin's add-and-subtract test (Sinha et al. 2010).** Seven runs (each slit alone, each pair, all three): the square rule says the three-slit pattern is fully predicted from singles and pairs, because a square has only pairwise cross terms; plain size and cube leave a three-way leftover. Measured leftover: zero within about 1% of the stripe strength (later tests tighter). The one experiment designed for the learner's question; arithmetic only. Slightly more bookkeeping to follow. **Fit 5.**

4. **Stern–Gerlach at several angles (spin-½ atoms).** Same logic as plan 1 but with half-angles (75% at 60°, 50% at 90°). The half-angle is an extra oddity that distracts from "why 2", and the clean quantitative angle-sequence data are less canonical than polarizers. **Fit 3. Rejected** in favour of plan 1 (same structure, cleaner numbers); mention in one line there.

5. **Bright-beam correspondence: Malus (1809) and Taylor (1909) feeble light; photon count rate ∝ classical intensity ∝ (wave height)².** Explains where the 2 came from historically (Born's analogy with light) and why classical waves already carry a square (energy is quadratic because restoring force is proportional to displacement). Alone it does not reach electrons and risks circularity (square-law detectors). **Fit 3. Folded into plan 2** as the classical-wave candidate and as Born's own route.

6. **Bell-test correlations (cos² of the angle difference) as the experiment, then the interpretations' derivations as the models.** The contested derivations are exactly what the request asks to label, but Bell tests are heavier than needed and the question is about one photon's chance. **Fit 3. Rejected as a lead;** each chosen strategy carries a short, labelled "where the interpretations stand" segment instead.

7. **Interferometer exits (Mach–Zehnder with single photons, Grangier 1986; neutron interferometer, Rauch 1974): the two exits always add to 100% at every phase setting.** Correct and clean, but it is plan 1 in different clothes (two outcomes, Pythagoras). **Fit 4. Rejected as separate;** one sentence in plan 1 notes the same bookkeeping holds in an interferometer.

8. **Spectral line intensities (transition rates ∝ squared matrix elements).** Needs Fermi's golden rule; too technical for the conceptual level and the square is less visible. **Fit 2. Rejected.**

9. **Half-silvered mirror 50/50 with single photons (Grangier, Roger & Aspect 1986).** The simplest instance of the square (two amplitudes of size 0.707), but the 0.707 is inferred, not seen, so it cannot carry an argument alone. **Fit 3. Folded into plan 1** (45° polarizer = a 50/50 split).

## Chosen: plans 1, 2, 3

Different experiments (polarizer / two slits / three slits), different lead accounts (geometry and Gleason / conservation and the wave equation / pairwise-only structure and Sorkin's hierarchy), different figures (arrow with shadows and flatness curves / stripe profiles under three rules and a spreading cloud / seven-run patterns and the leftover).

### Strategy 1: Pythagoras at the polarizer

**Experiments.** Photons sent one at a time (heralded single photons, as in undergraduate single-photon labs and Bell-test set-ups) at a polarizing beam splitter whose axis is turned by an angle θ from the photons' polarization. Observed: each photon leaves by one exit, never split; pass-fractions close to 100% at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, 0% at 90° (ideal lossless splitter; real ones leak and absorb a little); the two exits together account for every photon at every angle. Classical bright light gives the same fractions of the *energy* (Malus, 1809). One line: the same "two exits always add to 100%" holds in an interferometer at every phase setting.

**Classical candidates ruled out.** Classical wave: gets cos² for energy, but would split each photon's energy between exits; ruled out by whole-photon arrivals (Grangier, Roger & Aspect 1986 anticorrelation). Classical particle with a hidden polarization angle and a "nearest axis wins" rule: predicts a straight-line law (67% at 30°, 33% at 60°); ruled out by the measured 75%/25%.

**Accounts.** (1) The arrow model of states and measurements, Born rule as postulate: state = unit arrow; the two exits = two perpendicular directions; amplitude = the arrow's shadow on each direction (cos θ and sin θ, simple right-triangle arithmetic); chance = the squared shadow. Why this model: it reproduces every fraction above with one picture; cons: the square is put in by hand. (2) Why 2 and not 1 or 3, inside the arrow model (established mathematics): Pythagoras says the squared shadows add to the squared length, 1, at every angle; plain sizes add to 1.41 at 45°, cubes to 0.71, and among plain powers only 2 stays at 100% for all θ. Stronger form: a state you can turn continuously (rotate the splitter, or a wave plate) while chances stay at 100% is possible only for the square (for any other power, the only chance-preserving changes are relabellings and sign flips; the plain size is the one other exception and gives ordinary classical probability with no interference). Caveat stated: for two outcomes alone, contrived non-power rules exist. (3) Gleason's theorem (1957) and Busch (2003): with three or more distinguishable outcomes (a photon with polarization and position already has them), if each outcome's chance depends only on that outcome's own direction and the chances over any complete set of perpendicular directions add to 1, the rule must be the squared shadow; the "depends only on its own direction" assumption (non-contextuality) is a postulate, weaker than the Born rule. Pros: forces the exponent from consistency; cons: assumes outcomes are directions and does not say why there are chances at all. (4) Where the interpretations stand (one or two sentences each, labelled contested): Copenhagen-style keeps it a postulate; many-worlds claims derivations (Deutsch–Wallace, Zurek), critics object to circularity; pilot-wave: the square is an equilibrium distribution, with possible departures (Valentini) not seen; objective collapse: built into the collapse law, and Gisin's argument shows any other choice would allow faster-than-light signalling.

**Relations.** (1)–(3) are one geometric story at rising rigour: given that states are turnable arrows and outcomes directions, 2 is forced; why arrows at all is the open part, and that is where the interpretations differ while agreeing on every number.

**Figure request** `a-pythagoras`: left panel, a unit arrow at θ = 30° with its two shadows on perpendicular axes "pass" and "reflect", the right triangle marked 0.87 and 0.50 with squares 0.75 and 0.25 adding to 1; right panel, curves of (cos θ)^p + (sin θ)^p against θ from 0° to 90° for p = 1 (peaks at 1.41), 2 (flat at 1), 3 (dips to 0.71), with the measured-style points 100/75/50/25/0 % lying on cos²θ in an inset or as a second axis. Notice: only p = 2 is flat.

**Opening sentence.** "Send photons one at a time at a polarizing beam splitter and turn it: in an ideal splitter 100% pass at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, none at 90°, every photon leaves whole by one exit or the other, and the two exits together account for every photon at every angle."

### Strategy 2: Count the electrons

**Experiments.** The two-slit set-up of answer A (one electron at a time; ideal far screen, lossless, screen catches everything that passes the slits), now read quantitatively: (i) the stripes are rounded at the dark lines and at the bright centres (a smooth bowl, not a V-shaped cusp); (ii) at the centre, the rate with both slits open is four times the rate with one slit (measured routinely with light in Young's set-up; the electron patterns of Bach et al. 2013 fit the same rule); (iii) bookkeeping: both slits open catch exactly twice as many electrons as one slit in the ideal set-up, none created, none lost (charge conservation; in the ideal model this is exact because the two slit waves do not overlap at the wall). Plus a second observation: a free electron or neutron cloud spreads as it travels, and the total number detected does not change as it spreads.

**Classical candidates.** Classical particles: chances add, no exponent arises; ruled out by spots that go dark when a slit is opened (answer A). Classical waves (water, string, light): already carry a square, because a wave's energy is quadratic in its height (restoring force proportional to displacement), so Malus's cos² law and Young's 4× peak are classical; ruled out by single whole dots (A). So the "2" has a classical ancestor: energy.

**Accounts.** (1) Wave function with the Schrödinger equation, Born rule as postulate; why 2 inside it: the equation keeps exactly one simple total unchanged in time, the total of squared sizes; the total of plain sizes grows as a cloud spreads (for a bell-shaped cloud, in proportion to the square root of its width), the total of cubes shrinks. Since the electron must be found somewhere with 100% chance at every moment, the chance must be the one amount the equation conserves. The stripe arithmetic says the same: amplitudes +1 and +1 at a bright spot, +1 and −1 at a dark spot; squares give 4 and 0, averaging exactly 2 = twice one slit (the average of a cos² stripe is exactly half its peak); plain sizes give 2 and 0 averaging about 1.3, so a third of the electrons would vanish; cubes give 8 and 0 averaging about 3.4, electrons from nowhere. Pros: the most physical reason; cons: it assumes the equation, and why the equation is of the square-preserving (unitary) kind is the deeper question. (2) Sum over paths: hands tip to tail, squared length; same arithmetic, same conservation (Feynman's rule inherits the square). (3) Born's own route (1926): by analogy with light, a bright beam's photon count rate is proportional to the classical wave's energy, quadratic in its height; the rule was a guess that fit, and the footnote is the historical record. Pros: continuity with classical physics; cons: no classical wave to lean on for electrons. (4) Interpretations, labelled contested, one or two sentences each: Copenhagen-style (postulate), many-worlds (derivations claimed and disputed), pilot-wave (quantum equilibrium: the square is the distribution that stays stable under the steering rule, like a gas in thermal equilibrium; non-equilibrium departures proposed and not seen), objective collapse (square built into the collapse law; Gisin's no-signalling argument).

**Relations.** Wave function and sum over paths: one theory, same square. The conservation account is established mathematics given the equation; the interpretations keep the square and differ only on whether it is a postulate, a derived fact, or an equilibrium.

**Figure requests** (two): `b-three-rules`: the far-screen stripe profile across the middle of the screen for the ideal two-slit set-up of answer A (slit separation four times the width), computed from the same amplitudes under three rules, chance = size^1, size^2, size^3, each with the one-slit curve under the same rule for reference, and the area ratio both-open/one-slit printed on each panel (verifier to compute: about 1.3, exactly 2, about 3.4). Notice: only the square doubles the count; size^1 has V-shaped cusps at the dark lines, the square is a rounded bowl. `b-spreading-cloud`: a free bell-shaped electron cloud at three times as it spreads (top) and, below, the totals of size^1, size^2, size^3 against time, each scaled to 1 at the start: only the square stays flat; size^1 rises, size^3 falls.

**Opening sentence.** "Nothing is created or lost between the slits and the screen: every electron that passes the slits lands somewhere (in the ideal set-up where the screen catches them all), and that bookkeeping, together with the shape of the stripes, is enough to pin the power down."

### Strategy 3: Three slits, add and subtract

**Experiments.** Sorkin's test, done by Sinha et al. (Science 2010) with single and attenuated photons, later with a five-path interferometer (Kauten et al. 2017) and with large molecules (Cotter et al. 2017): a wall with three slits A, B, C and blockers; seven runs: each slit alone, each pair, all three; the full pattern recorded each time. Observed: the three-slit pattern equals (the three pair patterns added) minus (the three single patterns added), spot by spot, to within about one percent of the stripe strength in 2010 and tighter since; the small leftover that remains is the size standard quantum mechanics itself predicts once the idealization "each slit's wave is the same whether or not the others are open" is dropped (looped paths, Magaña-Loaiza et al. 2016).

**Classical candidates.** Classical particles: no stripes in any run (ruled out by the pair stripes); classical waves: energy quadratic, so they also pass the add-and-subtract test (ruled out elsewhere by whole dots). Then the real alternatives physicists considered: Sorkin's hierarchy (1994) of theories with genuine three-way interference, of which the plain size and the cube are two examples; the experiment is built to catch them.

**Accounts.** (1) The amplitude rule with the square (state vectors / wave function): a square of a sum has only pairwise cross terms, so every interference effect is a conversation between *pairs* of alternatives and the three-slit pattern is fixed by singles and pairs; arithmetic: amplitudes 1, 1, −1 give square 1 = (4 + 0 + 0) − (1 + 1 + 1), leftover 0; plain sizes: 1 − 2 + 3, leftover 2; cubes: 1 − 8 + 3, leftover −4. Why this model: the measured leftover is zero; pros: the exponent becomes a measurable yes/no; cons: the test checks "pairwise only", which is the signature of 2, but a textbook still writes the square as a postulate. (2) Sum over paths: the same statement, hands tip to tail; grouping all routes through each slit is exact. (3) The structural reading (established mathematics): "pairwise only" is what mathematicians call a quadratic form; Gleason's theorem says that, with the non-contextuality assumption, a quadratic form in the state is the only consistent chance rule for three or more outcomes, so the three-slit null result and Gleason point at the same thing from the lab and from the blackboard. (4) Interpretations, labelled contested, briefly as in the other strategies, with the extra note that all four agree the Sorkin leftover is zero in the ideal set-up.

**Relations.** Experiment (zero leftover) and theorem (quadratic form) meet; what remains open is why the world's chances have this structure at all, and the interpretations answer that differently while agreeing on every pattern.

**Figure request** `c-three-slits`: computed ideal far-screen patterns for three equal, equally spaced slits: row 1 the three singles (identical envelopes), row 2 the three pairs (two spacings, so two stripe widths), row 3 the triple; row 4 the leftover = triple − pairs + singles under the square rule (flat zero), and the same leftover under the plain-size and cube rules (clearly non-zero, oscillating). Idealization printed in the caption: each slit's wave unchanged by the others being open.

**Opening sentence.** "There is an experiment built for exactly your question: cut three slits instead of two, open them one at a time, two at a time and all three, and the square rule makes a prediction that the plain size, the cube and every other power get wrong."
