<!-- strategy: A: Pythagoras at the polarizer -->

Send photons one at a time at a polarizing beam splitter and turn it: in an ideal splitter 100% pass at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, none at 90°; every photon leaves whole by one exit or the other; and the two exits together account for every photon at every angle. Your two-slit answer used electrons; the exponent is easiest to pin down with a photon's polarization, because there the model's amplitudes are plain shadows you can draw with a ruler. Two questions hide inside yours. How do we *know* the power is 2, rather than the plain size of the amplitude (its modulus, what you called the module), its cube, or anything else? That is what this experiment measures: once the models put the amplitude at 0.866 for a splitter at 30°, the measured 75% is its square, not the amplitude itself (87%) nor its cube (65%). Why could it not have been otherwise? That is what the models answer, and the short version is: once amplitudes are the shadows of an arrow that can be turned, the square is the only power, whole or fractional, under which the two exits always add up to all the photons; and with three or more exits, and chances that do not depend on how the outcomes are grouped, it is the only rule of any kind. The rest of this answer makes both halves precise and says which parts are measurement, which are mathematics, which are postulates and which are still argued over.

**The set-up.** A source of single photons: a crystal that emits photons in pairs, so that catching one of a pair announces (physicists say "heralds") that its partner is on its way; with rare exceptions (two pairs emitted at once, stray light) there is then exactly one photon in the apparatus. A polarizer fixed at 0° prepares the polarization of each photon that gets through it. Then a polarizing beam splitter: a cube that sends light polarized along its axis out of one face (call that exit "pass") and light polarized at right angles to its axis out of another face ("reflect"). The splitter can be turned about the beam by an angle θ; in practice one usually turns the photon's polarization instead, with a thin crystal slab (a "half-wave plate") that turns the direction of a linear polarization, in front of a fixed splitter, which gives the same counts. A detector at each exit. Idealizations: a splitter that absorbs nothing and sorts perfectly, detectors that miss nothing, a perfectly prepared polarization. Real splitters leak a little of each polarization into the wrong exit and real detectors miss some photons; the numbers below are the ideal ones, which real single-photon runs match to within a few per cent.

**What is seen.**

(1) Each photon is caught at one exit or the other. The two detectors essentially never click together: far less often than a wave sharing its energy between the two exits would require, and in the ideal case never. (Grangier, Roger and Aspect, 1986, with single photons at a half-silvered mirror, the non-polarizing cousin of our splitter; repeated in undergraduate laboratories since, e.g. Thorn et al., 2004.)

(2) The fraction of photons taking the "pass" exit depends on the angle: 100% at 0°, 75% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 25% at 60°, 0% at 90°, and in between it changes smoothly: turning the splitter a little changes the fraction a little. (The same curve, with the angle between two polarizers in place of the splitter angle, is seen in the joint counts of entangled photon pairs, pairs born together in a crystal with linked polarizations, sent through a polarizer each, e.g. Dehlinger and Mitchell, 2002.)

(3) Turn the splitter and the counts shift from one exit to the other, but their total does not change: 75 + 25, 50 + 50, 25 + 75. In the ideal splitter every photon is accounted for at every angle; a real one loses a small fraction, and the total stays constant to within that fraction.

(4) With a bright beam instead of single photons, the same fractions describe how the *energy* divides between the exits. Malus found this law in 1809, long before photons were known.

(5) The pieces can be put back together. Lead the two exits along paths of equal length (equal to within a fraction of a wavelength) into a second splitter facing the other way, and the photon comes out polarized at 0°, as it went in: a test polarizer at 0° then passes it every time (ideal set-up). With the splitter at 30°, lengthen one path by half a wavelength and the photon comes out polarized at 60° instead (in general, at twice the splitter angle), and the 0° test polarizer passes only a quarter of the photons.

Neutrons and atoms split two ways and recombined in interferometers of their own show the same thing, fractions that change smoothly while the total stays at 100% (in the ideal lossless case); the 2 is not a quirk of light.

**What this rules out.** *Light as a classical wave.* A wave carries energy in proportion to the square of its height, and a wave polarized at 0° meeting a splitter at 30° divides into a part of relative height 0.866 along the pass axis and 0.500 along the reflect axis, so its energy divides 75/25. The classical wave gets the fractions of (2) and (3) as energy fractions, gets (4) outright and (5) right, and already contains a square. It is killed by (1): it divides every pulse's energy between the two exits, however weak the pulse, so weak pulses would sometimes fire both detectors at once, and the detectors say each photon arrives whole at one exit. *A particle carrying a hidden pointer.* Suppose each photon carries a definite polarization angle of its own, the splitter sends it out of whichever exit's axis is nearer to that angle, and leaves the angle alone. The 0° polarizer then lets through photons whose pointers lie within 45° of 0°, spread evenly over that range if the source sends all angles equally; a splitter at θ passes those within 45° of θ; the overlap of the two ranges shrinks in a straight line: 67% at 30°, 50% at 45°, 33% at 60°. Killed by (2): the measured 75% and 25% are not on that line. (A pointer rule can be tuned to fit any single-photon fractions, but then it merely records them; for *pairs* of photons, Bell tests rule out every rule in which each photon's exit depends only on its own pointer and its own splitter, given that the splitter settings are chosen independently of the pointers. That is a separate story.) What survives is a model in which the thing the splitter handles is an arrow, the exits are two directions at right angles, and the two pieces are the arrow's shadows on them: in (5) the pieces re-add to the original arrow, and reversing the sign of one of them (the half-wavelength delay) re-adds to a different arrow, exactly as the two components of an arrow would.

**Model 1: states as arrows, chances as squared shadows.** This is standard quantum mechanics in its state-vector form, applied to a system with two outcomes. *Intuition.* The photon's polarization is an arrow of length 1 lying in the plane across the beam. A splitter at angle θ offers two directions at right angles, pass and reflect. The amplitude for each exit is the arrow's shadow on that direction: the length you get by dropping a perpendicular from the arrow's tip onto the line, counted as negative if it lands on the far side of the centre. For a 0° arrow and a splitter at 30° the shadows are 0.866 on pass and 0.500 on reflect; at 45°, 0.707 and 0.707; at 60°, 0.500 and 0.866. The chance of each exit is the square of its shadow: 0.75 and 0.25; 0.50 and 0.50; 0.25 and 0.75. After leaving an exit, the photon's arrow lies along that exit's direction, which is why three polarizers at 0°, 45° and 90° pass a quarter of the photons that got through the first one (0.5 × 0.5) while 0° and 90° alone pass none. Observation (5) is the arrow picture run backwards: re-adding the two shadows rebuilds the arrow, and re-adding them with the reflect shadow's sign reversed builds the arrow's mirror image across the pass direction. The mirror image of a 0° arrow across a 30° line lies at 60°, and the 0° test then passes its squared shadow, 0.5² = 0.25. For light polarized in a circle or an ellipse, and for the electron amplitudes of the two-slit answer, the shadows are clock hands rather than plain positive-or-negative numbers; "squared size" then means the squared length of the hand, and everything below goes through unchanged.

*Why this model.* One picture gives every number in (2) to (5): the fractions, the constant total, the re-adding, the 60° after a sign reversal. It is the two-slit answer's "combine first, square after" with the two slits' amplitudes replaced by two shadows. *Pros.* The arrow idea, with more directions, is the state-vector form of the whole theory: spin, energy levels, qubits. *Cons.* As a textbook presents it, the square is a postulate: Born's rule. In Born's first 1926 paper on collisions the text has the probability given by the amplitude itself, and a footnote added in proof says that more careful consideration shows it is proportional to the square; his longer paper later that year backed the square by noting that squared amplitudes are what add up to 1, the argument of account 1 below. The rule was adopted because it fits and adds up, which is exactly the gap you noticed. The next three accounts fill it, each answering a different sceptic: one who tries other powers, one who doubts that the pieces are ordinary shadows at all, and one who tries rules that are not powers.

**Why 2, account 1: Pythagoras.** *(Status: established mathematics, given that the pieces are the shadows of an arrow of length 1.)* Draw the arrow and its two shadows: they form a right triangle whose longest side is the arrow. Pythagoras says the squares of the two short sides add to the square of the long side, which is 1, at every angle: 0.750 + 0.250 = 1 at 30°, 0.500 + 0.500 = 1 at 45°. That is observation (3): all the photons, at every angle. Now try the plain size instead. At 30°, 0.866 + 0.500 = 1.37; at 45°, 0.707 + 0.707 = 1.41: 141% of the photons, when the two exits are the only places a photon can go. Cubes: at 30°, 0.650 + 0.125 = 0.775; at 45°, 0.354 + 0.354 = 0.71: 29% of the photons unaccounted for, though (3) says they are all there. Fourth power: 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.5 at 45°. Of all the powers, fractional ones included, only the second keeps the total at exactly 1 for every angle; any smaller positive power overshoots at every angle between 0° and 90°, any larger one undershoots, worst at 45°.

*Why this account.* It turns "why 2" into geometry: two outcomes that exclude each other and exhaust the possibilities must have chances adding to 1, and squared shadows are what add to 1. *Pros.* Nothing but a right triangle. *Cons.* "Only" here means only among powers. With just two exits, a determined sceptic can write down a rule that is not a power and still totals 1: take the squared shadow and add a small wiggle to each exit's chance, with the wiggle chosen so that the one for the pass exit always cancels the one for the reflect exit. Measurements like (2) push any such wiggle below their error bars but cannot make it exactly zero. Account 2 says why the pieces must be ordinary shadows, which this account assumed; closing the gap for rules that are not powers takes account 3.

**Why 2, account 2: the arrow can be turned.** *(Status: established mathematics, given two things: that amplitudes add, carried over from the two-slit answer, and that the chance is some power of the amplitude's size.)* Account 1 took for granted that the two pieces are the ordinary shadows of an arrow, 0.866 and 0.500 at 30°. A sceptic could say: let the pieces at 30° be 0.909 and 0.630 instead, whose cubes come to 0.75 and 0.25, and the cube rule fits the counts just as well. Two facts shut this door. First, amplitudes add. In the two-slit answer the two slits' amplitudes were combined before the chance was taken; here the two pieces re-add to the arrow in (5). The precise form of the rule: whatever a splitter, a wave plate or a slit does to an arrow that is the sum of two others, it does to each part and then adds the results. Second, the arrow can be turned smoothly, by as small an angle as you like, with the two exits still totalling 100% afterwards. Keep the splitter fixed and put a tube of sugar water in the beam: it turns the polarization by an angle that grows with the length of the tube, and the counts at the two exits change smoothly as the tube is lengthened while, in the ideal lossless case, their total stays put (observation (3), with the photon turned instead of the splitter). Now try to build such a turn in a plain-size world. Start from the 0° arrow, pieces 1 and 0, total 1. An ordinary turn by 45° gives pieces 0.707 and 0.707, total 1.41; a turn by just 1° gives 0.9998 and 0.0175, total 1.017. Every ordinary turn, however small, breaks the total. In the square world the same turns give 0.500 + 0.500 = 1.000 and 0.9997 + 0.0003 = 1.0000: the total is kept at every angle, which is Pythagoras again.

Could some cleverer change than an ordinary turn rescue the plain size, or the cube? A theorem says no. Suppose the chance of an exit is the p-th power of the size of its piece, for some power p, and changes act on sums part by part. Then for every p other than 2, the only changes that keep the chances totalling 100% for every possible arrow are swapping the labels of the directions and reversing signs (for clock-hand pieces, turning each hand on the spot): jumps, not turns. For p = 2 the chance-keeping changes are exactly the turns and mirror flips (for clock-hand pieces, their generalization: the changes that keep every arrow's length and every angle between arrows), and you can turn by as little as you like. One exception: p = 1 with pieces that are never negative. Then the pieces are the chances themselves, nothing ever cancels, and the dark stripes of the two-slit answer, where opening a slit removed hits, rule that world out. *Why this account.* It says why the pieces must be ordinary shadows, and so why, among power rules, whole or fractional, a world whose amplitudes add, cancel and can be turned a little at a time has to use the square. *Pros.* It ties the power to the two features that make quantum mechanics quantum, cancelling and continuous change. *Cons.* It compares powers only: the wiggle rule of account 1 is not a power, depends on nothing but the squared shadow, and passes every turn untouched. And it rests on "changes act on sums part by part". Drop that and alternatives can be cooked up by renaming: the sceptic's 0.909 and 0.630 are the ordinary shadows raised to the power two-thirds, and "cube of the renamed piece" is just "square of the shadow" in disguise; but renamed amplitudes no longer add, and the two-slit stripes and the re-adding in (5) need amplitudes that do.

*Sources:* Aaronson, *Quantum Computing since Democritus* (2013), ch. 9 "Quantum" (why the 2-norm: only p = 1 and p = 2 allow non-trivial continuous changes).

**Why 2, account 3: Gleason's theorem.** *(Status: established mathematics; what it assumes, the arrow-and-directions structure plus one consistency requirement, is weaker than the square rule itself.)* Go to three or more exits. A photon's polarization alone offers only two, but a photon that could be on either of two paths, each with two polarizations, offers four, and a magnet that sorts a beam of certain atoms into three beams offers three. The model: outcomes are directions in the arrow's space; outcomes that exclude each other are directions at right angles; a measurement is a complete set of such directions; one of them must happen, so their chances total 100%. The assumption: the chance of a given direction depends only on that direction and on the arrow, not on which other directions you have grouped it with, so two measurements that share a direction give that direction the same chance. Gleason proved in 1957 that, for arrow spaces with three or more mutually perpendicular directions, the only chance rules satisfying this are the squared shadow of the arrow and, for an arrow you know only partly, averages of squared shadows; adding the observed fact that a photon prepared along a direction passes that direction every time picks out the squared shadow itself. This is not "only among powers": any rule at all, however contrived, is excluded. The wiggle of account 1 dies as soon as a third direction can be swapped in and the chances have to stay consistent across every regrouping. For a system with only two exits Gleason's theorem does not apply; Busch showed in 2003 that the conclusion returns if the assumption is extended to measurements that do not sort perfectly, like a real leaky splitter.

*Why this account.* It is the most general: once outcomes are directions and chances are consistent across all the ways of measuring, the square is not a free choice. *Pros.* Forces the power from consistency alone. *Cons.* Critics note that the assumption does most of the work: it has been checked for the groupings tested, but is assumed for all of them. (Bell made the related point in 1966 that, for hidden variables, an individual outcome could depend on the whole arrangement of the apparatus.) The theorem also takes the arrow-and-directions structure as given rather than explaining it, and says nothing about why there are chances at all or what counts as a measurement.

*Sources:* Gleason, *J. Math. Mech.* 6, 885 (1957); Busch, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 91, 120403 (2003); Bell, *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 38, 447 (1966), on the assumption behind Gleason's theorem.

**Where the interpretations stand.** All of them keep the square and agree on every number above; they differ on what the chance is a chance *of*, and on whether the square is a postulate, a derived fact or a stable condition. Everything in this section is contested.

*Copenhagen-style.* The square is a postulate; Gleason's theorem shows it is the only consistent one given the structure and the grouping assumption of account 3, and there the explanation stops. Pro: minimal. Con: critics object that a postulate about measurement outcomes leaves "measurement" undefined.

*Many-worlds.* The wave never collapses: both exits happen, in different branches, and "chance" has to be reconstructed. Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) argue that an agent who knows the branches and bets consistently must bet on them by their squared sizes; Zurek (2003) derives the square from a symmetry of entangled states (if swapping the two branches on the photon's side can be undone by a swap on its partner's side, leaving the joint state unchanged, the two branches must be equally likely, and unequal cases are built from equal pieces). Critics object that these derivations assume what they need, such as that branches should be weighed at all or weighed by size, and that in a theory where every outcome happens it is unclear what a chance is; supporters reply that the assumptions are requirements of rationality or symmetry, not the rule itself.

*Pilot-wave.* Particles always have positions and are steered by the wave (worked out for electrons and atoms; the version for light is less settled). A mathematical fact: if positions are spread according to the squared size of the wave at one moment, the steering keeps them spread that way for ever, while a spread according to the plain size or the cube would not in general stay that way. Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì (1992) argue that for a typical starting condition of the universe the square holds anyway, and in computer runs (Valentini and Westman, 2005) other spreads usually relax towards it, as a gas relaxes to a uniform temperature, though not for every wave. So here the square is an equilibrium rather than a postulate. Other spreads are possible in principle as "non-equilibrium" starting conditions; Valentini has proposed looking for relics of them from the early universe, and none have been found so far. Critics debate whether "typical" explains or merely restates.

*Objective collapse.* The wave is real and localizes itself at random, and in the collapse law the chance that the wave settles at a place is its squared size there (averaged over a tiny region), so the square is built in. There is a reason it has to be: Gisin showed in 1989 that, given the standard description of entangled pairs, a random collapse weighted by anything other than the squared size would let two people sharing entangled particles signal to each other faster than light. So in these theories the square is fixed by the ban on such signalling. Pro: a reason that comes from relativity rather than from a postulate about measurement. Con: new constants of nature, and the small departures from standard quantum mechanics that these theories predict have been searched for and not found so far, which has ruled out some versions and narrowed the room for the rest.

**How the accounts relate.** The three "why 2" accounts are one geometric story told with rising rigour. Pythagoras shows that, among powers, fractional ones included, only the square keeps two exits adding up to all the photons; the turning argument shows why the pieces must be ordinary shadows in the first place, so that a world where amplitudes add, cancel and change smoothly has no other power available; Gleason's theorem shows that, with three or more directions and chances that stay consistent across every way of grouping them, no rule other than the squared shadow survives at all. Each is established mathematics; what each assumes is the arrow-and-directions structure itself, which is where the postulates sit. The classical wave carries the same square as energy, and by Born's own later account he was guided by that analogy; the wave function and the sum over paths of the two-slit answer are the arrow idea with many directions instead of two, and inherit the square unchanged. The interpretations agree on every count in (1) to (5); they differ on why there are arrows at all and what the chances are chances of. No experiment has told the first three apart, while objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose small departures are still being searched for. So the honest status of your "why exactly 2" is this: the runs described here agree with it to a few per cent, and dedicated tests of its consequences, such as three-slit experiments that look for a three-way interference which the square rule forbids when the slits' amplitudes simply add (Sinha et al., 2010, and tighter work since), find no departure at the per-cent level or below; it is forced by consistency once states are turnable arrows; and it is still argued over at the level of what the arrows are. (Works are cited by chapter and section; page numbers are given only for journal articles.)
