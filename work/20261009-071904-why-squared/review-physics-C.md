## Issues
- [major] "then the chance must be the squared shadow" — This misstates Gleason's theorem. The listed assumptions are frame-additivity, non-contextuality and dimension ≥ 3. They are also satisfied by "every outcome equally likely, whatever the arrow" (1/N in N dimensions), which is not the squared shadow. What Gleason actually proves is that any such assignment of chances is a quadratic formula (a density operator). Getting |shadow|² for a given arrow needs one more assumption that ties the chances to the state, for example "an outcome along the arrow's own direction is certain". Also, Busch (2003) covers two-dimensional systems (a single qubit) when generalized measurements are allowed. These measurements can have more than two outcomes, so "two-outcome systems" is wrong. Fix: "...and if the outcome pointing along the arrow itself is certain, then the chance must be the squared shadow..."; add this link to the Cons as another assumption; write "two-dimensional systems (a single qubit), when generalized measurements are allowed".
- [major] "shows that 'pairwise only' and 'squared shadow' are the same statement" — The equivalence is overstated; the scope stated in Model 3 has been dropped. The equivalence holds only for a chance rule that is applied to the total amplitude at a spot, is continuous, gives 0 for a zero hand, is never negative and does not depend on phase. Sorkin's general framework contains "pairwise only" (quadratic) measures that do not come from amplitudes at all. In general probabilistic theories, "no three-way interference" gives quantum theory only together with further postulates (Barnum, Müller & Ududec 2014). Gleason's theorem does not use interference at all. Fix: "Model 3 shows that, for a chance computed from the total hand by one smooth, never-negative, direction-blind rule, 'pairwise only' forces the square; separately, Gleason shows that within the arrow picture (plus its assumptions) no other rule is consistent." In Model 3 itself, also say "a rule that computes the chance from the total hand alone".
- [major] "At every spot tested the leftover was zero within the measurement error" — This overstates the 2010 result. Its bound (κ below about 10⁻²) is set mainly by systematic errors such as detector nonlinearity and drifts, and not every κ value was statistically consistent with zero on its own. Two later phrases make the same overstatement: "the measurement shows exactly that signature" and "a yes-or-no answer". Experiments give bounds. They cannot give a yes. Fix: "the leftover was consistent with zero within the total (statistical plus systematic) uncertainty, which bounds it below about one hundredth of the two-way interference". Change "exactly" to "to within that precision", and say the test gives "a measurable number that the square predicts to be zero" rather than "yes-or-no".
- [major] "both halves have been checked" — The idealized "exactly zero" can never be confirmed exactly, and the slit waves of a real setup are never independent. What has been checked: ordinary slits give a leftover bounded at roughly 10⁻² to 10⁻³, consistent with the tiny predicted correction, which has not itself been resolved there. Engineered setups give a measured nonzero leftover that agrees with the calculation (plasmonic slits, 2016; see also the analysis of Sinha, Vijay & Sinha, *Sci. Rep.* 2015). The same paragraph also says real slits give a "tiny" leftover in general, but the 2016 setup made it large on purpose. Fix: "for ordinary slits the square rule predicts a leftover far below current precision, and experiments find none above about 1%; in set-ups built to strengthen looped routes, it predicts a sizeable leftover, and that was measured as predicted."
- [major] "Grouping every route through A into one hand ... is exact" — This contradicts the fine print. A looped route (in through A, back through B, out through C) belongs to no single group. Grouping by slit is exact only when each route crosses the plate once, which is the independent-slit-waves idealization. Fix: "In the idealization where every route crosses the plate once, grouping ... is exact, and the grid picture applies to the three group hands; the looped routes are what break this grouping in real slits."
- [major] "All four below predict the same patterns and the same zero leftover" — As an absolute this is false for objective collapse, and the text's own Con says those theories predict departures. The zero leftover does survive, because GRW-type theories stay quadratic. Fix: "All four predict the same patterns (objective-collapse theories only up to departures far too small to see in these experiments) and the same zero leftover for independent slit waves..."
- [minor] "for electrons, the single whole dots of answer A say the same" — Dots alone do not rule out a classical wave, because grainy detectors can produce dots from a smooth wave. That is why Grangier–Roger–Aspect needed the anticorrelation test. The evidence is one dot per electron, never two and never a fraction. Fix: "for electrons sent one at a time, each makes exactly one whole dot, never two at once, which says the same."
- [minor] "Zurek (2003–2005) derives it from a symmetry of entangled states" — Zurek presents his envariance argument as separate from many-worlds, though it fits with it. Separately, in Born 1926 the main text says the amplitude "determines" the probability, not that the chance is "proportional to" it. Fix: "Zurek's related argument from a symmetry of entangled states (which he places outside many-worlds proper)"; and "the main text tied the chance to the amplitude itself; a footnote added in proof corrected it to the square."

The treatment of the looped-path caveat as "the square rule applied more carefully" and the even-handed interpretation section are good.
