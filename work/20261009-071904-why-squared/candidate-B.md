Nothing is created or lost between the slits and the screen: every electron that passes the slits lands somewhere (in the ideal set-up, where the screen catches them all), and that bookkeeping, together with the shape of the stripes, is enough to pin the power down. The short version: of the plain powers (size, square, cube, and so on), the square is the one that moves electrons around the screen without inventing or losing any, and the one whose total a spreading electron cloud keeps fixed. In the textbooks the rule is still written down as a postulate (Born, 1926), and the interpretations differ on whether it is a postulate, a derived fact or an equilibrium. The rest of this answer unpacks that. One note on words: in answer A, "squared size" meant the square of the length of the clock hand, and that length is what mathematicians call the modulus; so your "modulus" alternative is that length with no square, which I call the plain size below.

**The experiment, read with numbers.** The same set-up as answer A: electrons one at a time, a wall with two equal narrow slits, a far screen. The idealizations are as before (one electron speed; a far screen; slits narrower than their spacing; a screen that catches every electron that passes the slits), plus one more that answer A used silently: each slit's wave is taken to be the same whether or not the other slit is open. That is true to high accuracy for slits many wavelengths apart, and the tiny departures from it have been measured with light. Four things are observed or guaranteed.

(1) *Shape.* The stripes ride on a slow fading towards the edges, and on that envelope they have a pure wave shape: every bright stripe and every dark stripe has the same rounded form, one the other upside down, with no sharp kink at the dark lines. Measured patterns, with light since Young and with electrons in the 2013 experiment, match this shape, allowing for the detector's blur.

(2) *Peak.* At the centre of the screen, the arrival rate with both slits open is four times the rate with one slit open, in the ideal set-up with equal slits. With light, where rates are easy to measure, this is standard wave optics and is checked in teaching labs; the electron patterns have the same shape.

(3) *Totals.* Count every electron on the whole screen. In the ideal set-up, both slits open catch exactly twice as many as one slit: each slit passes its own share, and nothing is created or destroyed on the way, because electric charge is conserved. An electron that passed the slits cannot simply fail to arrive. So the stripes rearrange where electrons land; they do not change how many land. (This one is guaranteed by conservation plus the idealizations rather than read off a counter.)

(4) *Spreading.* Send electrons (or neutrons) through one narrow slit and move a wide detector further and further away: the spot on it grows wider and fainter with distance, but the total count per second does not change (ideal: no losses, and a detector wide enough to catch the whole spread). A spreading cloud keeps its number.

Keep these four apart from any account of them. They are counts.

*Sources:* Hecht, *Optics*, 5th ed. (2017), ch. 10, §10.2.2 "The Double Slit"; Bach et al., *New J. Phys.* 15, 033018 (2013); Magaña-Loaiza et al., *Nat. Commun.* 7, 13987 (2016) (the tiny departures from "each slit's wave is unchanged"); Feynman, Leighton & Sands, *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, Vol. III (2010), ch. 1, §1-4 and §1-5.

**The classical candidates.** *Tiny balls:* each uses one slit, so the chance at a spot is one slit's chance plus the other's. There is no amplitude and no power to choose, and the picture is killed, as in answer A, by spots that go dark when a slit is opened. *A classical wave* (water, a string, sound, light): here a power already appears. The energy a gentle wave carries is proportional to the square of its height, because the restoring force grows in proportion to the displacement, as in a spring, whose stored energy grows with the square of its stretch; for light, the energy is likewise proportional to the square of the field strength. So a classical wave through two slits gives exactly (1), (2) and (3): wave-shaped stripes, a central peak four times one slit's, and a total twice one slit's, which for a wave is just conservation of energy. Young saw these stripes with light in 1801–03, and it is textbook wave optics. The picture fails for electrons because a wave's energy arrives everywhere, continuously, while electrons arrive as whole dots (answer A). But it shows that the 2 has a classical ancestor: for a wave, "how much" is the square of "how high".

**Model 1: the wave function, with the Schrödinger equation.** The picture from answer A: an amplitude at every point, a clock hand with a length and a direction, spreading as a ripple under the Schrödinger equation; the parts arriving from the two slits are added hand to hand; the chance of a dot is proportional to the square of the total hand's length. Two things in the experiment pin the exponent, once you accept that the hands add (the spots that go dark force that) and that the ripple has a wavelength (the stripe spacing fixes it).

*Counting across the screen.* Take the centre of the screen, where the two hands point the same way, each of length 1 in units where the one-slit chance there is 1: the total has length 2. Half a stripe away they point opposite ways: the total has length 0. As you move across the screen the angle between the hands turns steadily, so the total's length swings between 2 and 0 and back. Chance = square: 4 at the bright spot, 0 at the dark spot, and the square of a steadily turning total is a pure wave shape, observation (1), with the central peak four times one slit's, observation (2). Over one full stripe the square of that swinging length averages to exactly half its peak, 2 (ignoring the slow fading of the envelope across the stripe), which is twice the one-slit value, so both slits open catch twice the electrons, observation (3). Over the whole ideal screen the doubling is exact, envelope included: the extra "cross" part of the square, added up over the screen, comes to exactly zero whenever the two slit openings do not overlap (a fact of the mathematics of the model). Now try the plain size: 2 at the bright spot (against the 4 in observation 2), V-shaped valleys with a kink at every dark line (against observation 1), and over a stripe it averages to about 1.27, not 2: about a third of the electrons that passed the slits would have to vanish. Try the cube: 8 at the bright spot, and an average over a stripe of about 3.4: electrons would have to appear from nowhere. You could try to rescue the plain size by rescaling it at every moment so that it totals 100%, but that saves only the totals, not the shape or the 4-to-1 peak, and the chance at a spot would then no longer come from the hand at that spot alone. Among plain powers, only the square keeps the books.

*Counting over time.* Now the spreading cloud of observation (4). The equation makes the ripple spread, so the hand at the centre of the cloud shrinks as the cloud widens. The equation has one exact property that matters here: of all the totals of the kind "add up some power of the hand's length over all of space", only the total of squares stays fixed for every possible ripple; the other powers change as soon as the cloud spreads. The total of plain lengths grows as the cloud spreads (for a bell-shaped cloud spreading along one direction, in proportion to the square root of its width; faster if it spreads in all three directions); the total of cubes shrinks (as one over that square root). The electron is found somewhere with certainty at every moment, so the chance must be the one amount the equation holds fixed.

*Status, pros and cons.* In the textbooks "chance = squared size" is a postulate, Born's rule, as answer A said. What the two counts above show is that the exponent is not a free choice once you have hands that add and electrons that are conserved: any other plain power contradicts the counts. There is also a theorem behind it: for a system with three or more distinguishable outcomes, if each outcome's chance depends only on that outcome's own part of the state, the chance rule has to be the square (Gleason, 1957; the two-outcome case needs an extra ingredient, Busch, 2003). That is established mathematics with one stated assumption. Why this model rather than another: for this question there is no rival inside quantum mechanics; models 2 and 3 below are the same theory in other clothes and the origin story. Pros: this is the most physical reason on offer, conservation, and the same conservation runs through atoms, where the squared amplitude is the electron cloud that chemists draw. Cons: it assumes the equation. Why the ripple equation is of the square-preserving kind (physicists say "unitary") is the deeper question, and this account does not answer it; nor does it say why there are chances at all.

*Sources:* Griffiths & Schroeter, *Introduction to Quantum Mechanics*, 3rd ed. (2018), ch. 1, §1.4 "Normalization" (the equation preserves the total of squares); Feynman, Leighton & Sands, Vol. III, ch. 3, §3-1; Gleason, *J. Math. Mech.* 6, 885 (1957); Busch, *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 91, 120403 (2003).

**Model 2: a sum over paths.** One hand per route from source to spot, added tip to tail, with the chance the squared length of the total (answer A). The square is not chosen afresh here; it is inherited, because this is the same theory as model 1 in different mathematics (Feynman, 1948), with the same arithmetic (4 and 0 where adding chances would give 2 and 2) and the same conservation. Why this model: you use it when it is easier to think with, and the question "why 2" gets the same answer. Pros: it makes one thing visible that the square implies and that has been tested directly: a square of a sum is a sum over pairs, so interference is always a meeting of two routes at a time, never a genuine three-way effect; with three slits, the pattern is fully predicted from the one-slit and two-slit patterns, and that prediction was checked with photons in 2010 and holds to within about one per cent of the stripe strength (the plain size and the cube both predict a three-way leftover). Cons: as in answer A, and it still writes the square by hand.

*Sources:* Feynman, *QED* (1985), ch. 2; Sorkin, *Mod. Phys. Lett. A* 9, 3119 (1994); Sinha et al., *Science* 329, 418 (2010).

**Model 3: Born's route, by analogy with light (1926).** This is where the 2 came from historically. Born was calculating electrons scattering off atoms and had an amplitude for each outgoing direction. For light, the number of photons per second arriving at a spot is proportional to the classical wave's energy there, the square of the field, and diffraction patterns come out unchanged with light so feeble that, on average, far less than one photon's worth of energy is in the apparatus at a time (Taylor, 1909). Born guessed the same for the electron's ripple. In the paper he first wrote that the chance is proportional to the amplitude; in a footnote added in proof he corrected it: "More careful consideration shows that the probability is proportional to the square." Intuition: the electron's ripple is like the light wave, and what you count is like the energy. Why this model: it was the first, it reproduced the scattering, and it keeps continuity with the classical square of the previous section. Pros: for light the step is not a guess, since photon counts really are proportional to energy; it ties the quantum rule to two centuries of wave optics. Cons: there is no classical electron wave with an energy to lean on, so for electrons the analogy is a hint, not a reason. It is the conservation argument of model 1 that makes the guess stick.

*Sources:* Born, *Z. Phys.* 37, 863 (1926), the footnote; Born, Nobel lecture (1954), "The statistical interpretation of quantum mechanics"; Taylor, *Proc. Camb. Phil. Soc.* 15, 114 (1909).

**Where the interpretations stand.** All four below agree on every count above and on every number the models give; they differ on what the square *is*. Each of these positions is contested.

*Copenhagen-style.* The square is a postulate and the ripple a tool for predicting; the only thing to ask is whether the rule is consistent, and Gleason's theorem says the square is the consistent choice. Critics object that a postulate about "measurement" leaves the theory's own basic words undefined.

*Many-worlds.* The wave is everything and never collapses, so no postulate about chances is allowed: the square must come out of the wave itself. Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) argue that a rational agent inside the branching wave, betting on outcomes, must weight the branches by the squared size, the only weighting that respects the symmetries of the wave; Zurek (2003–05) argues the same from symmetries of entangled states. Critics object that these derivations assume what they set out to show, that branch weights are chances at all, and that "how many worlds" has no clean answer; supporters reply that the square is the only weighting left once the wave is taken seriously.

*Pilot-wave.* Electrons always have positions and are steered by the wave. The square is an equilibrium: if the starting positions in a large batch of runs are spread as the squared size, the steering keeps them spread as the squared size for ever (an exact property of the steering law, established mathematics), much as a gas stays at a uniform temperature once it has one. Why the positions are so spread to begin with is the open part: Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì (1992) argue it is overwhelmingly typical, while Valentini argues the universe may have started out of equilibrium and relaxed, with traces possibly visible in the relic radiation from the early universe; none have been seen so far.

*Objective collapse.* The square is built into the collapse law: the ripple localizes itself at random, with the chance of landing in a region set by the squared size there. Gisin (1989–90) argued that, with the standard wave equation, any other chance rule would let the partners of entangled pairs send signals faster than light, so within these models the square is also the no-signalling choice. Their small predicted departures from standard quantum mechanics have been searched for and not found so far.

Phys.* 67, 843 (1992); Valentini & Westman, *Proc. R. Soc. A* 461, 253 (2005); Gisin, *Helv. Phys. Acta* 62, 363 (1989) and *Phys. Lett. A* 143, 1 (1990); Bassi et al., *Rev. Mod. Phys.* 85, 471 (2013); Maudlin, *Philosophy of Physics: Quantum Theory* (2019), chs. 4–6.

**How they relate.** Sort the answer into three layers. Established: the four counts; the classical wave's square, which is energy; and the mathematics that, given hands that add and spread under the Schrödinger equation, only the total of squares is conserved, together with Gleason's theorem that the square is the only consistent rule for three or more outcomes once you assume each outcome's chance depends on its own part of the state alone. Postulate: the textbook Born rule, which simply states the square and lets the above explain why no other power could have worked. Contested: the interpretations' attempts to derive the square or to explain its status, which agree on every number and differ on whether it is a postulate, a theorem about rational betting, an equilibrium, or a law of collapse. Models 1 and 2 are one theory and give one answer; model 3 is the origin story, right for light and a lucky guess for electrons, made good by conservation. So "exactly 2" is not an arbitrary choice someone made: with amplitudes that add and particles that are neither created nor lost, the plain size would lose electrons and the cube would invent them.
