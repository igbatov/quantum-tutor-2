The record that kills the stripes does not have to push the particle off course, and it does not have to be permanent: both have been tested directly, with atoms tagged by a microwave flip whose push is roughly a hundred thousand times too weak to blur anything, and with photon pairs in which the tag was "erased", in the sense explained below, only after the tagged photon had already landed. Last time the rule was: a which-slit record fades the stripes more, the better its two end states can be told apart, because two parts of the amplitude can only cancel where they end in the same final situation. This answer takes up the two questions that rule leaves open, whether the record works by jostling the particle and whether anyone has to read it, and then shows what "decoherence" adds: tags written by the surroundings themselves, one collision at a time, and spread so widely that nothing can sort them out again. Throughout, "stripe strength" means the contrast of the stripes, from 1 (dark stripes that reach zero) to 0 (no stripes), and positions on the screen are in units of the stripe spacing, as before.

Experiment 1: a tag too weak to push (atoms, 1998). Set-up (Dürr, Nonn and Rempe, Konstanz). Rubidium atoms, laser-cooled to well under a thousandth of a degree above absolute zero, are released and fall through two short pulses of a standing light wave. The first pulse is the beam splitter: it sends part of each atom's wave straight on and deflects the other part sideways, so there are two paths instead of two slits. The second pulse splits each part again, so that a piece of the straight part and a piece of the deflected part travel on side by side; those pieces spread, overlap, and far below the atoms' positions are recorded, in the far-screen regime of last time (the other two pieces make a second pattern of the same kind). Where the pieces overlap, the atom density shows stripes. The tag: a rubidium atom's lowest energy state comes in two close versions, which differ in how the magnetism of the nucleus and of the outer electron are lined up (physicists call them hyperfine states; each is really a small family of states, and the experiment used one member of each). Call them A and B; they are separated in energy by a microwave frequency, about 3 GHz. With a pair of microwave pulses, one just before the splitting light pulse and one just after, each atom is left in state A if it went straight and in state B if it was deflected. (The trick: the first pulse mixes A and B half-and-half; the splitting light is tuned so that the deflected part's mix comes out with one half flipped in sign relative to the straight part's; the second pulse un-mixes, which sends the unflipped mix back to A and the flipped mix to B.) No part of the apparatus has to read the internal state for what follows; the tag simply sits in the atom until it lands. The only difference between a run with the tag and a run without is the two microwave pulses, and a microwave photon carries about a hundred-thousandth of the momentum of one of the optical photons that do the splitting (its wavelength is about ten centimetres against less than a thousandth of a millimetre), far too little to shift an atom by any noticeable fraction of a stripe. What is seen. With the microwave pulses off: stripes (not perfectly black and white; the real contrast was limited by the apparatus, and the figure idealizes it to full contrast). With the pulses on: no stripes could be seen, just the broad band of the two one-path chances added (exactly that, in the ideal case). In a companion experiment the tag was written only partly, so that its two end states overlapped instead of being fully distinct, and the measured stripe strength and telling-apart power obeyed the budget (stripe strength)² + (telling-apart power)² ≤ 1, which Model 1 below explains.

Experiment 2: a tag that can be sorted out, even late (photon pairs, 2002). Set-up (Walborn, Terra Cunha, Pádua and Monken, Belo Horizonte). A laser shone into a crystal now and then produces a pair of photons whose polarizations are entangled. For the pair state this source is built to make (ideally): measured along horizontal and vertical, the two photons always give opposite results; measured along the two diagonals, +45° and −45°, always the same result; and each photon on its own has no definite polarization at all, giving a 50/50 result along any direction you choose. One photon of each pair, the slit photon, goes to a double slit; the other, the partner, goes straight to its own detector. In front of each slit sits a quarter-wave plate: a thin crystal slab that delays light polarized along one of its two axes by a quarter of a wavelength relative to light polarized along the other. The two plates are set with their axes at +45° and −45° to the horizontal, and what a plate does depends on the polarization that arrives. Horizontally polarized light leaves circularly polarized, its field turning one way behind the left slit and the other way behind the right slit; vertically polarized light also leaves circular, with the two handednesses swapped. Diagonally polarized light lies along a plate axis, so it passes with its polarization unchanged, only delayed by a quarter wave behind one slit and not the other (and the other way round for the other diagonal). Since the slit photon has no polarization of its own, the tag is written into the pair: "through the left slit" ends as "left-turning with the partner vertical, or right-turning with the partner horizontal", and "through the right slit" as the same with the handednesses swapped. Those two end states can, ideally, be told apart with certainty, but only by looking at both photons; the slit photon's handedness alone, or the partner alone, says nothing about the slit. And a tag written in polarization gives the photon no sideways push. Everything is counted in pairs (coincidence counting): a dot of the slit photon is kept only if its partner was detected too, which is how the two photons of one pair are picked out from stray light. What is seen, scanning the slit photon's detector across the pattern far behind the slits (the figure below uses the ideal far screen with the slits four times further apart than they are wide, d = 4a, as last time, not the experiment's exact sizes): (a) plates removed: stripes. (b) plates in: no stripes; what is left is the two one-slit patterns added, a broad central band with faint one-slit side bands further out. (c) plates in, and a polarizer at +45° in the partner's path, counting only the pairs whose partner got through: stripes again; with the partner's polarizer at −45° instead, stripes again, shifted by half a stripe spacing, bright where the first group was dark; the two groups together make up the pattern of (b), exactly so in the ideal case. (d) Lengthen the partner's path so that it reaches its polarizer and detector after the slit photon has landed: (b) and (c) are unchanged. What is never seen: stripes in the slit photon's dots when nothing sorts them, whatever is done to the partner, because the two groups of (c) always add up to (b); if it were otherwise, the setting of the partner's polarizer could be read off at the double slit and used to send a signal. (The same tag-and-sort physics has been done with one photon in a two-arm interferometer, and in the "delayed-choice quantum eraser" of Kim et al., 2000, whose five-detector layout is harder to check; the pairs version carries all of it.)

What these two experiments rule out. Four pictures that each cope with last time's plain which-slit result fail here. (1) The detector jostles the particle, and the random kicks blur the stripes. This is how Feynman's lecture explains the light-scattering version, and for records made by bouncing something off the particle it is not wrong: there the kick picture and the record picture give the same numbers, which is why a real argument ran in 1994–95 (Storey, Tan, Collett and Walls for kicks; Englert, Scully and Walther against) over whether a kick could ever be dispensed with. Wiseman and Harrison pointed out in 1995 that the two sides meant different things by "kick": the spread of the particle's momenta does change whenever a record is made (on a far screen the pattern is that spread, and it loses its stripes), but not necessarily because the particle was given a random push; the argument over words went on for years. The 1998 experiment settled what matters here: the only change between stripes and no stripes was two microwave pulses whose photons push a hundred thousand times too weakly to blur anything, and the stripes went anyway. A tag in polarization gives no sideways push (for an ideal plate, none). (2) Someone has to read the record. Nothing has to read the atoms' internal states, and in (b) nobody does anything with the partner; the stripes are gone regardless. (3) The tag damages the wave, as friction smudges a ripple. A smudged ripple cannot be unsmudged by sorting dots afterwards; in (c) stripes sit inside each sorted group, made of dots that were already on the screen. (4) The photon used one slit, landed by that slit's own one-slit chances whatever its partner later does, and the tag merely reports the slit. Then any group picked out by the partner would be a mixture of the two one-slit patterns, and a mixture of those has no stripes; (c) shows striped groups, so that picture fails. Pilot-wave theory, below, is not this picture: its photon does use one slit, but where it lands is set by a wave that goes through both, and it reproduces (c).

Model 1: state vectors and measurement (two arrows for the tag). Intuition. The tag is a two-state thing: A or B for the atom; for the photon pair, the two joint-polarization states above. Draw the tag's state as an arrow of length 1. After "left slit" the tag ends up pointing one way; after "right slit", another; and, taking the overlap as a plain positive number as last time, the only thing about the tag that matters for the stripes is the angle, call it θ, between those two arrows. Their overlap, the cosine of θ, is the stripe strength: arrows alike (θ = 0, no record) give full stripes; arrows at right angles (θ = 90°, states that can be told apart with certainty) give none; θ = 60°, overlap 0.5, gives the half-strength stripes of last time's middle panel. (A complex overlap, a clock hand rather than a plain number, also shifts the stripes sideways; that is what the quarter-wave delay does in the photon experiment.) The sine of θ is the telling-apart power: how often the best possible reading of the tag would guess the slit right, rescaled so that 0 means no better than a coin toss and 1 means always right. Since cos² θ + sin² θ = 1, stripe strength and telling-apart power trade off like the two shorter sides of a right triangle whose long side is 1; that is the budget measured with the atoms, an equality for an ideal tag (a definite tag state for each slit, equal chances for the two slits) and an inequality for a tag that is also in a random state of its own. Erasing is measuring the tag in a way whose outcomes have the same chances whichever slit was used (a coin toss for a perfect tag), so that the result reveals nothing about the slit but fixes the sign with which the two slits' amplitudes now combine, plus for one result and minus for the other: one group shows stripes, the other anti-stripes, and the two together give back the pattern that was on the screen all along. For a single two-state tag, that means measuring along the direction halfway between the two arrows and its perpendicular; at θ = 60°, three quarters of the dots land in the stripes group and one quarter in the anti-stripes group, and the two add up to half-strength stripes. For the photon pair, the partner's diagonal polarizer is such a measurement: its result is 50/50 whichever slit was used, and the slit photon that goes with each result has the same diagonal polarization whichever slit it used, differing only by the plates' quarter-wave delay, which turns plus-or-minus into a quarter-turn each way, so each group's stripes sit a quarter spacing to one side of the untagged stripes instead of on them and half off them. Why this model. From nothing but two arrows and the rule "chance = squared overlap" it predicts the whole budget curve and the stripe/anti-stripe sorting, including the half-spacing shift between the groups and the flat sum. Pros. It turns "how much which-path information" into one number, and it applies to any tag at all: an internal state, the polarization of a pair, the direction a scattered photon went off in. Cons. The arrows live in an abstract space, not in the laboratory; there is nothing pointing or turning that you could see. "Measuring the tag" is taken as a primitive act with one outcome, exactly what the interpretations below disagree about. And once a tag has been written by many particles and spread further, the right measurement exists on paper but cannot be set up in practice, which is the subject of the decoherence section.

Model 2: the wave function with entanglement (one joint wave for particle and tag). Intuition. Last time the wave function was the particle's amplitude at each point. With a tag, the Schrödinger equation is applied to particle and tag together, and the joint amplitude after the slits has two parts: "through the left slit, and the tag in state A" and "through the right slit, and the tag in state B". The rule from last time stands word for word, only the final situation now includes the tag. If A and B are the same state, both parts end in the same situation at each spot and cancel there as before; if A and B are partly alike, they partly cancel; if A and B can be told apart with certainty, they never cancel and the chances add. Nothing has been destroyed: the stripes have moved into the correlation between where the dot lands and what the tag says. That is what "entangled" means here: particle position and tag no longer have separate descriptions, only a joint one; for the photons the tag is itself a correlation between the two polarizations, which is why neither photon alone shows it. Sorting is reading that correlation. Group the pairs by a result of the partner that says nothing about the slit but is correlated with the tag, the +45°/−45° polarizer. In the +45° group the slit photon's polarization is the same diagonal as its partner's, which the plates leave untagged, so the two parts end in the same situation again, "dot here, partner passed +45°", and cancel again; the plates' quarter-wave delay on that light sets where, a quarter of a spacing to one side of the untagged stripes. In the −45° group the delay is behind the other slit, so its stripes sit a quarter spacing to the other side: the two groups are half a spacing apart, each bright where the other is dark. (For a tag like the atom's, with no such delay, one group would show the untagged stripes and the other anti-stripes.) The time order plays no role, because a group is defined by both results together; nothing is sent from the partner to the slit photon, which is why the ungrouped dots never show stripes. Why this model. It is the same Schrödinger equation as last time with one more thing included, and no new rule; it explains the tag, the erasure and, in the next section, the surroundings with the one mechanism. Pros. The budget falls out as the overlap of the tag's two states, so Model 1 is derived rather than assumed. Cons. The joint wave lives in a space with three dimensions for the particle plus the tag's own, not in ordinary space; with two photons and their polarizations it is already something you compute with rather than picture. And the dot is still an added rule: the joint wave gives the chances for "dot here with tag A", not which of them happens.

Where decoherence comes in: tags written by the surroundings, then spread. The 2002 tag could be sorted out because it sat in the polarizations of two photons that were both caught, with a polarizer as the handle. Now let the tag be a gas atom that bounced off the particle on its way through. That atom's two possible outgoing states, "came off the left path" and "came off the right path", are the two arrows of Model 1, and when the gas atom's own wavelength is far shorter than the distance between the paths their overlap is close to zero: even a collision that barely deflects the heavy molecule is a near-perfect tag, as long as it passes on enough sideways push to tell the two paths apart (true of essentially every collision in such experiments). Two separate things then happen. First, more taggers: each further gas atom that hits the molecule adds a tag of its own, and the overlaps multiply, so the stripe strength falls fast; even if each tagger told the paths apart only weakly, say an overlap of 0.9 each, fifty of them leave stripes about half a per cent strong. Second, spreading: each gas atom flies off, hits the chamber wall, bounces off other atoms, and the record it carries is shared with more and more particles. That spreading does not weaken the stripes any further (the overlap between the two situations is set when the molecule is hit; later collisions among the surroundings alone leave it as it is), but it scatters the record over so many particles that sorting the stripes back out would mean catching every one of them and measuring it in the right way. Nothing forbids that in principle; nobody can do it in practice. That is decoherence: which-path tags written into the surroundings by ordinary interactions, continuously, without anyone intending it, and spread too widely to collect. Seen (Hornberger et al., Vienna, 2003): C70 molecules, 70 carbon atoms each, sent through a three-grating interferometer in which the stripes form at one particular distance behind the gratings (a near-field arrangement, not a far screen) and are read by sliding the last grating; the paths that make them are about a micrometre apart. The chamber's gas pressure was raised step by step. The stripe strength of the molecules that still arrived (not merely their number) fell exponentially with the pressure, each equal step in pressure cutting it by the same factor, over a range of a few millionths of a millibar, about a billionth of atmospheric pressure, as expected if each collision with a gas atom, whose wavelength of tens of picometres is tens of thousands of times shorter than the path separation, writes a near-perfect tag. Seen too (Hackermüller et al., Vienna, 2004): the same kind of interferometer in good vacuum, with the molecules heated by a laser before they enter. A hot molecule glows faintly, now and then sending off a photon that nobody detects. Straight from the oven (roughly 1,000 K inside) the stripes were at full strength; as the laser raised the molecules' internal temperature they faded, and at a few thousand kelvin they were nearly gone (the temperatures are inferred from the laser power with a model of how the molecules absorb and radiate, so they are approximate). The reading: a photon leaving from one path or the other is a tag whose two end states overlap almost completely if its wavelength is much longer than the path separation, and little or not at all once it is shorter than about twice the separation, Feynman's point in §1-6 that light of too long a wavelength cannot tell the holes apart; a hotter molecule sends off more photons, and shorter ones. Heat on its own is not a record: C60 molecules straight from an oven at about 900 K gave clear stripes in 1999, diffracted by a grating with slits 100 nanometres apart; a record is made only when something leaves the molecule carrying its position. Last time's dust grain is the same thing without a chamber: for a grain some ten micrometres across in ordinary air, each air molecule that hits it is a near-perfect tag of where it is, to within a grain's width, and the standard estimate, a calculation and not a measurement, has the stripes between two such positions fading in far less than a millionth of a billionth of a second. One honest limit: decoherence explains why the two paths' chances add, not why one dot appears; it says when a record is beyond sorting, and leaves the single outcome to the interpretations.

What the interpretations say "erasing" is. All four agree, to within differences far too small to measure, on every count above; they differ on what the words mean.

Copenhagen-style. Which-path information is what the records would let you predict about the slit; a tag is a record whether or not it is read, and "erasing" is choosing to measure the tag in a way that predicts nothing about the slit but fixes the sign of the cancelling. Nothing in the past changes; the sorting changes what you know about dots that already exist. Pro: nothing happens to the slit photon, so no signalling puzzle ever arises. Con: critics object that it still does not say at what point a tag becomes a record; supporters reply that decoherence tells you when treating it as one is harmless, even if not when it is compulsory.

Many-worlds. The joint wave is all there is. Tag states A and B are two branches, but in this view a world only splits for good once a record has spread into countless particles; a tag held in one atom or in one pair of photons has not split anything, which is why it can still be rotated back or sorted on a partner. Decoherence is the spreading that makes branches permanent. Pro: tag, erasure and decoherence are one process with no cut anywhere. Con: critics object that it is unclear what the chances mean when every outcome occurs; supporters reply that this is the wave equation taken seriously and that decoherence is what picks out the branches.

Pilot-wave. Every atom and every photon takes one definite route and lands where the wave steers it; the tag records that route truthfully. Sorting by the partner's later result picks out, from the dots already made, those whose partner the shared wave steered through the polarizer, and because the pair is steered by one joint wave the dots in that subset are striped. For the atom, a tag that is rotated back makes the two parts of the wave overlap again, and their contributions interfere in the steering again. Nothing is erased and nothing runs backward; the steering across the pair is instantaneous, which is the usual cost. Same predictions as the other views here (its treatment of photons is less settled than of atoms).

Objective collapse. A tag in one atom or one pair of photons is far too small to trigger a collapse, so erasure works indistinguishably from standard theory (the difference, the tiny collapse rate these models give to even a single atom, is far below anything measurable), and these experiments do not test the proposal. A record spread through a huge number of particles would collapse, but that is hidden under ordinary decoherence anyway. The tests are elsewhere: interference of ever larger molecules (about two thousand atoms by 2019), and searches for the faint spontaneous heating and radiation the models predict for ordinary matter, which so far find no departure from the standard theory and narrow the room for it (one proposed version was ruled out by an underground radiation search, Donadi et al.).

How they relate. Model 1 is Model 2 with the particle's routes grouped into two amplitudes and the tag's two states drawn as arrows: one theory, two levels of detail, and they always agree. Decoherence is Model 2 applied to tags written by very many particles, each of which scattered off the object; no new rule, only overlaps that multiply, plus the practical fact that the record then spreads beyond collecting. Copenhagen-style, many-worlds and pilot-wave agree on every count in both experiments (pilot-wave given its usual assumption about where particles start), so taste and economy decide, and the question is open; objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose difference is far too small to see for tags this small. Two pictures have gone: a record need not push the particle, and it needs no reader. One thing has not: why one dot appears, which decoherence narrows down but does not answer. If you want to read on, the passage in Feynman's QED, chapter 3, where he puts detectors at the holes is the shortest honest account of the "same final situation" rule, and Schlosshauer's chapter 2 is the next step up.
