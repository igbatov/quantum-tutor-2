Experiment 1 (Chapman et al., 1995) as the laser slides along the beam, so the separation d of the two paths at the moment of scattering grows from 0 to 2 photon wavelengths λ. Panel A is a schematic, not to scale: in the real set-up d at the laser is at most about 1.2 µm, while at the middle grating the paths are tens of µm apart. Each new atom scatters a random number of photons (drawn from a Poisson distribution with mean 1, so some atoms scatter none and some several), and nobody detects them. Panels B and C are computed from a simple model: emission equally likely in all directions (the real atom has a dipole pattern, which changes the details but not the half-wavelength scale); each photon multiplies the stripes by the signed overlap c(d) = sin(2πd/λ)/(2πd/λ); the photon number is Poisson with mean 1, which gives a visibility for all arriving atoms of exp(−(1 − c(d))); and the visibility with the laser off is set to 1. The common kick from absorbing laser photons is left out. What to notice: (1) atoms keep arriving and the mean count stays the same; only the stripe depth changes. (2) The total visibility (black) is lowest, about 0.30, at d ≈ 0.72λ, a little beyond λ/2, where the one-photon overlap is most negative. It comes partly back, to about 0.42 near d ≈ 1.23λ, then dips again (about 0.34 near 1.74λ) and settles towards e⁻¹ ≈ 0.37, the share of atoms that scattered no photon and so keep full stripes. It never reaches zero. (3) In panel B, the atoms that scattered exactly one photon (red dashed) have stripes shifted by half a period whenever c(d) is negative (between λ/2 and λ, and again between 1.5λ and 2λ). The total for all atoms is never shifted, because exp(−(1 − c)) is always positive. Only the ratio d/λ matters.
