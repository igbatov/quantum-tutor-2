Experiment 3 (2004), animated: the molecule's temperature rises from 1,000 K to 3,000 K. Panel A is a schematic and not to scale. One molecule travels on two paths about 1 µm apart (drawn much larger), and it glows more strongly as it heats up; the glow colour is only for illustration. It gives off photons, and each photon's wavelength is drawn from the ideal black-body spectrum at the current temperature. Photons shorter than 2 µm are drawn as thick, short, dark-blue wiggles. Longer ones are drawn as thin, long, red wiggles. The number of photons drawn grows as T³, the total black-body photon rate (27 times more at 3,000 K than at 1,000 K), but it is scaled for display: a real molecule emits only a few photons during its flight. Panel B is computed: the ideal black-body photon-number spectrum at the current temperature, on log axes and on one common scale, with the dotted 1,000 K and 3,000 K curves for reference, a blue line at the 1 µm path separation and a red line at the 2 µm "half-wavelength" limit. The hatched part is the photons shorter than 2 µm: about 2% of them at 1,000 K, 25% at 2,000 K and 49% at 3,000 K, and their total rate grows about 600-fold. Panel C uses an illustrative model, not a fit to the 2004 data. It sets visibility V = exp(−K ∫ n_λ(T)(1 − |sin(2πd/λ)/(2πd/λ)|) dλ), with d = 1 µm, an ideal black body, emission equally likely in every direction, and photons emitted independently. The scale K is chosen so that V = 0.10 at 3,000 K, which gives V ≈ 0.97 at 1,000 K and about 0.58 at 2,000 K. A real molecule's emission only roughly follows the black-body shape, and the temperatures in the experiment are inferred, so only the trend is meaningful. What to notice: the stripes stay at 1,000 K even though the molecule is hot, because almost none of its photons are short enough to tell the paths apart. They fade as the hatched short-wavelength part of the spectrum grows, and in panel A the dark-blue "record" photons become common.
