- Conventions worth keeping for continuity: positions in units of the stripe spacing; bold = what is seen, dashed = the two one-path chances added; d = 4a when the double slit appears.

## What the question actually asks

Two linked things: (i) *which-path information*: a physical record of the path removes the stripes, in proportion to how well the record tells the paths apart, whether or not anyone reads it; (ii) *decoherence*: the environment makes such records on its own, continuously and irreversibly in practice, which is why big things never show stripes and why a recorder can be treated as classical. The honest core that every candidate must land: decoherence explains why the two paths' chances *add* (no stripes) but does not by itself say why *one* dot appears; the interpretations differ on that and the question is open.

Classical candidates to rule out (each candidate picks the ones its experiments actually test):
- "The detector kicks the particle and the kick blurs the stripes" (Heisenberg-microscope / recoiling-slit disturbance). Honest status: for scattering-type records (a photon or gas atom bouncing off) the kick picture and the record picture give the *same* numbers (the fading factor is the Fourier transform of the kick distribution), so scattering experiments do not rule it out; it fails for records that give no kick (microwave-tagged atoms, 1998: the tag's nudge is about 10^5 times smaller than the optical recoil, and the stripes still vanish completely). Label the 1994–95 Storey/Tan/Collett/Walls vs Englert/Scully/Walther exchange as a real debate that was settled by Wiseman & Harrison (1995) and the 1998 experiment.
- "Something has to be read by someone": ruled out by every experiment here (nobody reads the gas atom, the photon or the hyperfine state).
- "The stripes are destroyed" (the record damages the wave, like friction damping a classical wave): ruled out by erasure experiments (the stripes come back in sorted subsets) and by decoherence happening far faster than any energy loss (cavity cat 1996; dust-grain estimates).
- "The particle took one path and the tag merely reports it" (classical ignorance): predicts no stripes in any sorted subset; erasure experiments show stripes in subsets. (Pilot-wave is *not* this: its particle does take one path, but the wave through both paths steers it, and it reproduces every result.)

## Candidate plans (fit 1–5)

1. **Tag without a kick, then untag it.** Dürr–Nonn–Rempe 1998 (atoms tagged by an internal-state flip; no usable kick; stripes gone) + Walborn et al. 2002 (polarization tags on photons at the two slits; stripes gone; a polarizer on the entangled partner "erases" the tag and stripes return only in sorted subsets, even when the partner is detected later). Models: state vectors and measurement (the tag as a two-state system, overlap = stripe strength, erasure = measuring the tag "halfway"), wave function with entanglement (final-A's "same final situation" rule extended), interpretations with the emphasis on what "information" and "erasing" mean. Fit **5**: directly answers "which-path information", kills the two commonest wrong pictures (disturbance; a reader), builds on exactly the rule the learner already has; decoherence enters as "a tag that has spread into so many particles that no polarizer can be arranged to undo it", with one real decoherence result in a short section. **Chosen (A).**

2. **A record nobody meant to make: scattering.** Chapman et al. 1995 (one photon scattered off an atom in an interferometer: contrast lost as the path separation approaches half the photon wavelength, partially regained by sorting atoms by their recoil) + Hornberger et al. 2003 (C70 fringes fade exponentially with background-gas pressure) + Hackermüller et al. 2004 (hot C70 fade as they emit thermal photons; long-wavelength photons can't resolve the paths) + the Joos–Zeh dust-grain estimate (calculation, not experiment, and said so). Models: wave function with an environment (each scattered particle a partial recorder, overlaps multiply, exponential fading, position as what gets recorded), sum over paths with the environment factor (pairs of routes; why heavy things follow one path), interpretations with the emphasis on "does decoherence explain the dot?". Fit **5**: directly answers "decoherence" with the experiments Schlosshauer's textbook is built around; no apparatus is "the detector", which is the point. **Chosen (B).**

3. **Dialling the recorder and the environment.** Bertet et al. 2001 (Bohr's recoiling slit done for real: a Ramsey interferometer whose first beam splitter is a cavity field; with about one photon the field records the path and stripes vanish, with tens it cannot and they return) + Brune et al. 1996 (a few-photon cavity field used as a "meter" left in a superposition of two states; a second atom watches the coherence between them die with delay, faster for more distinct states) + Myatt et al. 2000 (a trapped ion in a superposition of two places, with a deliberately noisy environment; decay rate grows as the square of the separation). Models: the meter chain (particle → recorder → environment, with sizes), a 2-by-2 bookkeeping table (the density matrix in words: chances plus one "can-still-cancel" number that decays), interpretations with the emphasis on Schrödinger's cat and "for all practical purposes". Fit **4**: the cleanest demonstration that "what counts as a record" is a matter of size and leakage, and the only route where decoherence is watched in time; cost is unfamiliar apparatus (cavities, traps), which must be drawn honestly. **Chosen (C).**

4. **Single-photon Mach–Zehnder and Wheeler's delayed choice.** Grangier–Roger–Aspect 1986 (one photon: anticorrelation at a beam splitter, yet interference), Jacques et al. 2007 (delayed choice), then a wave plate in one arm as a tag and a polarizer as the eraser. Fit **4** for clarity (two outputs, easy geometry), but its models would be the same two-state-tag models as plan 1, and it says nothing about decoherence. **Rejected**: would read as a rephrasing of A; keep as a one-line pointer inside A.

5. **Neutron interferometer with a spin flip in one arm** (Badurek, Rauch & Summhammer 1983: intensity fringes vanish, but the interference reappears in the final spin direction). Fit **3**: elegant, but duplicates plan 1's content with a less familiar instrument, and the sources (Rauch & Werner) are outside the learner's preferred textbooks. **Rejected.**

6. **Electrons with a detector you can turn up gradually** (Buks et al. 1998: Aharonov–Bohm ring with a quantum-point-contact detector; stripes fade as detector current rises). Fit **3**: continuous control is nice, but "quantum dot" and "point contact" are hard to picture at conceptual level, and it was already cited last time. **Rejected.**

7. **Stern–Gerlach interferometer and the Humpty-Dumpty argument** (Feynman Vol. III ch. 5 thought experiment; Englert–Schwinger–Scully 1988 on why recombining the beams needs impossible precision; a full-loop realization only in 2021). Fit **3**: a lovely bridge from "record" to "unrecoverable record", but mostly a thought experiment and the recent realization is hard to state confidently. **Rejected**; the "record you cannot rewind" idea is carried by C instead.

8. **Delayed-choice quantum eraser as the headline** (Kim et al. 2000). Fit **3**: famous but invites "changing the past" mysticism and has a four-detector geometry that is hard to check. **Rejected** as headline; Walborn's version in A carries the same physics with fewer traps, and A must state plainly that the unsorted pattern never has stripes.

9. **Qubit decoherence and echoes** (Ramsey decay in superconducting qubits, dynamical decoupling). Fit **2**: no recorded interest, numbers are device-dependent, and separating reversible dephasing from true decoherence takes care the learner has no scaffolding for yet. **Rejected.**

10. **Theory-first dust grain** (Joos & Zeh 1985 / Zurek 1991 localization-rate table as the spine). Fit **2** on its own: not experiment-first. **Rejected** as a standalone; folded into B as a clearly labelled estimate.

11. **The complementarity budget** (Englert 1996 inequality, measured by Dürr–Nonn–Rempe PRL 1998 and Schwindt–Kwiat–Englert 1999: stripe strength and telling-apart power trade off like the two sides of a right triangle). Fit **4**: one simple relation that carries the idea, and quantitative. **Rejected** as a separate strategy because its experiments and models coincide with A's; the quarter-circle picture is folded into A as its second figure.

12. **Feynman's spin-flip scattering** (Vol. III §3-3: a nucleus whose spin was flipped is a record of which nucleus scattered the neutron, so spin-flip scattering gives no sharp peaks). Fit **3**: perfect source for the learner, but crystal diffraction geometry is a new front. **Rejected** as a main experiment; B uses it as a one-sentence aside with the Feynman citation.

The three chosen differ on all three axes: A tags built on purpose and then undone (sorted-subset figures, arrows and a quarter circle); B records made by accident and never undoable (curves against separation-over-wavelength, pressure and size); C a recorder and an environment with adjustable size and noise (curves against time and against photon number, a 2-by-2 table).

### Strategy A: Tag it, then untag it

**Experiments.**
1. Dürr, Nonn & Rempe, *Nature* 395, 33 (1998). Cold rubidium atoms in a two-path interferometer made of standing light waves. A pair of microwave pulses, one before and one after the splitting, leaves each atom's internal (hyperfine) state labelled by the path. A microwave photon carries about 10^5 times less momentum than the optical photons that do the splitting, so the tag cannot shift an atom by anything like a stripe. Seen: with the tag written, the stripes vanish; with the pulses off, they are back. The companion paper (*Phys. Rev. Lett.* 81, 5705, 1998) wrote partial tags and measured that stripe strength and telling-apart power obey the budget (stripe strength)² + (telling-apart power)² ≤ 1.
2. Walborn, Terra Cunha, Pádua & Monken, *Phys. Rev. A* 65, 033818 (2002). Pairs of polarization-entangled photons; one photon goes through a double slit with a quarter-wave plate in front of each slit (fast axes at +45° and −45°), which turns the polarization into opposite circular polarizations depending on the slit, a tag that gives no kick. Seen: the pattern of this photon, counted on its own, has no stripes, with or without anything done to its partner. With a polarizer in the *partner's* path at ±45°, and counting only pairs, stripes return: one polarizer angle gives stripes, the orthogonal one gives stripes shifted by half a spacing, and the two sorted groups add up to the unstriped total. Lengthening the partner's path so it is detected *after* the slit photon changes nothing. Conditions to name: far-field (lens) detection; counting in coincidence; ideal wave plates.

**Classical candidates ruled out here:** the kick picture (dead: no usable kick, stripes still gone); "a reader is needed" (dead: nobody reads the hyperfine state); "the tag destroys the wave" (dead: the stripes return in sorted subsets); "one slit plus a label that reports it" (dead: predicts no stripes in any subset).

**Models, in this order.**
1. *State vectors and measurement*: the tag is a two-state system; draw its two possible end states as two arrows; their overlap (the cosine of the angle between them) is the stripe strength; the rest of the budget (the sine) is how well a best possible reading tells the slits apart. Erasing = measuring the tag along the direction halfway between the two arrows, which says nothing about the slit but fixes the sign of the cancelling, hence stripes in one outcome's group and anti-stripes in the other. Why: it predicts the budget and the eraser with nothing but two arrows; pros: fits every tag experiment, makes "how much information" a number; cons: the arrows live in no familiar space, and reading the tag is taken as a primitive act.
2. *Wave function with entanglement*: the joint wave of particle + tag; the rule from last time ("only parts that end in the same final situation can cancel") now with the tag as the extra part of "the situation"; the partner's polarizer selects a subset in which the two tag states have been made the same again (or opposite). Why: it is the Schrödinger equation applied to particle and tag together, no new rule; pros: one mechanism for tag, eraser and (next section) environment; cons: the dot still needs the chance rule; the joint wave lives in a bigger space than ordinary space.
3. *Where decoherence comes in* (short section, one real result): a tag that has leaked into many particles cannot be measured "halfway": quote Hornberger et al. 2003 (C70 stripes fading exponentially with gas pressure, around a millionth of a millibar) as the one example, in a paragraph, and say that undoing it is not forbidden in principle but requires catching every gas atom.
4. *Interpretations*, emphasis on information and erasure: Copenhagen-style (information = what the records allow you to predict; the eraser changes nothing in the past, only the sorting); many-worlds (tag states are branches; a small tag's branches can be re-merged by rotating the tag, which is why branching only becomes permanent once a record has spread into the environment); pilot-wave (the atom took one path and the tag records it truthfully; after erasure the wave's other branch steers again, so stripes return in the subset; same predictions); objective collapse (a one-atom or one-photon tag is far too small to trigger a collapse, so erasure works exactly as in standard theory; departures would appear only for large records, and none have been seen).
5. *How they relate* + a closing paragraph that names the two misconceptions defused (disturbance; a reader) and the one honest open point (why one dot).

**Plan.** Open with the two facts (a kick-free tag kills the stripes; a tag can be undone, but only in sorted groups), then describe the 1998 atom experiment with its set-up and what was seen, then the 2002 photon experiment, both with figures. Rule out the four classical pictures in one paragraph tied to specific observations. Present the arrows model first because it makes the eraser and the budget obvious; then the joint-wave model as the mechanism, pointing back to final-A's "final situation" rule; then the short decoherence section; then the interpretations read through "what does erasing information mean"; then how they relate; then one line on where a reader could go (Feynman QED's "measurement" pages) and the check question. Keep each section to one or two paragraphs plus its figure and sources. Cautions: never say the eraser "restores" the stripes on the screen (it sorts the dots); never say the partner's choice "changes" anything about the slit photon; say "fringes and anti-fringes shifted by half a spacing"; name coincidence counting and far-field detection as conditions; the budget is an inequality in general and an equality for an ideal, pure tag.

**Figures (one per section).**
- `a-atom-tag` (schematic): the two-path atom interferometer with the microwave pulses before and after the splitter; right panel, computed: far-field stripes with pulses off (visibility set by the real experiment's order, a few tens of per cent, stated as "idealized to this value") versus the flat pattern with the tag on; caption notes the microwave kick is ~10^5 times smaller than the optical one.

J. **Opening sentence.** "The record that kills the stripes does not have to touch the particle, and it does not have to be permanent: both have been tested directly, with atoms tagged by a microwave flip too gentle to nudge them and with photons whose tag was undone after they had already landed."

### Strategy B: Records nobody meant to make

**Experiments.**
1. Chapman et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 75, 3783 (1995), "Photon scattering from atoms in an atom interferometer: coherence lost and regained." Sodium atoms in a three-grating interferometer; a resonant laser beam (wavelength 589 nm) crosses the two paths where their separation is d, chosen between 0 and a few wavelengths; each atom typically scatters one photon. Seen: the stripe contrast falls as d grows, is nearly gone when d reaches about half a wavelength, and shows weaker partial revivals at larger d, matching the overlap of the photon's two possible outgoing waves (the Fourier transform of the recoil distribution). Regained: sorting the atoms by the sideways recoil they received (which fixes where the photon went) brings back stripes within each sorted group, shifted by an amount that depends on the recoil. Idealization in the figure: isotropic scattering (the real dipole pattern changes the curve's details but not the half-wavelength scale).
2. Hornberger et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 90, 160401 (2003): C70 fullerenes (840 atomic mass units) in a Talbot–Lau interferometer with gratings of period about 1 µm, in a chamber whose argon (and other gas) pressure is raised step by step. Seen: stripe visibility decays exponentially with pressure, over a range of a few millionths of a millibar (give only the order of magnitude), as theory predicts if a single glancing collision, which barely deflects the heavy molecule, leaves a gas atom whose two possible waves (for the two paths) are already fully distinguishable: the gas atom's own wavelength (tens of picometres) is a hundred thousand times shorter than the path separation.
3. Hackermüller et al., *Nature* 427, 711 (2004): C70 heated by laser passes before the interferometer, from about a thousand kelvin to a few thousand. Seen: the stripes fade as the temperature rises; the molecules then emit visible and near-infrared photons whose wavelengths are short enough to resolve the micrometre path separation, and emit more of them. Contrast with Arndt et al. 1999: C60 at about 900 K interferes fine; internal heat is not a record until something leaves the molecule carrying its position.
4. *Estimate, not experiment*: Joos & Zeh 1985 / Zurek 1991 for a 10-micrometre dust grain in air or sunlight: the stripes between two positions a grain's width apart would fade in a time far shorter than any ever measured (give it as "under a trillionth of a trillionth of a second" or in words that are a safe bound; name it as a calculation with stated assumptions).

**Classical candidates ruled out here:** classical wave with damping (fading would come with energy loss; here collisions are glancing and in the cavity-cat experiment, cited in a clause, coherence dies many times faster than energy leaks); "a reader is needed" (nobody detects the gas atoms); "collisions just knock molecules out of the beam" (the molecules that arrive have lost their stripes, not merely their numbers). Be explicit that the random-kick picture gives the *same* fading curve for scattering and is only ruled out by kick-free tags (one sentence, cite Dürr 1998 and Wiseman & Harrison 1995).

**Models, in this order.**
1. *Wave function with an environment*: every scattered particle is a partial recorder; its two outgoing waves overlap by a factor between 1 (long wavelength, cannot resolve the paths) and 0 (short wavelength); independent recorders multiply their factors, so stripe strength falls exponentially with the number of scatterings, hence with pressure, time, or temperature. The surviving pattern equals the two one-path chances added, indistinguishable from "it went one way, we don't know which", yet the whole (molecule + gas) is still one joint wave; the stripes have been exported into correlations no experiment can collect. Position is what gets recorded because collisions depend on position. Why: forced by the quantitative agreement (exponential in pressure; half-wavelength scale); pros: predicts rates, explains why position is the classical variable and why a hot molecule still interferes; cons: joint wave in a huge space; says nothing about why one dot.
2. *Sum over paths with the environment*: for each *pair* of routes, the environment contributes a factor that shrinks the cross-term for pairs it can tell apart; for a dust grain only pairs closer than a tiny distance keep their factor, so the grain's chances are those of a single classical path. Why: same theory, the natural language for "why does a big thing follow one path" (Feynman–Vernon 1963; Caldeira–Leggett); pros: makes classical trajectories emerge; cons: heavy to compute, rarely needed for one atom.
3. *Interpretations*, emphasis on "does decoherence explain the dot?": Copenhagen-style (decoherence says when a record counts as classical, answering part of the old objection; critics say "for all practical purposes" is not a principle, Bell 1990); many-worlds (decoherence defines the branches and keeps them from interfering; supporters say this fixes "which basis"; critics: probabilities); pilot-wave (the molecule has one path all along; decoherence makes the other branch of the wave permanently ineffective, so no erasure; same predictions); objective collapse (a real collapse hiding behind decoherence; the molecules of Fein et al. 2019, about 2,000 atoms, still interfere when isolated, which bounds collapse models; testing them means beating decoherence).
4. *How they relate* and the dust-grain closing: two reasons big things show no stripes (tiny wavelength; constant recording), now with numbers' orders of magnitude and named assumptions.

**Plan.** Open with "the environment writes which-path records by itself", then the single-photon experiment (one record at a time, with a knob: separation over wavelength), then the gas and heat experiments (many records, exponential fading), then the dust-grain estimate, each with a figure. Rule out the classical pictures against those observations, and be candid that the kick picture survives scattering data. Present the wave-function-with-environment model first (it is what the experiments were compared with), with the rule from last time extended to "every scattered particle is a recorder you cannot read", and the Feynman neutron spin-flip aside; then the sum-over-paths version as the way to see classical paths emerge; then the interpretations through the "dot" question; then how they relate; then the closing with the two misconceptions defused ("decoherence collapses the wave": no, it hides the cancelling; "big things don't interfere because they are big": no, 2,000-atom molecules do when isolated). Cautions: "exponential in pressure" is what was measured, the pressure scale only as an order of magnitude; the sinc curve is the isotropic idealization; "a single collision suffices" is the theory's statement, consistent with the data; never say the superposition is "destroyed"; decoherence time numbers as bounds unless confident.

**Figures (one per section).**
- `b-one-photon` (computed): contrast versus separation d in units of the photon wavelength, for isotropic scattering (|sin(2πd/λ)/(2πd/λ)|), 0 to 2 wavelengths, marked where the 1995 data were taken (0 to about 2 λ); inset schematic of the atom's two paths and the photon leaving from one or the other.

### Strategy C: Dialling the recorder and the environment

**Experiments.**
1. Bertet et al., *Nature* 411, 166 (2001), "A complementarity experiment with an interferometer at the quantum–classical boundary." Rydberg atoms cross two "beam splitter" zones that put the atom's internal state into a superposition and then recombine it (a Ramsey interferometer: the stripes are the probability of ending in one state as the phase between the zones is varied). The first zone is a microwave field stored in a superconducting cavity whose mean photon number can be set from about one to tens. When the atom takes the "absorb" branch it removes one photon. Seen: with about one photon, the field's two possible states (one photon fewer or not) are distinguishable, and the stripes are nearly gone; with tens of photons the loss of one is invisible, and the stripes are back. This is Bohr's recoiling slit done for real, with the slit's "mass" set by the photon number. Hedge all numbers ("about one", "tens").
2. Brune et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 77, 4887 (1996), "Observing the progressive decoherence of the 'meter' in a quantum measurement." A cavity field of a few photons acts as a meter: an atom crossing it in a superposition of two states shifts the field's phase one way or the other, leaving the field in a superposition of two states, the more distinct the larger the phase shift. A second atom sent after a delay tests whether the two field states can still cancel. Seen: the test signal decays with the delay, in a time several times shorter than the time the field's energy takes to leak out of the cavity, and faster for more distinct field states, in agreement with theory. Idealizations: the cavity's leak is the whole environment; the atoms are perfect probes.
3. Myatt et al., *Nature* 403, 269 (2000), "Decoherence of quantum superpositions through coupling to engineered reservoirs." A single beryllium ion in a trap, its motion split into two wave packets a few packet-widths apart, with a deliberately noisy electric field applied as the environment. Seen: the interference contrast (read through the ion's internal state) decays faster as the noise is turned up, and the decay rate grows as the square of the packets' separation.

**Classical candidates ruled out here:** "a measuring device is a special kind of thing" (dead: the same cavity field records at one photon and does not at tens; nothing but size changes); "a classical wave damped by friction" (dead: coherence between the two field states dies several times faster than the field's energy leaks, and in the ion the environment adds energy rather than removing it); "a reader is needed" (dead: the field is never read in the Bertet experiment, it is merely left different).

**Models, in this order.**
1. *The meter chain (state vectors and measurement)*: particle → recorder → environment. A recorder records to the extent its two end states can be told apart; a big recorder is not changed by one particle's worth of difference (its two states overlap nearly completely), so it does not record, while a small one does; the environment then records the recorder, at a rate growing with how far apart the recorder's two states are, and this second step cannot be undone in practice. Why: the experiments' dials are exactly this chain's variables (photon number, phase separation, delay, noise); pros: makes "what counts as a record" quantitative, and shows why the same object can be a quantum system or a classical pointer depending on size; cons: the chain has no natural end (von Neumann's regress), and the last step, one outcome, is still taken as given.
2. *The 2-by-2 table (the density matrix in words)*: when you ignore the environment, the particle's situation is a table with the two chances on the diagonal and one more number off the diagonal, the "can-still-cancel" number, whose size is the stripe strength; the Schrödinger equation for particle + environment makes that number shrink, typically exponentially, while the chances stay put; this is literally what the three experiments plot. Why: it is the bookkeeping every decoherence calculation uses; pros: one number to track, decay rates computable (e.g. the rate ∝ separation² seen in the ion); cons: it describes the particle alone and so cannot say whether the whole is still one joint state (it is) or which outcome happens; identical tables can come from "we don't know which" and from "entangled with something we ignore", and the table cannot tell them apart.
3. *Interpretations*, emphasis on Schrödinger's cat and "for all practical purposes": Copenhagen-style (the cut between system and apparatus can be moved; decoherence says where it is harmless to put it; critics: "harmless" is not "principled"); many-worlds (the cat is both, in two decohered branches; decoherence is what makes the branches robust; critics: why these probabilities); pilot-wave (the cat has one state; decoherence makes the empty branch irrelevant for good); objective collapse (a real collapse, at a rate that grows with size, hidden under decoherence for these small systems; the experiments above are consistent with no collapse, and larger tests continue).
4. *How they relate*: the chain and the table are one theory viewed at two levels; the interpretations agree on everything measured here; the one experiment-sensitive proposal is objective collapse.
