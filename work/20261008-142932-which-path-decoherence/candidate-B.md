Nobody has to build a which-path detector: a single photon bouncing off an atom, or a single gas atom brushing past a molecule, writes the record by itself, and that, done by the whole surroundings all the time, is decoherence. Last time the rule was that two parts of the amplitude can only cancel where they end in the same final situation, and that a recorder whose two end states can be told apart puts them in different situations. The experiments below use no recorder at all. The "recorder" is a photon that flies off, a gas atom that drifts away, a flash of heat radiation: things nobody built, nobody reads, and nobody can catch again. The models then have to say what that does to the stripes, and the honest ending is that they say why the stripes go, and not why one dot appears.

**Experiment 1: one photon makes one record (1995).** Set-up: a beam of sodium atoms passes through three identical gratings in a row (slits about 200 nanometres apart). The first grating splits each atom's wave into two paths that separate slowly, the second bends them back together, and in the plane of the third they overlap and form stripes with the grating's own spacing, read by sliding that grating sideways and counting the atoms that get through (a hot wire ionizes and counts them). Just after the first grating, where the two paths are still less than a couple of micrometres apart, a thin laser beam tuned to the atoms' yellow resonance (wavelength 589 nanometres) crosses them; each atom absorbs a photon from the beam and re-emits it in a random direction, about one photon per atom on average. Sliding the laser along the beam sets the separation $d$ of the two paths at the moment of scattering, from zero to about two photon wavelengths. Nobody detects the scattered photons. Seen: the number of atoms arriving does not change as the laser is moved, but the stripes' visibility (how deep the stripes are relative to the average: 1 when the dark stripes reach zero, 0 when there are no stripes) falls as $d$ grows, is nearly gone when $d$ reaches about half a photon wavelength, and at larger separations comes partly back and fades again, following the curve set by the overlap of the photon's two possible outgoing waves. Regained: the atom's sideways recoil is fixed by the direction the photon left in (conservation of momentum) and shifts where the atom arrives, so the arriving atoms can be sorted by recoil; within each sorted group the stripes are back, shifted sideways by an amount set by the recoil, and all the groups together add up to the stripe-free total.

**Experiment 2: a gas writes records at a rate you can dial (2003).** Set-up: C70 fullerene molecules (70 carbon atoms, about 840 times the mass of a hydrogen atom) from a hot oven fly through three identical gold gratings with slits about one micrometre apart. The gratings are set at the particular spacing (a few tens of centimetres) at which the molecular wave forms stripes in the plane of the third grating, with the grating's own spacing; the stripes are read by sliding that grating. This "Talbot–Lau" arrangement is a near-field one: the stripes exist at that distance, not on a far screen, and the pairs of paths that make them are about a micrometre apart. Argon (and, in other runs, other gases) is let into the vacuum chamber a little at a time, raising the pressure through a range of a few millionths of a millibar. Nobody detects the gas atoms. Seen: as the pressure rises, fewer molecules reach the detector, and among those that do arrive the stripes fade: the visibility falls exponentially with pressure (each equal step in pressure cuts it by the same factor), in agreement with the rate predicted by Model 1 below. The theory's reading, consistent with the data, is that a single collision is enough to erase a molecule's stripes: a gas atom at room temperature has a wavelength of a few hundredths of a nanometre, tens of thousands of times shorter than the micrometre separation, so even a glancing collision, which deflects the heavy molecule only slightly, sends the gas atom off in a state that differs, depending on which path the molecule was on, by far more than the atom's wavelength can blur.

**Experiment 3: a hot molecule writes its own records (2004), and a merely hot one does not (1999).** Set-up: the same C70 interferometer in good vacuum, with a laser beam crossing the molecular beam several times before the first grating to heat the molecules; the laser power sets their internal temperature, from roughly 1,000 K to about 3,000 K (the temperature is inferred from the laser power with a model of how the molecules absorb and radiate, so these numbers are approximate). A hot molecule glows faintly, like an ember: it emits thermal photons now and then, more of them and at shorter wavelengths the hotter it is. Nobody detects those photons. Seen: at about 1,000 K the stripes are at full strength; as the temperature is raised the visibility falls, and near 3,000 K the stripes are nearly gone. The reading: a photon that leaves from one path or the other is a record only if its wavelength is short enough to tell the paths apart. A photon with a wavelength much longer than the path separation leaves two outgoing waves that overlap almost completely and records almost nothing; one with a wavelength shorter than about twice the separation records nearly everything (the same half-wavelength rule as in Experiment 1). At 1,000 K hardly any emitted photons are that short, and few are emitted at all; by 3,000 K the molecule emits visible and near-infrared photons during its flight, each one a record, and enough of them to wipe out the stripes. Contrast: in 1999, C60 molecules straight from an oven at about 900 K gave clear interference stripes when diffracted by a grating whose slits were 100 nanometres apart. Internal heat by itself is not a record of anything; it becomes one only when something leaves the molecule carrying its position.

**An estimate, not an experiment: a grain of dust.** No one has put a dust grain through two slits, so this is a calculation with stated assumptions, first made by Joos and Zeh in 1985. Take a grain ten micrometres across and ask how long stripes between two positions one grain-width apart would survive. Each air molecule or photon that bounces off the grain is a recorder like the ones above, and whether one bounce resolves ten micrometres depends on its wavelength. Air molecules (wavelength a few hundredths of a nanometre) and sunlight (about half a micrometre) resolve it easily, so one impact is a complete record and the fading time is just the time between impacts. In sea-level air at room temperature a ten-micrometre grain is struck about $10^{18}$ times per second (counting impacts on its surface), so the stripes would be gone within about a billionth of a billionth of a second; in full sunlight with no air, roughly $10^{11}$ photons per second scatter off it, and the time is a few millionths of a millionth of a second. Even the faint microwave glow left over from the big bang, whose millimetre wavelength is far too long for any one photon to resolve ten micrometres, does it by sheer numbers in about a second by the Joos–Zeh estimate: each photon records only a little, and the little amounts multiply up. Assumptions: a grain of that size in those surroundings, impacts counted as independent, and the long-wavelength rule (fading rate growing as the square of the separation) used only where the wavelength is longer than the separation; beyond that the rate stops growing and simply equals the impact rate. These numbers are rough, factors of ten either way, and they are why nobody expects to see a dust grain's stripes.

**What this rules out.** A classical wave losing energy to its surroundings, like a sound wave damped by air: it gets dimmer everywhere but keeps its stripes, and in Experiment 1 the atom count stays the same while the stripes fade; in Experiment 3, molecules at 1,000 K, carrying far more internal energy than cold ones, keep full stripes, which go only when photons short enough to resolve the paths leave; and in a 1996 experiment with a few-photon field in a cavity left in two states at once, the cancelling between the two states died faster than the field's energy leaked away, and faster the more distinct the two states were. "Something has to be read": nobody reads the photons, the gas atoms or the heat radiation, and the stripes still go. "The gas simply knocks molecules out of the beam": it does knock some out, and the counts fall, but the visibility is a ratio taken among the molecules that arrive, and that falls too. "The molecule really went one way, and the record just reports it": then no sorting of records could ever bring stripes back, and in Experiment 1 sorting by recoil brings them back, in shifted sets. (The pilot-wave model below is not this picture: its particle does take one path, but it is steered by a wave that went through both.) One classical-sounding picture does survive these experiments: "the photon's recoil kicks the atom sideways by a random amount, and randomly shifted stripes average out to no stripes." For scattering it gives exactly the same numbers as the record picture, because the recoil and the photon's outgoing direction are tied together by conservation of momentum. Whether "kick" or "record" is the right word was argued over in 1994–95 (Storey, Tan, Collett and Walls on one side, Englert, Scully and Walther on the other), and the resolution was that the two bookkeepings agree whenever the record comes with a kick, so only a record without a usable kick can tell them apart. In 1998 atoms were tagged by flipping an internal state with a microwave photon, whose kick is about a hundred thousand times smaller than the kick of the laser light used to split the atoms' paths, far too small to shift the stripes by any noticeable fraction of their spacing, and the stripes vanished completely. The record is what matters; a kick is one way of making one.

**Model 1: the wave function, with the surroundings included.** *Intuition.* Write one amplitude for the whole: the molecule plus everything it has touched. Each particle that scattered off it is part of the final situation, so "dot here, gas atom flew off this way" and "dot here, gas atom flew off that way" are different situations, and the rule from last time applies: the two paths' parts can cancel only to the extent that the scattered particle ends in the same state either way. "The same state" is a matter of degree, measured by the overlap of the two outgoing waves, a number from 1 (a long-wavelength photon that leaves the same way whichever path it came from: no record) to 0 (a short-wavelength gas atom whose two outgoing waves are completely different: a full record), with the curve of Experiment 1 in between. Independent recorders multiply: three scatterings with overlap 0.7 each leave $0.7 \times 0.7 \times 0.7 \approx 0.34$ of the stripes, and since the number of scatterings grows with pressure, time or temperature, the visibility falls exponentially, which is what Experiment 2 measured. The pattern left over is the two one-path chances added, exactly the pattern "it went one way, we don't know which" would give. But the whole, molecule plus gas atom, is still one joint amplitude in which both paths are present; the stripes have not been destroyed but exported into correlations between the molecule and the particle that flew off. Experiment 1 shows the export directly: sort by the recoil and each sorted set has its stripes. For a gas atom you would have to catch it and measure it; nothing forbids that in principle, and nothing makes it possible in practice, which is all that "irreversible" means here. Why position is what gets recorded: a scattering depends on where the scatterer is, so what the surroundings copy, over and over, is position; that is why large things are found at places rather than in superpositions of places. Feynman's example of the same rule: neutrons scattering off a crystal whose nuclei have spin. When the neutron's spin flips, one nucleus's spin has flipped with it, a record of which nucleus did the scattering, and the sharp diffraction peaks are replaced by a smooth spread; with no flip there is no record, and the peaks are sharp. *Why this model.* It is the Schrödinger equation applied to molecule and surroundings together, with no new rule, and it is what the three experiments were compared with: the half-wavelength scale, the exponential in pressure and the temperature at which heat radiation starts to count all come out of it. *Pros:* it predicts rates, not just "the stripes go"; it says which variable becomes classical (position, for surroundings that scatter) and why a hot molecule still interferes. *Cons:* the joint amplitude lives in a space with three dimensions for every particle involved, which for a gas is an unimaginable space; and it says nothing about why one dot. After decoherence the molecule's own chances are those of "one path, unknown which", and from the molecule's pattern alone the model cannot say whether that is because it is entangled with the surroundings or because it really took one path: the two cases give the same pattern.

**Model 2: the sum over paths, with the surroundings included.** *Intuition.* Last time every route from source to spot got a clock hand, and the routes through the two slits gave two group totals that could point alike or opposite. Now add the surroundings: for every pair of routes, the surroundings contribute a factor that scales the cancelling or reinforcing between that pair, equal to how well the surroundings cannot tell the two routes apart: 1 for routes that scatter things identically, down toward 0 for routes far enough apart that a scattered particle leaves differently. For one atom scattering one photon, the factor between the left and right paths is the overlap curve of Experiment 1, so the two models give the same numbers here; they are the same theory. The new thing is a heavy object: among all its routes, only pairs closer together than the surroundings can resolve keep their factor, and that resolving distance shrinks with time as more particles bounce off. What remains is cancelling between routes so close together that they bunch, as before, around the classical path, and the object's chances become those of a classical object following one path, with nothing left for distant routes to cancel against. *Why this model.* Same theory as Model 1 in different mathematics, forced by nothing the other is not; chosen when the question is "why does a big thing follow one path?", where routes and shrinking factors are the natural language (Feynman and Vernon worked the factor out in 1963; Caldeira and Leggett used it in 1983 for an object in a heat bath). *Pros:* classical trajectories emerge in front of you, and it is the standard tool for a quantum object with friction. *Cons:* heavy to compute and rarely needed for one atom; it is as silent about the dot as Model 1; and "the object takes every route" is a slogan, not something the method says.

**Does decoherence explain the dot? What the interpretations say.** Everything above is about why the two paths' chances add. None of it says why a single molecule makes one dot rather than a smear of the two added chances, and here the mainstream views differ, with identical predictions for every experiment above in all but the last.

*Copenhagen-style.* Decoherence is welcomed: it says when a trace counts as a classical record (when its overlap is practically zero and cannot be undone in practice), which is part of what this view was criticized for leaving vague. The dot is the result; no mechanism is offered. Critics, Bell most sharply, object that "for all practical purposes" is not a principle: the theory should say what happens, not what we can get away with.

*Many-worlds.* Decoherence is the heart of the view: parts of the joint amplitude that have become correlated with the surroundings can no longer interfere, and those parts are the branches; the dot is the one in our branch. Supporters say decoherence answers "which branches?" (branches by position, because the surroundings copy position). Critics object that if every outcome happens it is unclear what the chances mean, and that branches have no sharp count; supporters reply that the chances can be derived and that branches need no sharp count.

*Pilot-wave.* The molecule had one path all along; the wave went through both, and the surroundings became correlated with the wave's two parts. The part the particle is not in is now tied to states of the surroundings that the particle's own part is not tied to, so it stops steering, permanently in practice: decoherence is why the "empty" part never comes back to matter. The dot is where the particle is. Same predictions as the two views above.

*Objective collapse.* A real, random localization hides behind decoherence: its rate grows with the amount of matter, and for a C70 molecule it is far too slow to see, so these experiments are explained by decoherence alone and set bounds on the collapse rate; the 2019 interference of molecules of about two thousand atoms, kept isolated enough that decoherence was beaten, tightened those bounds. Testing collapse means doing what Experiments 2 and 3 show is hard: isolating ever bigger objects from everything that could record them.

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2 are one theory in two sets of clothes and say the same thing about every scattered photon and every gas atom. Copenhagen-style, many-worlds and pilot-wave use that theory unchanged and agree on every count here; they disagree about what the dot is, no experiment has told them apart, and the question is open. Objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose difference is invisible for molecules and grows with size, and decoherence is the thing its tests must beat. So a dust grain shows no stripes for two separate reasons, now with numbers: its wavelength (for a grain of a millionth of a millionth of a kilogram drifting at a millimetre per second, under a billionth of a nanometre, a thousand times smaller than an atomic nucleus) would make any stripes far too fine to resolve, and its surroundings record its position about $10^{18}$ times a second in air. Two things to unlearn: "decoherence collapses the wave" (it does not; it spreads the cancelling into the surroundings, where it still is, uncollectable), and "big things don't interfere because they are big" (molecules of two thousand atoms do, when isolated; what matters is the wavelength and what the surroundings manage to copy).
