The record that kills the stripes does not have to touch the particle, and it does not have to be permanent: both have been tested directly, with atoms tagged by a microwave flip far too gentle to nudge them, and with photon pairs in which the tag was "erased", in the sense explained below, only after the tagged photon had already landed. Last time the rule was: a which-slit record fades the stripes in proportion to how well its two end states can be told apart, because two parts of the amplitude can only cancel where they end in the same final situation. This answer takes up the two questions that rule leaves open, whether the record works by jostling the particle and whether anyone has to read it, and then shows what "decoherence" adds: a tag that has spread into the surroundings so widely that nothing can sort it out again. Throughout, "stripe strength" means the contrast of the stripes, from 1 (dark stripes that reach zero) to 0 (no stripes), and positions on the screen are in units of the stripe spacing, as before.

**Experiment 1: a tag too gentle to nudge (atoms, 1998).** *Set-up* (Dürr, Nonn and Rempe, Konstanz). Rubidium atoms, laser-cooled to far below a thousandth of a degree above absolute zero, are released and fall through two short pulses of a standing light wave. The first pulse is the beam splitter: it sends part of each atom's wave straight on and deflects the other part sideways, so there are two paths instead of two slits. The second pulse deflects again so that the two parts come back together; they spread, overlap, and far below the atoms' positions are recorded. Where the parts overlap, the atom density shows stripes: the far-screen regime of last time. The tag: every rubidium atom has two internal ground states, call them A and B, which differ in how the magnetism of the nucleus and of the outer electron are lined up (physicists call them hyperfine states) and are separated in energy by a microwave frequency, about 3 GHz. With a pair of microwave pulses, one just before the splitting light pulse and one just after, each atom is left in state A if it went straight and in state B if it was deflected. (How: the first pulse puts the atom's internal state into a half-and-half mix of A and B; the splitting light is tuned so that only the deflected part has the two halves of its mix flipped in sign relative to each other; the second pulse turns "halves alike" into A and "halves opposite" into B.) Nothing in the apparatus reads the internal state afterwards; the tag simply sits in the atom. The only difference between a run with the tag and a run without is the two microwave pulses, and a microwave photon carries about a hundred-thousandth of the momentum of one of the optical photons that do the splitting (its wavelength is about ten centimetres against less than a thousandth of a millimetre), far too little to shift an atom by anything like a stripe. *What is seen.* With the microwave pulses off: stripes (not perfectly black and white; the real contrast was limited by the apparatus, and the figure idealizes it to full contrast). With the pulses on: no stripes, just the smooth pattern, the two one-path chances added. In a companion experiment the tag was written only partly, by adjusting the microwave pulses, and the measured stripe strength and telling-apart power stayed inside the budget (stripe strength)² + (telling-apart power)² ≤ 1, which Model 1 below explains.

**Experiment 2: a tag that can be sorted out, even late (photon pairs, 2002).** *Set-up* (Walborn, Terra Cunha, Pádua and Monken, Belo Horizonte). A laser shone into a crystal now and then produces a pair of photons whose polarizations are entangled: whichever direction you choose to measure the partner's polarization along, the result tells you the slit photon's polarization along that same direction. One photon of each pair, the slit photon, goes to a double slit; the other, the partner, goes straight to its own detector. In front of each slit sits a quarter-wave plate: a thin crystal slab that delays light polarized along one of its two axes by a quarter of a wavelength relative to light polarized along the other. What a plate does depends on the polarization that arrives. Light polarized along either of its axes passes with its polarization unchanged, apart from the delay. Horizontally polarized light leaves circularly polarized, its field turning one way behind the left slit and the other way behind the right slit, because the two plates are set with their axes at +45° and −45° to the horizontal; vertically polarized light also leaves circular, with the two handednesses swapped. So for a horizontally or vertically polarized photon the plates write a tag, left-turning behind one slit and right-turning behind the other, and a tag written in polarization gives the photon no sideways push. Everything is counted in pairs (coincidence counting): a dot of the slit photon is kept only if its partner was detected too, which is how the two photons of one pair are picked out from stray light. *What is seen*, scanning the slit photon's detector across the far pattern (the figure below uses the ideal far screen with the slits four times further apart than they are wide, d = 4a, as last time, not the experiment's exact sizes): (a) plates removed: stripes. (b) plates in: no stripes, the smooth two-one-slit-chances-added pattern. (c) plates in, and a polarizer at +45° in the partner's path, counting only the pairs whose partner got through: stripes again; with the partner's polarizer at −45° instead, stripes again, shifted by half a stripe spacing, bright where the first group was dark; the two groups together are exactly the smooth pattern of (b). (d) Lengthen the partner's path so that it reaches its polarizer and detector after the slit photon has landed: (b) and (c) are unchanged. What is never seen: stripes in the slit photon's dots when nothing sorts them, whatever is done to the partner, because the two groups of (c) always add up to (b); if it were otherwise, the setting of the partner's polarizer could be read off at the double slit and used to send a signal. (The same tag-and-sort physics has been done with one photon in a two-arm interferometer, and in the "delayed-choice quantum eraser" of Kim et al., 2000, whose four-detector layout is harder to check; the pairs version carries all of it.)

**What these two experiments rule out.** Four pictures that each cope with last time's plain which-slit result fail here. (1) *The detector jostles the particle, and the kicks blur the stripes.* This is how Feynman's lecture explains the light-scattering version, and for records made by bouncing something off the particle it is not wrong: there the kick picture and the record picture give the same numbers, which is why a real argument ran in 1994–95 (Storey, Tan, Collett and Walls for kicks; Englert, Scully and Walther against) over whether the kick could ever be dispensed with. Wiseman and Harrison showed in 1995 that the two sides were defining "kick" differently, and the 1998 atom experiment showed that a random push is not what does it: the only change between stripes and no stripes was two microwave pulses whose photons push a hundred thousand times too weakly to blur anything, and the stripes vanished. A tag in polarization gives no push at all. (2) *Someone has to read the record.* Nobody reads the atoms' internal states, and in (b) nobody does anything with the partner; the stripes are gone regardless. (3) *The tag damages the wave, as friction smudges a ripple.* A smudged ripple cannot be unsmudged by sorting dots afterwards; in (c) stripes sit inside each sorted group, made of dots that were already on the screen. (4) *The photon used one slit and the tag merely reports it.* Then sorting by the partner could only pick subsets of the same stripe-free pattern, never stripes. Pilot-wave theory, below, is not this picture: its photon does use one slit, but it is steered by a wave that goes through both, and it reproduces (c).

**Model 1: state vectors and measurement (two arrows for the tag).** *Intuition.* The tag is a two-state thing: A or B for the atom, left-turning or right-turning for the photon. Draw the tag's state as an arrow of length 1. After "left slit" the tag ends up pointing one way; after "right slit", another; and the only thing about the tag that matters for the stripes is the angle, call it θ, between those two arrows. Their overlap, the cosine of θ, is the stripe strength: arrows alike (θ = 0, no record) give full stripes; arrows at right angles (θ = 90°, states that can be told apart with certainty) give none; θ = 60°, overlap 0.5, gives the half-strength stripes of last time's middle panel. The sine of θ is the telling-apart power: how often the best possible reading of the tag would guess the slit right, rescaled so that 0 means no better than a coin toss and 1 means always right. Since cos² θ + sin² θ = 1, stripe strength and telling-apart power trade off like the two shorter sides of a right triangle whose long side is 1; that is the budget measured with the atoms, an equality for an ideal tag (a definite tag state for each slit, equal chances for the two slits) and an inequality for a tag that is also in a random state of its own. *Erasing* is measuring the tag along the direction halfway between the two arrows, and its perpendicular. Both arrows lean on the halfway direction equally, so the result is a coin toss whichever slit was used and reveals nothing about the slit; but it fixes the sign with which the two slits' amplitudes now combine, plus for one result and minus for the other, so one group shows stripes and the other anti-stripes. For the photon, left-turning and right-turning are the two arrows, the two diagonal polarizations are the halfway directions, and the partner's polarizer, through the entanglement, is what measures the tag along them. *Why this model.* From nothing but two arrows and the rule "chance = squared overlap" it predicts the whole budget curve and the stripe/anti-stripe sorting, including the half-spacing shift and the flat sum. *Pros.* It turns "how much which-path information" into one number, and it applies to any tag at all: an internal state, a polarization, the direction a scattered photon went off in. *Cons.* The arrows live in an abstract space, not in the laboratory; there is nothing pointing or turning that you could see. "Measuring the tag" is taken as a primitive act with one outcome, exactly what the interpretations below disagree about. And once a tag has spread into many particles, the halfway direction exists on paper but cannot be set up in practice, which is the subject of the decoherence section.

**Model 2: the wave function with entanglement (one joint wave for particle and tag).** *Intuition.* Last time the wave function was the particle's amplitude at each point. With a tag, the Schrödinger equation is applied to particle and tag together, and the joint amplitude after the slits has two parts: "through the left slit, and the tag in state A" and "through the right slit, and the tag in state B". The rule from last time stands word for word, only the final situation now includes the tag. If A and B are the same state, both parts end in the same situation at each spot and cancel there as before; if A and B are partly alike, they partly cancel; if A and B can be told apart with certainty, they never cancel and the chances add. Nothing has been destroyed: the stripes have moved into the correlation between where the dot lands and what the tag says. That is what "entangled" means here: particle position and tag no longer have separate descriptions, only a joint one. Sorting is reading that correlation. Group the pairs by a result of the partner that says nothing about the slit but is correlated with the tag, the +45°/−45° polarizer, and within one group the two parts end in the same situation again, "dot here, partner passed +45°", so they cancel where they did without a tag; in the other group they also end in one situation, but with one part's sign flipped, so they cancel where they used to add and add where they used to cancel: anti-stripes. The time order plays no role, because a group is defined by both results together; nothing is sent from the partner to the slit photon, which is why the ungrouped dots never show stripes. *Why this model.* It is the same Schrödinger equation as last time with one more thing included, and no new rule; it explains the tag, the erasure and, in the next section, the surroundings with the one mechanism. *Pros.* The budget falls out as the overlap of the tag's two states, so Model 1 is derived rather than assumed. *Cons.* The joint wave lives in a space with three dimensions for the particle plus the tag's own, not in ordinary space; with two photons and their polarizations it is already something you compute with rather than picture. And the dot is still an added rule: the joint wave gives the chances for "dot here with tag A", not which of them happens.

**Where decoherence comes in: a tag that has spread.** The 2002 tag could be sorted out because it sat in one photon with a handle on it, a polarizer. Now let the tag be a gas atom that bounced off the particle on its way through. That atom's two possible outgoing states, "came off the left path" and "came off the right path", are the two arrows of Model 1, and when the gas atom's own wavelength is far shorter than the distance between the paths they are at right angles: one glancing collision, which barely deflects a heavy molecule, is a near-perfect tag. The gas atom then flies off, hits the chamber wall, bounces off other atoms, and the record spreads into more and more particles, each of which now has a "left" state and a "right" state. The overlap of the two situations is the product of all the individual overlaps, so it collapses toward zero fast: even if each particle told the paths apart only weakly, say an overlap of 0.9 each, fifty of them leave stripes about half a per cent strong, and sorting would mean catching every one of them and measuring it along its own halfway direction. Nothing forbids that in principle; nobody can do it in practice. That is decoherence: which-path information written into the surroundings by ordinary interactions, continuously, without anyone intending it, and spread too widely to collect. *Seen* (Hornberger et al., Vienna, 2003): C70 molecules, 70 carbon atoms each, sent through a grating interferometer in a chamber whose gas pressure was raised step by step. The stripe strength of the molecules that still arrived (not merely their number) fell exponentially with the pressure and was gone within a few millionths of a millibar, about a billionth of atmospheric pressure, as expected if each collision with a gas atom, whose wavelength of tens of picometres is tens of thousands of times shorter than the micrometre or so between the paths, writes a near-perfect tag. Last time's dust grain is the same thing without a chamber: air and sunlight write its position into the surroundings faster than any laboratory can resolve, by the standard estimates (a calculation, not a measurement). One honest limit: decoherence explains why the two paths' chances add, not why one dot appears; it says when a record is beyond sorting, and leaves the single outcome to the interpretations.

**What the interpretations say "erasing" is.** All four agree on every count above; they differ on what the words mean.

*Copenhagen-style.* Which-path information is what the records would let you predict about the slit; a tag is a record whether or not it is read, and "erasing" is choosing to measure the tag in a way that predicts nothing about the slit but fixes the sign of the cancelling. Nothing in the past changes; the sorting changes what you know about dots that already exist. Pro: nothing happens to the slit photon, so no signalling puzzle ever arises. Con: critics object that it still does not say at what point a tag becomes a record; supporters reply that decoherence tells you when treating it as one is harmless, even if not when it is compulsory.

*Many-worlds.* The joint wave is all there is. Tag states A and B are two branches, but a world only splits for good once a record has spread into countless particles; a tag held in one atom or one photon has not split anything, which is why it can still be rotated back or sorted on a partner. Decoherence is the spreading that makes branches permanent. Pro: tag, erasure and decoherence are one process with no cut anywhere. Con: critics object that it is unclear what the chances mean when every outcome occurs; supporters reply that this is the wave equation taken seriously and that decoherence is what picks out the branches.

*Pilot-wave.* Every atom and every photon takes one definite route and lands where the wave steers it; the tag records that route truthfully. Sorting by the partner's later result picks out, from the dots already made, those whose partner the shared wave steered through the polarizer, and because the pair is steered by one joint wave the dots in that subset are striped. For the atom, a tag that is rotated back makes the two parts of the wave overlap again, and both steer the atom again. Nothing is erased and nothing runs backward; the steering across the pair is instantaneous, which is the usual cost. Same predictions as the other views here (its treatment of photons is less settled than of atoms).

*Objective collapse.* A tag in one atom or one photon is far too small to trigger a collapse, so erasure works exactly as in standard theory, and these experiments do not test the proposal. A record spread through a huge number of particles would collapse, but that is hidden under ordinary decoherence anyway; the tests are the large-molecule experiments, which so far find no departure from the standard theory and narrow the room for it.

**How they relate.** Model 1 is Model 2 with the particle's routes grouped into two amplitudes and the tag's two states drawn as arrows: one theory, two levels of detail, and they always agree. Decoherence is Model 2 applied to a tag made of very many particles; no new rule, only overlaps that multiply. Copenhagen-style, many-worlds and pilot-wave agree on every count in both experiments (pilot-wave given its usual assumption about where particles start), so taste and economy decide, and the question is open; objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose difference is far too small to see for tags this small. Two pictures have gone: the record does not work by jostling, and it does not need a reader. One thing has not: why one dot appears, which decoherence narrows down but does not answer. If you want to read on, Feynman's *QED* pages on measurement are the shortest honest account of the "same final situation" rule, and Schlosshauer's chapter 2 is the next step up.
