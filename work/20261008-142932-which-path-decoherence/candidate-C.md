Who records whom depends on who is bigger: in a set of experiments with single atoms and a single ion, the "detector" could be dialled from an empty box to a dozen or more photons, and the surroundings from quiet to noisy, so you can watch a which-path record being written and then spreading beyond recall. Last time, a which-slit record killed the stripes to the extent that the recorder's two end states could be told apart (the "overlap" of that figure: 1 means no record, 0 a perfect one). This time the recorder is a few photons of microwave light, and the two questions are: how big must a thing be before it stops being a recorder, and what happens to a record once the rest of the world gets hold of it.

**Experiment 1: Bohr's recoiling slit, done for real (Paris, 2001).** Instead of two slits in space, this interferometer uses two internal states of an atom, call them *upper* and *lower*, as its two paths. A rubidium atom, prepared in a very high energy level (a Rydberg state, which couples strongly to microwaves) and starting in the upper state, flies through two zones. In each zone a microwave field can take an upper-state atom into an even mix of upper and lower, and a lower-state atom into a mix as well. The first zone splits the atom onto the two paths; the second recombines them; a detector at the end reports upper or lower. The chance of "lower" depends on the phase between the atom's two parts when they meet in the second zone, and the experimenters change that phase with a dial (in practice, the frequency or phase of the second zone's microwaves): turning the dial swings the chance of "lower" between near 0 and near 1 and back. Those swings are this interferometer's stripes: a chance plotted against the dial setting, not dots on a screen. The first zone is the recorder: a cavity made of two superconducting mirrors facing each other, holding a stored microwave field whose mean photon number, call it N, is set in advance anywhere from nearly empty to a dozen or more. On the branch where the atom drops from upper to lower inside the cavity, it leaves one extra photon behind; on the branch where it stays upper, it leaves none. The second zone is fed by an ordinary, large microwave source. Idealizations: every atom crosses alone, the detector is perfect, and nothing else touches the field.

*What is seen.* With the cavity nearly empty, the swings were very weak; as N was raised to a dozen photons or more, they came back, growing steadily with N. Nothing about the apparatus changed but the size of the field in the first zone.

*Why size matters.* A stored field with N photons on average does not hold a definite number of photons; its count is spread by about the square root of N. One extra photon is a sure record against an empty cavity (none versus one) and an invisible one against a count that is already spread by ten (N = 100). Bohr imagined exactly this in the 1930s with the slit itself: mount it on springs, and the electron's deflection gives it a kick; a light enough slit would reveal the path, but then its own position would be too uncertain to produce stripes; a heavy slit shows stripes and reveals nothing. Here the slit's "heaviness" is the photon number, and it is dialled.

**Experiment 2: watching a record leak away (Paris, 1996).** Same kind of atom, same kind of cavity, but now the field is the meter and the atom is what gets measured. The cavity holds a handful of photons on average and is tuned slightly off the atom's frequency, so no photon is exchanged; instead the atom's presence shifts the timing of the field's oscillation, its phase, one way if the atom is upper and the other way if it is lower. Think of the phase as a needle. A first atom, put into an even mix of upper and lower by a zone before the cavity, crosses it: the needle is now tied to the atom, pointing one way with "upper" and the other way with "lower". A zone after the cavity mixes upper and lower again and the atom is detected, which leaves the field in a superposition of the two needle positions (which of two possible superpositions depends on whether the atom came out upper or lower). The two needle positions can be set further apart by shifting the phase more. After a chosen delay, a second atom crosses the same cavity and zones. If the field's two needle positions can still cancel, the second atom's result depends on the first atom's result; if they cannot, it does not; the size of that dependence plays the role of the stripes. Idealizations: the only environment is the slow leak of microwaves through the imperfect mirrors; the atoms are perfect probes.

*What is seen.* The dependence faded with the delay, in a time several times shorter than the time the field's energy takes to leak out of the cavity, and it faded faster when the two needle positions were further apart, both as the theory predicted.

*Where the record goes.* Light leaking from a field whose needle points one way differs from light leaking from a field whose needle points the other way. So the leaked light is a copy of the needle, travelling into the laboratory at the speed of light; the further apart the two needle positions, the sooner the leaked light can tell them apart. Nobody collects it. The field's energy, meanwhile, drains slowly: the record is copied out long before the meter runs down.

**Experiment 3: an environment with a volume knob (Boulder, 2000).** A single beryllium ion is held in the electric field of a trap, which keeps it at the bottom of a bowl-shaped valley of electric potential, and laser-cooled until its motion is close to the lowest state the trap allows, so its position wave packet is as compact as it can be. Laser pulses that push the ion differently depending on its internal state split the packet into two packets some distance apart, up to a few packet widths, each tied to one of two internal states. Then, for a set time, a deliberately random voltage is applied to the trap's electrodes: a fluctuating electric field, a stand-in for a hot environment, whose strength can be dialled. Then the packets are pushed back together, and the ion's internal state is read out as a phase dial is turned; the contrast of the swings is the stripe strength.

*What is seen.* The stripe strength fell as the noise was turned up; comparing runs with the packets at different separations, the fading rate grew roughly as the square of the separation; and all the while the random field was adding energy to the ion's motion, not removing it.

*Where the fading comes from here.* This environment does not record anything. The random field is uniform over the ion's tiny region, so it gives both packets the same push, and it never learns which packet is where. But a push changes the relative phase of two packets by an amount proportional to the push times their separation, so from run to run the swings shift sideways by random amounts; averaged over runs, they wash out, and twice the separation means twice the phase shift per push and four times the fading rate. This is the "kick" picture in its purest form, and it fades the stripes by exactly the law decoherence theory gives for a hot environment, whether that environment records or merely pushes; the second model below says why the two cannot be told apart from the ion alone.

**What this rules out.** "A measuring device is a special kind of thing, and only devices make records": dead. The same cavity field is a near-perfect recorder when nearly empty and almost none at a dozen photons or more; nothing changed but its size (experiment 1). "A record is like friction, wearing the wave down": dead. In the cavity the cancelling died several times faster than the energy leaked (experiment 2), and in the trap the environment put energy in while the stripes faded (experiment 3). "Someone has to read the record": dead. In experiment 1 the cavity field was never read; it was merely left different, and that was enough. One classical picture is not dead but demoted: "the environment kicks the particle and the kicks blur the stripes". Bohr's recoiling slit, and the ion's random pushes, really are kicks, and for a kicking environment the kick account and the record account give the same numbers; a real debate in 1994–95 over whether kicks explain every loss of stripes was settled by a kick-free record (atoms tagged in 1998 by an internal flip whose nudge was about a hundred thousand times too small to matter), which removed the stripes completely. Kicks are one way to lose the stripes; a record of any kind is the general rule.

**Model 1: the meter chain.** Take the rule from last time, that two parts of the amplitude can only cancel where they end in the same final situation, and apply the Schrödinger equation to the particle, the recorder and the environment together. It then says this. A recorder records to the extent that its two end states can be told apart: the stripe strength is their overlap. The first dial is *size*. A field of N photons on average, changed by one photon, has its two end states overlap by the square root of N/(N+1): 0 for an empty cavity, about 0.7 at N = 1, 0.95 at N = 10, 0.995 at N = 100. So the same object is a quantum recorder when nearly empty and a classical, non-recording beam splitter at a hundred photons, and the second zone, fed by a source of countless photons, records nothing for the same reason. The line between "quantum thing" and "classical apparatus" is drawn by the overlap, not by what kind of object it is. The second dial is *leakage*. The environment plays recorder to the recorder: anything that leaves it (the leaked light) or hits it (gas, random fields) ends in a state that depends on which of the recorder's two states it met, so the environment's two end states drift apart, and their overlap falls, typically exponentially in time, at a rate that grows as the square of how far apart the recorder's two states are, for the leaking cavity and the pushed ion alike. Each small record multiplies the overlap by its own factor: a hundred glancing records of overlap 0.99 multiply to about 0.37, which is where the exponential comes from (a steady stream of small records). Undoing a record means bringing its copies back together: for one photon or one tag that can be a pulse or a polarizer, for leaked light or a thousand gas atoms it is hopeless in practice, though forbidden by no law.

*Why this model.* The dials of the three experiments (photon number, separation, delay, noise) are exactly the chain's variables, and the chain predicted the shapes seen: a trend set by N, a decay several times faster than the energy's, a square law in separation. *Pros:* it turns "what counts as a record" into a number; it shows the quantum–classical boundary is about overlap and leakage, not about kinds of objects; one rule covers a built detector, a few photons, and a dust grain. *Cons:* the chain has no natural end (von Neumann pointed this out in 1932): environment, then more environment, then whatever reads it, then you; applied to all of it, the theory gives one joint state with two branches that can no longer cancel, and the fact that one result is seen is still put in by hand, through the chance rule. The chain says why the branches cannot cancel, not why there is one.

**Model 2: the 2-by-2 table.** If you ignore the recorder and the environment and ask only about the particle's two paths, the full description boils down to a small table: the two chances on the diagonal (upper and lower, each one half here) and one more number off the diagonal, call it c, the can-still-cancel number, whose size is the stripe strength. The pattern at any dial setting is the two one-path chances added, plus c times the full-strength wiggle; at the dial's dark settings, where with no record the chance of "lower" is zero (ideally), a perfect record makes it one half. (In the full theory c is a clock hand, a complex number: its length is the stripe strength and its angle shifts the stripes sideways; here it is taken as a plain positive number.) The Schrödinger equation for particle plus everything else, with everything else averaged over (that is, summed over every state the rest could be in, because you are not looking at it), makes c shrink while the two chances stay put; c is the product of the overlaps along the chain. This is literally what the three experiments plot: c against photon number, c against delay, c against noise and separation. Physicists call the table the density matrix, and the shrinking of c is what the word decoherence means.

*Why this model.* It is the bookkeeping every decoherence calculation uses, and it tracks one number. *Pros:* rates are computable (c falls at a rate proportional to noise times separation squared for the ion, at D²/2 times the leak rate for the cavity, early on); the same table serves a cavity field, an ion and a dust grain; and it says when something may be treated as classical: once c is negligible, you can treat the particle as being on one path or the other with the given chances, and nothing you can measure on the particle alone will contradict you. *Cons:* it describes the particle alone, so it cannot say whether the whole is still one joint state (in the theory it is) or which outcome happens. Worse for intuition: two very different situations give the identical table. "The atom is on one path or the other, we just don't know which" gives a table with zero off the diagonal. "The atom's two paths are tied to two environment states we are not looking at" gives, once c has decayed, the same table. So does the ion's random phase, which in any single run is a definite phase that nobody wrote down. The table cannot tell these apart; only an experiment that gathers up the environment could. The table is the right tool for predicting what you will see and the wrong tool for saying what is.

**What the models leave open: the cat, and four views.** Schrödinger's 1935 cat is this chain with a cat at the end: an atom that may or may not decay within the hour, a counter that records it, a hammer and a flask of poison. He wrote that the theory, taken literally, has the living and the dead cat "mixed or smeared out in equal parts", and meant it as a reductio of taking the wave function as a literal description of the large-scale world. Decoherence sharpens his question without closing it. A cat's two states are recorded by air, light and the cat's own body in a time too short to measure; its c is zero for every practical purpose; no experiment could ever show cat stripes; and the table for the cat is the ignorance table: alive or dead, half and half. But the un-averaged description, if nothing but the Schrödinger equation ever acts, is still one joint state with two branches. Whether that is a problem, and what to do about it, is where the mainstream views part. All four agree on every number in the three experiments above.

*Copenhagen-style.* There is a cut between the quantum system and the classical apparatus that describes it, and the cut can be moved; decoherence says where it is harmless to put it, namely anywhere past the link in the chain where c has become negligible, and the Paris cavity shows the cut moving with size. The cat is alive or dead and the theory gives the chances. *Pro:* nothing added, and this is how the experiments above are written up. *Con:* critics (Bell, most sharply) object that "harmless for all practical purposes" is not a principle, and that a fundamental theory should not need the words "measurement" or "apparatus" in its axioms; supporters reply that a theory of predictions needs nothing more.

*Many-worlds.* The joint state is all there is: the cat is alive in one branch and dead in another, and decoherence is what makes the branches robust, since once the environment has copied the difference the branches can no longer meet, and it is decoherence that picks out which states count as branches (positions, needle settings, alive or dead) rather than strange mixtures of them. *Pro:* no cut and no collapse; the chain's lack of an end is a feature. *Con:* critics object that if both branches are real it is unclear what "a one-half chance" means, and that decoherence makes the branches only approximately separate; supporters reply that approximate is enough for everything observable.

*Pilot-wave.* The cat, like every particle in it, has one definite state all along, steered by the wave; decoherence makes the other branch of the wave permanently unable to steer anything, because in the space of all the particles' positions the two branches no longer overlap, and nothing in practice can bring them back. *Pro:* a definite outcome with no cut; identical predictions to the standard theory for everything here. *Con:* the empty branch is still there in the mathematics; the steering acts instantly across any distance; critics call the positions unobservable extra structure.

*Objective collapse.* The wave function localizes itself at random, at a rate that grows with the amount of matter in the superposition. For one ion, or a few photons, that rate is utterly negligible, so these experiments see decoherence alone, with no room needed for collapse; the collapse would only show up as extra fading for much larger objects. *Pro:* the chain ends for a reason, and the reason can be tested. *Con:* new constants of nature, and the searches so far (interference of large molecules, hunts for the faint heating and radiation the collapse would cause) have found nothing, which has ruled out some versions and narrowed the rest.

**How they relate, and the dust grain.** The chain and the table are one theory seen at two levels: the chain is the joint state of particle, recorder and environment, still a single state with two branches; the table is the particle's share of it, with the rest averaged over, and c is the product of the chain's overlaps. Copenhagen-style, many-worlds and pilot-wave are three readings of that one theory (whether pilot-wave is a reading or a separate theory with the same predictions is itself debated); they agree on every number here and no experiment has told them apart, so taste and economy decide, and the question is open. Objective collapse is a slightly different theory whose extra fading grows with size, is far too small to see in these experiments, and is still being hunted in larger ones. Now the dust grain from last time: its recorder is every air molecule and every photon that bounces off it, each a tiny copy of its position, each multiplying its c by an overlap just under one; the stream never stops, so for two positions a grain's width apart c falls to nothing in a time far shorter than anything measured (a calculation, with stated assumptions about the air and the light, not an experiment). Two things to take away: decoherence is not something that happens once, it is a rate, and the rate is the physics; and "which-path information" needs no device, since an empty cavity, one photon, or light nobody collects will do, while a dozen photons or more in the same box will not.

**Check yourself:** A very long-wavelength photon bounces off a dust grain and can barely tell its two possible positions apart: the overlap of its two end states is 0.999, so after that one photon the grain's stripes are still 99.9% as strong. After a thousand such photons, one after another, are the stripes still almost at full strength, or much weaker?
