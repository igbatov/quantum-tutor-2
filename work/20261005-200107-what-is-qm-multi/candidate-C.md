Quantum mechanics is a new way of describing what matter and light can do. Everyday physics describes a system by listing its definite properties. Quantum mechanics instead gives an *amplitude* for each possible outcome. Amplitudes can reinforce or cancel each other, and they set the probability of each outcome when the system is measured.

Most of what people hear about the subject comes as a few catchy claims, each a grain of truth wrapped in a misleading picture. Replacing the four most common ones is a good tour of what quantum mechanics is really about. The subject's most famous image makes a good running example.

**The setup.** In 1935 Erwin Schrödinger imagined a cat sealed in a box with a Geiger counter (a device that clicks when it detects radiation), a vial of poison, and a speck of radioactive material so small that there's a 50% chance one of its atoms decays within the hour. If one does, the counter clicks, a hammer smashes the vial, and the cat dies. The popular version: "until someone opens the box, the cat is both dead and alive."

**Myth 1: "Quantum things are in two states at once."** Take the radioactive speck on its own. After an hour, quantum mechanics gives one amplitude for "an atom has decayed" and another for "nothing has decayed", sized so that each has a 50% chance. That combination is called a *superposition*. It doesn't mean two copies, or a half-decayed atom: whenever you check, you find one definite result. But it isn't just "one or the other, we don't know which" either, because amplitudes can cancel. Where alternatives can recombine, you can see this happen. In the double-slit experiment, electrons sent one at a time almost never land on certain spots that they do reach when only one slit is open. "Both at once" and "secretly just one" both miss what a superposition is.

**Myth 2: "Nothing is real until a conscious observer looks."** In quantum mechanics a *measurement* is any physical interaction that leaves a record, and the Geiger counter makes one the moment an atom decays. From then on, "decayed" is tied to a click, a falling hammer, a dead cat and countless jostled air molecules; "not decayed" is tied to all of those staying put. Alternatives that have left such different marks on the world can no longer interfere, so for all practical purposes they behave like ordinary either-or possibilities. This is called *decoherence*, and for anything as big as a counter or a cat it is practically instant. As far as any experiment can tell, opening the box later only tells you what already happened. Schrödinger invented the cat to show how absurd it is to carry the quantum description naively up to everyday objects; he introduced it with the words "one can even set up quite ridiculous cases."

**What's honestly still open.** Decoherence explains why a cat never shows dead-and-alive interference. On its own, it doesn't explain why one particular outcome happens. That is the *measurement problem*, and physicists still disagree about it:

- In some views the description "collapses" to a single outcome.
- In the many-worlds view every outcome happens, in branches that can no longer affect each other.
- In pilot-wave theory, particles always have definite positions, guided by a wave.

All of these agree on the results of every experiment done so far.

**Myth 3: "Quantum means random, so anything goes."** For a single event, like one atom decaying, quantum mechanics predicts only the odds, but it predicts them exactly. A carbon-14 atom (the kind used in radiocarbon dating) has a 50% chance of decaying within the next 5,700 years or so, and that is all anyone can say about it in advance. Yet in any large sample, almost exactly half the atoms decay in that time, every time, which is why carbon dating works.

In the same way, the *uncertainty principle* isn't vagueness or clumsy measuring. It's a precise rule: certain pairs of properties, such as a particle's position and its momentum (mass times velocity), can never both be sharply defined at once.

**Myth 4: "It's only about tiny things."** As far as we know, quantum mechanics applies to everything, cats included. Decoherence hides its strangest effects in large, warm objects, but its consequences are everywhere. It explains why atoms don't collapse, why each element glows in its own sharp colors and why atoms bond into molecules, and it is how transistors, lasers and MRI scanners work. Everyday ("classical") physics is what quantum mechanics looks like for large objects: an excellent approximation, not a separate set of laws.

**Putting it together.** Quantum mechanics is about how nature handles possibilities. A system is described by amplitudes for its possible outcomes. Left alone, the amplitudes change smoothly and predictably, reinforcing or cancelling. When an interaction records an outcome, their squared sizes give the probabilities. Some quantities, like an atom's energy, come only in fixed amounts, *quanta*, which is where the name comes from. How to calculate what you'll observe is settled, to extraordinary precision. What the amplitudes really are is still debated.

**Check yourself:** A Geiger counter left running overnight in a locked, empty lab logs the time of every click. Were those decays measured, even though nobody was there to watch?
