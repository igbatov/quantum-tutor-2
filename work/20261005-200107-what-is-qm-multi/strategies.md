# Strategies: "Describe what quantum mechanics is about"

Learner: conceptual level, profile empty (first question of the session; no past style choices; no recorded misconceptions, so the likely ones are the pop-science pictures most newcomers bring). Mode: new-question. Because the question asks for a broad overview, each candidate has to work as a complete answer on its own: what quantum mechanics (QM) describes, its central new idea (amplitudes that combine and can cancel, giving probabilities), quantization, what "measurement" means, the scope (everything, not just tiny things) and why it matters. The candidates differ in which door they use to enter the subject.

## Lenses considered

1. **Mechanism-first (fit 3).** Open with three principles: each way an event can happen has an amplitude; amplitudes for indistinguishable ways add; the probability is the squared size of the total. Then derive interference, quantization and the "observer" effect from them. The content is right, but stating principles before showing any phenomenon is abstract for a first-time conceptual learner. Once it picks a concrete vehicle it turns into the experiment-first candidate, so it is not chosen separately; candidate B reaches the same rule from data.

2. **Experiment-first (fit 5).** Send single electrons through two slits and watch them arrive dot by dot. Each one lands as a single dot, yet stripes build up; opening a second slit empties some spots; recording which slit each electron used removes the stripes. The amplitude rule then falls out of what was seen. This is the iconic experiment, it can be shown visually, it needs only arithmetic with signed numbers, and it goes straight to the central novelty. **Chosen (B).**

3. **Geometric (fit 3).** Treat photon polarization as an arrow and a polarizer as a projection, with probability cos²θ, or use Feynman's little rotating arrows. It is a powerful picture, but projections and cos² add abstraction this learner hasn't asked for. Rejected for now. It would be a good "deeper" or "another angle" option on superposition later.

4. **Classical-contrast (fit 5).** Build the clockwork picture (definite paths, any energy allowed, full predictability) and apply it to hydrogen. Clockwork predicts the atom collapses in about 10⁻¹¹ s while giving off a continuous sweep of light; in reality atoms are stable, every hydrogen atom is identical, and hydrogen glows in four sharp visible lines. QM's wave description fixes all three: a trapped wave gives an energy ladder, squeezing a wave costs energy so there is a bottom rung, and E = hf turns fixed rungs into sharp colors. This names the classical expectation explicitly, explains the "quantum" in the name, defuses the planetary-atom picture and connects to things the learner has seen (neon signs). **Chosen (A).**

5. **Analogy-led (fit 4).** Music: a guitar string's allowed notes correspond to an atom's energy levels, and the spectral lines are the atom's "chord", with the limits stated (nothing material vibrates; you always find a whole electron). It is honest and intuitive, but its natural example (atomic energy levels) is the same as A's, so the two would converge. It survives as a one-paragraph bounded analogy inside A and a one-line mention in B.

6. **Worked-example-first (fit 4).** One photon in a two-path interferometer with half-silvered mirrors. Each full route has amplitude ±½, so detector 1 gets 100% and detector 2 gets 0%; block one arm and each detector gets 25% while 50% is absorbed. The arithmetic is crisp, but it teaches exactly the double-slit lesson with a less familiar device and would duplicate B. Rejected; keep it for a "deeper" follow-up.

7. **Historical (fit 3).** Blackbody radiation, the photoelectric effect, the Bohr atom, de Broglie, Schrödinger and Heisenberg, then Born's probability rule. The story has narrative appeal, but it delays the core idea, invites the Bohr-orbit picture the learner would later have to unlearn, gives fewer tools for reasoning, and is prone to errors in dates and attributions. Rejected. Its strongest puzzle, the unstable atom, appears in A without the chronology.

8. **Question-led (fit 3).** A chain of predictions with polarizing sunglasses: one filter, then two crossed filters, then a third at 45° between them that brings the light back, then single photons. It is hands-on and engaging, but classical wave optics already explains the brightness changes. The honest quantum point (each photon passes or is absorbed whole, with exact odds) therefore arrives late and behind a caveat, and it would be a second experiment route next to B. Rejected for this broad question; it suits a later question on measurement or incompatible properties.

9. **Formal derivation (fit 1).** Hilbert space, the Schrödinger equation, the Born rule and commutators. The learner is at conceptual level with nothing recorded as understood. Rejected.

10. **Misconception-first (fit 4).** A newcomer almost certainly arrives with pop-science claims: "in two places at once", "a conscious observer creates reality", "quantum means anything goes", "it's only about tiny things". Replace each with the real idea, using Schrödinger's cat (radioactive atom plus Geiger counter) and carbon-14 decay as the running examples. Unlike A and B, this route can clearly separate established physics, interpretation and open questions. **Chosen (C).**

## Chosen: A, B, C

The three differ in:
- **Route:** a classical prediction that fails (A), an experiment first (B), popular myths replaced (C).
- **Main example:** the hydrogen atom (A), the double slit (B), Schrödinger's cat and carbon-14 (C).
- **Representation:** energy ladder, spectrum and E = hf (A); dot build-up and ±1 arithmetic (B); myth-to-reality pairs and decay curves (C).
- **Emphasis:** quantization and stability (A); amplitudes and interference (B); measurement, probability, and what is settled versus debated (C).

Each still covers the whole core briefly, so it can stand alone.

### Strategy 1: Classical-contrast: the atom that shouldn't exist (candidate A)

**Plan.** Give the core idea first: nature isn't clockwork; there are no definite paths in the description, quantities come in steps, and the theory predicts odds. Then state the clockwork rules plainly and apply them to hydrogen. They predict collapse in roughly 10⁻¹¹ s, atoms of every size, and a continuous sweep of light. Reality gives stable, identical atoms and four sharp visible lines. Replace the ball with a wave of possibility (explicitly not a wave of material) and let two properties of waves do the work: (1) a trapped wave has only certain patterns, giving an energy ladder (the guitar-string analogy, with its limit stated at once); (2) squeezing a wave costs energy, giving a bottom rung. Point (2) is the uncertainty principle at work, described as a property of waves rather than measurement clumsiness. E = hf, with every symbol defined first, turns fixed rungs into fixed colors (neon signs, sodium lamps, starlight); "quantum" means a fixed amount. Then generalize in four short bullets (steps, odds, waves of possibility, measurement as physical interaction with no conscious observer), list what it explains (chemistry, chips, lasers, MRI), and treat classical physics as the large-scale approximation. Defuse the misconception of the atom as a tiny solar system.

**Example.** The hydrogen atom: classical collapse versus stable atoms and Balmer lines (656, 486, 434, 410 nm).

**Figure request.** `a-hydrogen-ladder`, two panels. On the left, hydrogen's energy rungs E_n = −13.6/n² eV with arrows from rungs 3–6 down to rung 2, labelled by wavelength. On the right, a continuous-spectrum strip next to hydrogen's four lines. The learner should notice that each drop between fixed rungs gives one sharp color and nothing in between.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the set of rules that matter and light actually obey, and its central discovery is that nature doesn't run like clockwork."

**Check question.** If the rungs were spaced farther apart, would the emitted light be redder or bluer? (This is transfer through E = hf; the text deliberately doesn't say "bigger drop means bluer".)

### Strategy 2: Experiment-first: one electron at a time through two slits (candidate B)

**Plan.** The core rule comes first: nature assigns an amplitude to each way something can happen; amplitudes add and can cancel; probability comes from the total. Then the experiment, with three observations: one dot per electron, unpredictable landing spots, stripes in the statistics. Contrast with how water waves make stripes, then give the sharpest puzzle: opening a second slit empties a dark-stripe spot, which bullets could never do. Then the rule, as arithmetic in units of one slit's amplitude: +1 and −1 give 0, so no hits; +1 and +1 give 2, so 4 units of hits, four times one slit, not twice; over the whole screen, two slits give twice the hits of one, with electrons moved from dark stripes to bright ones rather than destroyed. Note honestly that amplitudes are really arrows. Next, "what is the electron doing?": neither "both slits at once" nor "smeared electron-stuff" is right; standard QM is silent about the path when nothing recorded it, and the interpretations differ here while agreeing on predictions. Then the which-slit detector: once a record exists, the ways end in different situations and their probabilities add, so the stripes vanish; a stray photon or air molecule is enough; no consciousness is involved; the measurement problem gets one honest line. Finally generalize: atoms (stable amplitude patterns, energy levels, sharp colors, "quanta"), chemistry and technology, quantum computers, the scope (everything; big objects are constantly being "recorded").

**Example.** The single-electron double slit, with a which-slit detector added.

**Figure request.** `b-two-slit-buildup`, three panels of simulated hits sampled from sinc²(u)·cos²(5πu): 50 electrons (looks random), 5,000 electrons (stripes), and 5,000 electrons with the slit recorded (sinc²(u) only, smooth hump). The learner should notice single dots, stripes that exist only statistically, and stripes that vanish once a record exists.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the physics of how matter and light behave, and at its heart is one rule with no everyday counterpart."

**Check question.** If the detector records only half the electrons at random, what does the screen show? (Answer: faded stripes; the dark bands are no longer fully dark. This is transfer of the add-amplitudes versus add-probabilities rule.)

### Strategy 3: Misconception-first: Schrödinger's cat, unpacked (candidate C)

**Plan.** The core comes first: QM describes systems by amplitudes for possible outcomes rather than lists of definite properties; amplitudes reinforce or cancel and set the probabilities when a measurement is made. Then announce the route (four catchy claims, each a grain of truth in a misleading picture) and set up Schrödinger's cat accurately (1935; Geiger counter, poison, a 50% chance of a decay within the hour). Myth 1, "two states at once": a superposition of decayed and not-decayed is neither two copies nor "secretly just one", because amplitudes can cancel (one line pointing to the double slit). Myth 2, "a conscious observer creates reality": a measurement is any interaction that leaves a record; the counter measures; decoherence explained plainly; opening the box later only tells you what happened, as far as any experiment can tell; Schrödinger meant the cat as a reductio ("quite ridiculous cases"). Then "what's honestly still open": the measurement problem, with collapse, many-worlds and pilot-wave presented even-handedly, all agreeing with every experiment so far. Myth 3, "random means anything goes": exact odds, carbon-14 (half-life about 5,700 years) and why radiocarbon dating works; the uncertainty principle as a precise rule, not vagueness. Myth 4, "only tiny things": QM applies to everything; decoherence hides its strangest effects; what it explains; classical physics as the large-scale approximation. Close by putting the picture together, including quanta and the name, and what is settled versus debated.

**Example.** Schrödinger's cat (radioactive speck, Geiger counter), plus carbon-14 decay.

**Figure request.** `c-decay-randomness`, fraction of atoms not yet decayed against time in half-lives: a 10-atom sample (jagged steps), a 10,000-atom sample, and the curve (1/2)^t. The learner should notice that individual decays are random while the large sample follows the prediction almost exactly.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is a new way of describing what matter and light can do."

**Check question.** A Geiger counter logs clicks overnight in a locked, empty lab: were those decays measured? (Answer: yes. This is transfer of "measurement = recorded interaction", testing the conscious-observer myth in a new setting.)
