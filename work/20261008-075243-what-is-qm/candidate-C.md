Quantum mechanics is the physics that explains what ordinary matter is and why it holds together, and the plainest evidence for it is light: make hydrogen gas glow, spread its light into a spectrum, and instead of a rainbow you get four sharp lines.

**What is seen.** Put a high voltage across a tube of thin hydrogen gas and it glows. Send that light through a prism next to the light of a hot lamp filament: the filament gives a continuous rainbow. Hydrogen gives four narrow lines, red (656 nm), blue-green (486 nm), blue-violet (434 nm) and violet (410 nm), more in the ultraviolet and infrared, and almost nothing between. The same pattern appears in light from the Sun and from galaxies billions of light-years away, every line shifted by the same fraction by the source's motion or the universe's expansion, which is how astronomers know hydrogen is there. (Ordinary hydrogen; heavy hydrogen's lines sit slightly apart.)

Second, atoms last. Hydrogen in a flask, in the Sun, in gas whose light set out billions of years ago: all give the same lines, none has shrunk, flashed and vanished, and every ordinary hydrogen atom, left alone, settles to the same size, about a ten-billionth of a metre across.

Third, Franck and Hertz (1914) accelerated electrons through mercury vapour and measured the current reaching a collector as the voltage rose. The current drops sharply each time the voltage has risen by another 4.9 volts, and once the electrons carry 4.9 electron-volts (the energy an electron gains from 4.9 volts) the vapour starts giving off ultraviolet light of one wavelength, 254 nm. The electrons are losing energy to the atoms not bit by bit but in lumps of 4.9 eV. (Real curves are shifted a volt or so as a whole and the drops are not to zero; the spacing is the point.)

**The classical atom, and why it fails.** Alpha particles fired at gold foil occasionally bounce straight back, so an atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass sit in a tiny nucleus. Put a light negative electron near it and apply Newton's mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism, which work flawlessly for planets, motors and radio, and you get a planetary atom, the electron circling like a moon. A circling charge radiates, so the electron should spiral into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second, giving off a continuously changing smear of light on the way: killed by atoms lasting and by the sharp lines. Orbits could have any size, so atoms need not be alike: killed by every hydrogen atom being the same. Bohr's patch (1913) kept the orbits but allowed only certain sizes, by decree, with light given off only when the electron moves between them. It reproduces hydrogen's lines but gives no reason for its rule, fails for helium and every heavier atom, has no rule for line brightness, and its orbits contradict the full theory, in which the lowest state has no orbiting at all: a stepping stone, not a model in use today.

**Model 1: Schrödinger's standing patterns.** Here a bound electron is not a dot on an orbit but a pattern of amplitude around the nucleus: a number at each point of space that can be positive or negative, whose squared size is the chance of finding the whole electron there if you look. A guitar string fixed at both ends can vibrate only in patterns that fit, one half-wave, two, three, never two and a half (or mixtures of these); in the same way only certain patterns fit around the nucleus, each with a definite energy, and ranked by energy they form a ladder. The analogy ends there: nothing is vibrating, the pattern is three-dimensional, the whole electron is always found at one spot, and hydrogen's rungs are not evenly spaced but crowd together towards the top, where the electron comes free.

Colour follows. When the electron goes from one rung to a lower one, the atom gives off the gap's energy as light, almost always as a single photon, and a photon's energy is proportional to its frequency (Planck's relation), so the gap sets the colour: bigger gap, bluer light. Rung 3 to rung 2 is the red line, 4 to 2 the blue-green, 5 and 6 to 2 the violets; drops to rung 1 are ultraviolet. Upper rungs are temporary; an atom typically drops within a millionth of a second. Franck–Hertz is the same ladder seen without light: a passing electron can give an atom a whole rung-gap or nothing, mercury has a rung 4.9 eV above its lowest (the one electrons most readily lift it to), and a 254 nm photon carries 4.9 eV.

Stability follows too. Squeeze a pattern into less room and it must bend more sharply, and sharper bends mean more of what the theory counts as energy of motion (half the wavelength, four times the energy). The nucleus's pull favours small patterns, the bending cost favours large ones, and the bottom rung is where they balance. No rung lies below it, so there is nothing to fall to: that is why atoms last, and why hydrogen atoms are alike, since the same patterns fit around the same nucleus.

*Why this model:* it produces the ladder instead of decreeing it, gives hydrogen's lines and their brightnesses, and goes on to helium, every other atom, molecules and solids. *Pros:* pictures (the clouds chemists call orbitals) you can reason with; the workhorse of atomic, chemical and materials physics. *Cons:* for many electrons the pattern lives in a space with three dimensions per electron, so beyond a few electrons exact computation is impractical and approximations take over; and "one whole electron, found at one spot, with these chances" is an added rule, not something the pattern's own equation produces.

**Model 2: Heisenberg's tables.** Heisenberg (1925) started from what is seen, the frequencies and brightnesses of the lines, arranged in a table with a row and a column for every rung, each entry describing one possible drop, and found rules for combining such tables. They give the same ladder, and Schrödinger showed in 1926 that the two are one theory in different clothing. *Why this model:* when a system has only a few states, such as an atom's magnetism or a qubit, the tables are small and the wave picture is unnecessary. *Pros:* exact, compact, no picture to misread. *Cons:* no picture to lean on; clumsy for positions and motion. A third equivalent formulation, Feynman's sum over paths, adds up contributions from every route the electron could take; it is the main tool of particle physics and famously awkward for atoms.

**What the cloud means.** Here the mainstream interpretations part ways, and the lines decide nothing. Copenhagen-style: the pattern is a catalogue of chances for what you would find; where the electron is before you look is not a question the theory answers (critics object that "look" is never defined). Many-worlds: the pattern is the electron's whole real state; looking makes you part of it, one branch for each place it is found (supporters say nothing is added; critics ask where the chances then come from). Pilot-wave: the electron has an exact position at every moment, steered by the pattern; in hydrogen's lowest state it sits still, what the standard theory counts as energy of motion being here the energy of the wave's push, and across many atoms the positions are spread like the cloud (critics call them unobservable extras). Objective collapse: the pattern is real and localizes itself at random, so rarely for one electron that a single atom is practically unaffected. All four give the same hydrogen lines; objective collapse adds effects too small to have shown up in any atomic experiment so far.

**How they relate.** Patterns, tables and paths are one theory, chosen by convenience: patterns for atoms and molecules, tables for few-state systems, paths for particle physics. The interpretations change no line and are chosen by taste and economy; only objective collapse predicts anything different, for objects far larger than atoms, and experiments on such objects are under way.

So quantum mechanics is about this: matter comes in allowed states, which form ladders; what you find when you look comes by chance, from clouds; beneath both is a pattern of amplitude that fits, like a wave, in the room it has. Add one more quantum rule, that no two electrons can share a state, and the periodic table, chemical bonds, metals and insulators follow; the ladders of atoms and crystals are what lasers, LEDs and transistors are built on. The full quantum theory now predicts hydrogen's lines to better than a part in a billion; what the cloud is remains debated.
