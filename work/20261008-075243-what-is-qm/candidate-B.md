Quantum mechanics is the physics of atoms, electrons and light, and one of its most basic lessons can be seen with a beam of silver atoms, a magnet and a glass plate: nature answers some questions with only a few possible answers, by chance, and asking one question can erase the answer to another. It is the theory underneath chemistry, materials, lasers and transistors.

**One magnet (Stern and Gerlach, 1922).** Silver atoms evaporating from an oven form a narrow beam that flies through a vacuum, between the poles of a magnet shaped so that the field is much stronger near one pole than the other, and lands on a glass plate. Each silver atom is a tiny magnet because of one of its electrons: an electron's magnetism is built in (physicists call the property behind it "spin", though nothing is rotating), and a non-uniform field pushes a tiny magnet along the line from one pole to the other, call it up or down, in a direction and by an amount that depend on how the magnet is oriented. *Seen:* two separate bands on the plate, one shifted up and one down, with nothing in between at the centre of the pattern. Idealization: for a very narrow beam, two clean spots with an empty gap; on the real plate the two bands join at their ends, where the field is weaker.

**Magnets in sequence.** Block the "down" band and send the "up" atoms through a second, identical magnet. *Seen:* all come out "up". Send "up" atoms instead through a magnet turned by 90°, so that it sorts left from right (call the up/down magnet Z and this one X). *Seen:* half left, half right, and nobody can predict which way a given atom will go. Take the "left" atoms and send them through a third magnet set like the first, along Z. *Seen:* half up, half down; the earlier "up" answer is gone. Counts are averages over many atoms; 100%, 50% and 0% are for an ideal set-up. Sequences like these have been realized in several forms with atoms and with neutrons; the clean three-magnet version is their textbook idealization.

**Classical pictures, and what kills each.** Tiny bar magnets pointing every which way: the push would take every value between the two extremes, giving one continuous band; killed by the two separate bands. A subtler picture: each atom carries fixed labels, up/down and left/right, and each magnet is a sieve that reads one off without changing anything. This fits the first three experiments; the third magnet kills it, because every atom reaching it was labelled "up", so a sieve would have returned all of them "up", not half "down". What survives is any picture in which a magnet changes the atom as it sorts it. All the quantum models are of that kind; what they add is a precise account of the change.

**Model 1: states as directions, magnets as questions.** *Intuition.* Picture the atom's magnetic state as a point on a globe. A magnet set along some axis asks one question, "towards this axis's north pole or its south pole?", and the question has only two possible answers, ever: hence two spots, never a third. The chance of each answer depends only on the angle between the atom's point and the magnet's axis: certain at the pole, 50/50 at 90° from it, zero at the opposite pole, and in between, the nearer a pole, the likelier that answer. After the answer, the point sits at the pole of the answer given (for an ideal magnet). That is the whole model. The oven lines nothing up, so, as far as any experiment can tell, atoms arrive with their points scattered evenly over the globe: half up, half down. A repeated question finds the point already at its pole: same answer. An X question finds it 90° from both left and right: 50/50, and leaves it at "left", which is 90° from up and from down, so a fresh Z question is 50/50 again. In this model nothing was hidden and scrambled; the state simply no longer carries the old answer, because no point on the globe is at a Z pole and an X pole at once.

*Why this model.* It is the simplest known structure that gives all four observations together with the chance at every angle in between, and that chance rule has been checked at many angles with neutrons and with the polarization of light, another two-answer system. The same globe describes anything with two possible answers; with directions in bigger abstract spaces, which have no globe to picture, it describes every quantum system. *Pros:* compact, exact, general; the language of quantum computing, where a qubit is exactly one of these globes. *Cons:* it treats "asking" as a primitive and says nothing about what the magnet and plate physically do; the globe is exact only for two-answer questions; the atom's position is left out.

**Model 2: the same magnet as a splitting wave.** *Intuition.* The atom also has a wave of amplitude for where it is, its wave function. Inside the magnet, the part of the state tied to "north" is pushed one way and the part tied to "south" the other, so the wave splits into two separating packets, one per answer, and the plate finds the whole atom in one of them, with chances given by the squared sizes of the two parts. The magnet is not a black box: it ties the atom's magnetism to a position the plate can see. *Why this model.* It shows the question being asked as ordinary motion, right up to the dot, and it predicts something no label-scrambling sieve could: send Z-up atoms into the X magnet, steer its two packets back together before anything records which is which, and the original state returns, so a later Z magnet gives "up" every time (in an ideal set-up; in the real experiments, nearly). This has been done with atoms in recent years. *Pros:* the same wave function describes atoms, molecules and solids, so most of a measurement becomes ordinary physics. *Cons:* more to hold in mind; and the last step, "one whole atom, in one packet, with these chances", is still an added rule, so both models share that open question.

**What happens at the dot?** Here physicists divide; every view below gives identical counts for everything above.

*Copenhagen-style:* the point and the packets are tools for predicting what the plate will show; the dot is the result, and before it "up or down?" has no value the theory assigns. Pro: minimal; it matches how physicists use the theory day to day. Con: critics object that it never says what makes the plate a recorder and the magnet not.

*Many-worlds:* both packets persist, the plate's atoms join each, and from then on there are two branches, upper dot and lower dot, with a version of you in each. Pro: no added rule. Con: critics object that if both outcomes happen it is unclear what "a 50% chance" means; supporters reply that the squared sizes are what a branch's inhabitant should expect.

*Pilot-wave:* the atom always has an exact position and rides inside one packet; which one depends on exactly where in the beam it started, so each outcome is determined but unpredictable in practice, and no up/down label is involved. Pro: a clear picture with nothing vague at the dot. Con: for several atoms the guidance acts instantly across any distance, and its extension to light and high-energy physics is less settled.

*Objective collapse:* the packets are real and persist until the plate, where the vast number of particles involved triggers a genuine, random localization within a tiny fraction of a second. Pro: a mechanism, and a testable one. Con: new constants of nature, and experiments with large molecules and tiny vibrating devices have so far found no sign of collapse, only limits on how strong it could be.

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2 are one theory at two levels of detail: the first leaves the atom's position out and treats the magnet as a question, the second puts the position in and shows the question being asked; they never disagree. The four interpretations agree on every count in the figures and differ on what is real and on what happens at the dot; what decides between them today is taste and conceptual economy, and, for objective collapse, experiments with ever larger objects. Zoomed out, quantum mechanics is about states that are directions rather than lists of fixed facts, questions with only a few possible answers, chances set by angles, and answers that update the state and erase others. The same kind of two-answer magnetism in the hydrogen nuclei of your body is what an MRI scanner works with, and a quantum computer's qubit is one of these globes.
