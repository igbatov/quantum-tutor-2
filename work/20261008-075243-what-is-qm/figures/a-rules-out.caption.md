What each classical picture predicts, compared with what is seen (schematic set-ups, but every curve and dot pattern is computed; horizontal scales differ between the top and bottom rows). (a) Tiny balls flying straight through slits of width a = d/4 in a nearly parallel beam: two bands, one behind each slit, and each band is exactly that slit's own hits. (b) The same balls, with those passing close to a slit edge deflected by a random bounce (a simple made-up bounce rule): wider bands that merge in the middle, but each ball still uses one slit, so the both-open curve is the sum of the two one-slit curves and is never below either; opening a slit only adds hits. (c) A classical wave on a far screen (the ideal far-screen pattern of the earlier figures): stripes, but the energy arrives continuously over the whole pattern, and dimming the source 10× gives the same shape, fainter everywhere, never a single dot. (d) What is seen with electrons, one at a time: whole dots at unpredictable places (30 electrons) that build up stripes (3,000 electrons); near the middle, the dark stripes get hits with one slit open (grey) and none with both (ideal set-up). Notice: (a) and (b) fail because a second slit takes hits away in (d); (c) fails because electrons arrive as single dots.
