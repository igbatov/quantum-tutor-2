Quantum mechanics is the physics that explains what ordinary matter is and why it holds together, and the plainest evidence for it is light: make hydrogen gas glow, spread its light into a spectrum, and instead of a rainbow you get a few sharp coloured lines.

**What is seen.** Put a high voltage across a tube of thin hydrogen gas and it glows. Send that light through a prism next to the light of a hot lamp filament: the filament gives a continuous rainbow. Hydrogen gives four bright narrow lines, red (656 nm, nanometres, billionths of a metre), blue-green (486 nm), blue-violet (434 nm) and violet (410 nm), then fainter and fainter lines crowding together at the violet edge of sight (397, 389, 384 nm) and on into the ultraviolet, further sets of lines in the deep ultraviolet and the infrared, and almost nothing between. The same wavelengths show up in sunlight as dark lines cutting across its rainbow (hydrogen in the Sun's outer layers absorbs exactly those colours), and as bright or dark lines in light from galaxies billions of light-years away, every line shifted by the same fraction by the source's motion or the universe's expansion, which is how astronomers know hydrogen is there. (Ordinary hydrogen; heavy hydrogen's lines sit slightly apart.)

Second, atoms last. Hydrogen in a flask today, in the Sun, and in gas whose light set out billions of years ago all show the same wavelengths, so atoms then were the same as atoms now, as far as anyone can measure; and hydrogen atoms are alike: left alone, each settles to the same size, about a ten-billionth of a metre across.

Third, Franck and Hertz (1914) accelerated electrons through mercury vapour and measured the current reaching a collector as the voltage rose. Instead of climbing steadily, the current drops sharply at roughly regular steps, about every 4.9 volts, and once the electrons carry 4.9 electron-volts (the energy an electron gains from 4.9 volts) the vapour starts giving off ultraviolet light of one wavelength, 254 nm. The electrons are handing energy to the atoms' insides not bit by bit but in lumps of 4.9 eV; an electron that merely bounces off an atom loses only a minute recoil share. (Real curves are shifted a volt or so as a whole, the drops are not to zero and the step spacing wanders slightly; the near-regular steps are the point.)

**The classical atom, and why it fails.** Alpha particles fired at gold foil occasionally bounce back, so an atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass sit in a tiny nucleus. Put a light negative electron near it and apply Newton's mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism, which describe planets, motors and radio with great accuracy, and you get a planetary atom, the electron circling like a moon. A circling charge radiates, so the electron should spiral into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second, giving off a continuously changing smear of light on the way: killed by atoms lasting and by the sharp lines. Orbits could have any size, so atoms need not be alike: killed by every hydrogen atom being the same. Bohr's patch (1913) kept the orbits but allowed only certain sizes, by decree, with light given off only when the electron moves between them. It reproduces hydrogen's lines (and those of any other one-electron atom, such as ionized helium) but gives no reason for its rule, fails for neutral helium and every atom with more than one electron, gives only rough estimates of line brightness, and its orbits contradict the full theory, in which the lowest state has no orbiting at all: a stepping stone, not a model in use today.

**Model 1: Schrödinger's standing patterns.** Here a bound electron is not a dot on an orbit but a pattern of amplitude around the nucleus: a number at each point of space with a size and a direction, like a clock hand (the fitting patterns described next can be chosen so that, at any one moment, the number is simply positive or negative); the squared size tells how likely you are to find the whole electron in a small region there if you look. A guitar string fixed at both ends can vibrate only in patterns that fit, one half-wave, two, three, never two and a half (or mixtures of these); in the same way only certain patterns, and mixtures of them, fit around the nucleus. Each pure pattern has a definite energy, and ranked by energy they form a ladder. The analogy ends there: nothing material is vibrating (the clock hands of a fitting pattern do all turn together, at a rate set by its energy), the pattern is three-dimensional, the whole electron is always found at one spot, and hydrogen's rungs are not evenly spaced but crowd together towards the top, where the electron comes free.

Colour follows. When the electron goes from one rung to a lower one, the atom gives off the gap's energy as light (bar a minute recoil), almost always as one photon: the smallest lump of light of a given colour there can be; light of that colour comes in whole photons, one, two, three, never one and a half. A photon's energy is set by the light's colour, bluer meaning more (Planck's relation: energy proportional to frequency), so the gap sets the colour: bigger gap, bluer light. Rung 3 to rung 2 is the red line, 4 to 2 the blue-green, 5 and 6 to 2 the violets, 7, 8 and 9 to 2 the faint lines at the violet edge; drops to rung 1 are ultraviolet. Upper rungs are temporary; an atom typically drops within a millionth of a second. Franck–Hertz shows the same kind of ladder, mercury's, without light: a passing electron can change an atom's inner energy only by a whole rung-gap (the tiny recoil of a bounce aside, and short of knocking an electron out of the atom altogether), mercury has a rung 4.9 eV above its lowest, the one that then gives off the 254 nm light (with neighbours at 4.7 and 5.5 eV that blur the steps a little), and a 254 nm photon carries 4.9 eV.

Stability follows too. Squeeze a pattern into less room and it must bend more sharply, and sharper bends mean more of what the theory counts as energy of motion (half the wavelength, four times the energy). The nucleus's pull favours small patterns, the bending cost favours large ones, and the bottom rung is where they balance. No rung lies below it, so there is nothing to fall to: that is why atoms last, and why hydrogen atoms are alike, since the same patterns fit around the same nucleus.

*Why this model:* it produces the ladder instead of decreeing it, gives hydrogen's lines and their brightnesses, and goes on to helium, every other atom, molecules and solids. *Pros:* pictures (the clouds chemists call orbitals) you can reason with; the workhorse of atomic, chemical and materials physics. *Cons:* for many electrons the pattern lives in a space with three dimensions per electron, so beyond a few electrons exact computation is impractical and approximations take over; and "one whole electron, found at one spot, with these chances" is an added rule, not something the pattern's own equation produces.

**Model 2: Heisenberg's tables.** Heisenberg (1925) started from what is seen, the frequencies and brightnesses of the lines, and arranged them in a table with a row and a column for every rung, each entry standing for one possible drop: the entry for "rung 3 to rung 2" holds the red line's frequency (the difference between the two rungs' clock-hand rates) and a size that sets the line's brightness. With Born and Jordan he then found how to multiply such tables and a rule they must obey; early in 1926 Pauli used it to work out hydrogen's rungs: the same ladder. Schrödinger showed in 1926 that the two are one theory in different clothing. *Why this model:* when a system has only a few states, such as an atom's magnetism or a qubit (the two-state unit of a quantum computer), the tables are small and the wave picture is unnecessary. *Pros:* exact, compact, no picture to misread. *Cons:* no picture to lean on; clumsy for positions and motion. A third equivalent formulation, Feynman's sum over paths, adds up contributions from every route the electron could take; extended to fields it is a central tool of particle physics, and it is famously awkward for atoms.

**What the cloud means.** Here the mainstream interpretations part ways, and the lines decide nothing: all four below give the same hydrogen spectrum (the last adds effects far too small to show there).

- *Copenhagen-style:* the pattern is a catalogue of chances for what you would find; where the electron is before you look is not a question the theory answers. Supporters say this adds nothing and is exactly how the theory is used; critics object that "look" is never defined, so the theory never says when chances become one outcome.
- *Many-worlds:* the pattern is the electron's whole real state, and looking makes you part of it, one branch for each place it is found; every outcome happens, each in its own branch. Supporters say nothing is added to the equation; critics ask where the chances then come from, if everything occurs.
- *Pilot-wave:* the electron has an exact position at every moment and is steered by the pattern; in hydrogen's lowest state it sits still, what the standard theory counts as energy of motion being here the energy of the wave's push, and across many atoms the positions are spread like the cloud. Supporters say there is then no puzzle about outcomes, you find the electron where it is; critics call the positions unobservable extras and dislike that the steering acts instantly across any distance (though it cannot carry a message).
- *Objective collapse:* the pattern is real and now and then localizes itself at random, so rarely for one electron that a single atom is practically unaffected, but a large object, with its vast number of particles, gets pinned down almost at once. Supporters say it is the one view in which the pattern itself settles on a single outcome by a stated mechanism, and that it can be tested; critics say it changes the theory, adds new constants nobody has measured, and the faint extra glow and warming of matter it predicts have not turned up, which has already ruled out some versions.

**How they relate.** Patterns, tables and paths are one theory, chosen by convenience: patterns for atoms and molecules, tables for few-state systems, paths for particle physics. The interpretations change no line and are chosen by taste and economy; only objective collapse predicts anything different, mainly for objects far larger than atoms, and experiments to catch it are under way.

So quantum mechanics is about this: matter that is held together, an electron in an atom or atoms in a molecule, has only certain allowed energies, a ladder (a free electron can have any energy, and in a crystal the rungs of its countless atoms crowd into bands so dense they act as continuous, often with gaps between); what you find when you look is given only as chances, read off the cloud; and beneath both is a pattern of amplitude that fits, like a wave, in the room it has. Add one more quantum rule, that no two electrons can be in the same state, and the periodic table, chemical bonds, metals and insulators follow; the levels of atoms and the bands of crystals are what lasers, LEDs and transistors are built on. The full quantum theory now predicts hydrogen's lines to better than a part in a billion; what the cloud is remains debated.
