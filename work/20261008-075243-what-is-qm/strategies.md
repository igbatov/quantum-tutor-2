# Strategies: "Describe what quantum mechanics is about"

**Situation.** First question of the session, mode `new-question`, no previous run named, empty history, no recorded choices. Level: conceptual (no override). Two standing preferences from the learner: (1) every explanation experiment-first, then the mainstream models that explain the experiments, with intuition, "why this model rather than another" and pros and cons for each; (2) nothing false even at beginner level: words and pictures instead of formulas are fine, but every sentence must be literally true.

The question is as broad as it gets, so every candidate must be a complete answer to "what is quantum mechanics about", not an answer about one effect. Each candidate needs:

1. A one-sentence answer with scope up front (matter and light at the scale of atoms and below; the theory under chemistry, materials, lasers, transistors).
2. One or more real experiments: set-up, what is done, what is seen, with "what is seen" kept apart from any interpretation, conditions and idealizations named.
3. The classical candidates (particles, classical waves, pre-set labels, planetary atom) and exactly which observation rules each one out.
4. Two or three mainstream quantum models, each with intuition, why this model, pros and cons. Where the candidate's experiments raise the question of meaning, the main interpretations (Copenhagen-style, many-worlds, pilot-wave, objective collapse), even-handed, contested judgments labelled ("critics object…", "supporters reply…").
5. How the models relate (equivalent formulations; rival interpretations with identical predictions; what decides between them).
6. One zoom-out in the candidate's own vocabulary: what quantum mechanics is about and what it underlies.
7. Exactly one `**Check yourself:**` line.

Conceptual level: no notation, at most one simple relation stated in words, pictures and concrete grounding. The structure needs room: aim for 900–1,000 words, hard ceiling about 1,100. No headings; short bold lead-ins at most ("**What is seen.**"). Since nothing is known about this learner yet, the three candidates should differ in experiment, in model set and in representation. Which one they pick will tell us whether they think best in waves and chances (A), in questions and answers (B), or in matter and energy (C).

## 1. Candidate plans

**1. Double slit, one electron at a time, then a which-slit record (fit 5, chosen as A).** Electrons fired one at a time at two narrow slits: whole single dots at unpredictable spots, stripes after thousands, one slit closed gives a wide band without fine stripes, and a record of which slit each electron used removes the stripes. Leads to the two formulations that are most natural for "where does the electron land": wave function with the Schrödinger equation, and the sum over paths; the which-slit record raises the measurement question, so the four interpretations follow. The most famous quantum experiment, needs no background, shows lumpiness, chance, interference and measurement in one setting; fits a first-contact conceptual learner best.

**2. Stern–Gerlach sequences with silver atoms (fit 4, chosen as B).** A beam of silver atoms through a non-uniform magnet lands in two separate groups instead of a continuous band; magnets in sequence show that repeating a question gives the same answer, a different question gives 50/50, and asking it erases the previous answer. Leads to the state-vector-and-measurement formulation (the natural one for "what answers can a question have"), the wave-function view of the same magnet, and the interpretations of what happens at the plate. Concrete (atoms, magnets, spots on glass) and the cleanest demonstration of quantized answers, measurement and incompatible questions. Fit 4 rather than 5 because the three-magnet sequence asks the learner to hold three steps in mind and "each atom is a tiny magnet" has to be taken on trust.

**3. Hydrogen's sharp colours and the stability of atoms, with Franck–Hertz (fit 5, chosen as C).** Glowing hydrogen gives four sharp visible lines instead of a rainbow, the same pattern from the lab to distant galaxies; atoms left alone never collapse; electrons shot through mercury vapour lose energy only in 4.9 eV lumps. Classical physics predicts collapse in a hundred-billionth of a second, a smear of colours and atoms of any size, so the planetary atom is ruled out; Bohr's patched orbits explain hydrogen but fail beyond it. Leads to Schrödinger's standing patterns and energy ladder, Heisenberg's equivalent table formulation, and a short look at what the cloud means. Answers "what is it about" in the most literal sense (what matter is) with everyday hooks; fits a conceptual learner who wants to know why the theory matters.

**4. Single photons through polarizing filters (fit 3, rejected).** Two crossed filters block everything, a third filter between them lets a quarter through; with single photons each one passes whole or not at all. Leads to the same state-vector model as plan 2. Rejected: with bright light every count is ordinary wave optics, so the classical wave candidate is only ruled out by single-photon detection, which is hard to make vivid; polarization itself needs explaining first; and plan 2 delivers the same model with atoms and visible spots, plus quantization, which filters don't show. Its three-filter beat can serve as a one-line aside in B if room allows (it won't).

**5. Single photons at a beam splitter and a Mach–Zehnder interferometer (fit 3, rejected).** One photon never triggers both detectors behind a beam splitter, yet with two paths recombined all photons go to one detector and blocking either path sends half to each. Leads to the sum over paths most directly. Rejected: it duplicates plan 1's logic with a less familiar apparatus, and the beam splitter's 50/50 and the recombination need more set-up than slits. A keeps the slits and still presents the sum over paths.

**6. Large-molecule interference and decoherence (fit 3, rejected).** Molecules of hundreds of atoms show stripes; heating them or adding gas washes the stripes out. Leads to the wave function plus decoherence, and the interpretations differ on whether decoherence settles anything. Rejected for a first contact: it answers "why don't I see quantum effects" better than "what is quantum mechanics about", and it needs plan 1 first. The best follow-up to A.

**7. Bell tests with entangled photon pairs (fit 3, rejected).** Measuring two photons at chosen angles gives correlations no pre-set labels can produce. Leads to joint states and to the interpretations' different handling of nonlocality. Rejected: a first answer can't carry correlation statistics across angle settings, and it invites the signalling myth. The strongest second question.

**8. Tunnelling: the scanning tunnelling microscope and alpha decay (fit 3, rejected).** A current flows across a gap no classical electron could cross, falling off steeply with distance; alpha-decay lifetimes spread over twenty powers of ten. Leads to the leaking wave function. Rejected: narrow (nothing about measurement or quantized states), and the classical candidate is ruled out only through indirect evidence. One sentence in C at most.

**9. Photoelectric effect (fit 2, rejected).** Light ejects electrons whose energy depends on colour, not brightness. Historically central, but the classical-wave prediction is subtle to state correctly and the effect can be reproduced with quantized atoms and classical light, so it doesn't cleanly force photons. Rejected.

**10. Blackbody glow (fit 2, rejected).** The classical prediction (ever more light at short wavelengths) needs statistical mechanics to state honestly; impossible to make concrete and literally true in a paragraph at this level. Rejected.

**11. Quantum eraser and delayed choice (fit 2, rejected).** Fascinating but needs entanglement and coincidence counting, and it invites the "changing the past" myth. Rejected.

## 3. Strategies

### Strategy A: Dots that make stripes

**First line of the candidate:** `<!-- strategy: A: dots that make stripes (double slit, one electron at a time) -->`

**Experiments.**
1. *Two slits, one electron at a time.* Electron source, a wall with two narrow slits, a screen that registers each arrival. The electrons are sent so slowly that each has landed before the next leaves. Seen: (i) each electron makes one dot, whole, at a spot nobody can predict; (ii) after thousands, bright and dark stripes; the stripe spacing depends on the electrons' speed (faster electrons, narrower stripes); (iii) with one slit covered, a wide bright band in the middle with much fainter bands further out and no fine stripes, and some spots that stay dark with both slits open now receive electrons. Done with electrons arriving one at a time (Bologna 1976 and Hitachi 1989 with an electron "biprism", the equivalent of two slits; with real slits in 2013). Idealizations: one electron speed; screen far away compared with the slit spacing.
2. *A which-slit record.* Anything that records which slit each electron used. Seen: the stripes are gone and the pattern is the plain sum of the two one-slit patterns; the more reliably the record tells the slits apart, the fainter the stripes. Done cleanly with atoms (Rempe group, 1998) and photons; for electrons the equivalent has been done in tiny electronic circuits.

**Classical candidates and what kills them.** Particles moving in straight lines, or bouncing off slit edges: two bands, possibly spread and merged, but opening a second slit could never mean fewer hits anywhere; killed by (iii). Classical waves (water, sound): stripes yes, but the energy arrives continuously, spread over the whole screen, and the dimmer the beam the fainter the splash everywhere; killed by (i). Electrons crowding or bumping into each other: killed by one at a time. A particle steered by a wave is not killed; it is the pilot-wave model below.

**Models.** Wave function with the Schrödinger equation; sum over paths; then, because the which-slit record raises "what happens between the slits and the dot", the four interpretations.

**Plan.** One-sentence answer, then straight into experiment 1 with the three observations in order and a-dots-buildup after (i)–(ii), then the one-slit comparison with a-one-vs-both, then experiment 2. Dismantle the classical candidates one observation each. Then the wave function: a ripple of amplitude (a number that can be positive or negative, a small clock hand in the full theory) spreads from the source through both slits and the two ripples overlap on the screen; the chance of a dot at a spot is the amplitude's size squared; the Schrödinger equation is the rule for how the ripple changes in time; give a two-line number example (equal and opposite amplitudes give zero; equal and alike give double, squared four, where plain chances would give two). Then the sum over paths: for each spot, every route from source to spot gets a clock hand whose angle is set by the route's length, the hands are added tip to tail, and the chance is the squared length of the total; group all routes through the left slit into one combined hand and all through the right into another, bright where they point alike, dark where opposite. Then the interpretations in four short paragraphs, each with its picture of what the electron does between slits and dot and at the which-slit record, one pro and one con each, judgments labelled. Relation: the two formulations are the same theory in different clothing, always the same predictions, chosen by convenience; the four interpretations give identical predictions for everything here (objective collapse only differs for very large objects, where experiments are now probing), chosen by taste and economy. Zoom out: lumps, chances, amplitudes that add and cancel, records that decide what can interfere; the same amplitude rule builds atoms, chemistry, transistors and lasers; big objects never show stripes because their surroundings record them constantly.

**Model notes for the drafter.**
- *Wave function.* Why: the only known consistent way to get dark stripes from independent whole arrivals is to combine something wave-like for the two slits first and square afterwards; the same picture predicts the stripe spacing from the electron's speed and, in the hydrogen atom, the colours it emits. Pros: the workhorse for atoms, molecules and solids; vivid pictures; the equation is deterministic between measurements. Cons: for several electrons the ripple lives in a space with three dimensions per particle, not in ordinary space, so it is hard to picture and the cost of computing it grows exponentially with the number of particles; the rule "one dot, with these chances" is an extra rule, not something the ripple equation produces by itself (the measurement problem). Limits of the ripple analogy, in the same breath: not a wave of any stuff; part of an electron is never detected.
- *Sum over paths.* Why: mathematically equivalent to the Schrödinger equation (Feynman, 1948), so no new predictions; chosen when it is easier to think with: the double slit becomes two combined hands, and classical physics drops out for heavy objects (hands for routes near the straight path line up, the rest cancel, so only the familiar path matters). Pros: makes interference and the classical limit intuitive; the main tool of particle physics. Cons: clumsy for bound states such as atoms; infinitely many routes; it is a bookkeeping method, and "the electron takes every path" is a slogan, not something the method says.
- *Copenhagen-style.* The wave function is a tool for predicting; the dot is the result; between source and screen "which slit?" has no answer the theory gives, and a record changes what can be predicted. Pro: minimal, matches how physicists work. Con: critics object that it never says what counts as a record or a measurement.
- *Many-worlds.* The wave function is everything and never collapses; at the screen, electron and screen become one joint state with a branch for each dot position, and we, in one branch, see one dot; a which-slit record ties each route to a different detector state, so the two routes can no longer meet. Pro: no special measurement rule; explains the which-slit result naturally. Con: critics object that if every outcome happens it is unclear where the chances come from, and to the enormous number of branches; supporters reply it is just the ripple equation taken seriously.
- *Pilot-wave.* The electron is a particle with a definite position at every moment, guided by a wave that passes through both slits; each electron goes through one slit on a definite bent path, steered away from the dark lines; chance comes only from not knowing the exact starting point; a which-slit record disturbs the guiding wave. Pro: a clear picture with nothing left vague about the dot. Con: for several particles the guidance acts instantly across distance, and its extension to light and high-energy physics is less settled; critics call the paths unobservable extra structure.
- *Objective collapse.* The ripple is real and localizes itself at random, extremely rarely for one electron (so the stripes appear as usual) but almost at once for the trillions of particles in the screen, which is where the single dot comes from. Pro: a definite mechanism that makes testable predictions. Con: new constants; slightly different predictions from standard quantum mechanics for large objects, and experiments with large molecules and tiny vibrating devices have so far found no sign of them.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the physics of matter and light at the scale of atoms and below, and the quickest way to see what it is about is one experiment: fire electrons at a wall with two narrow slits, one electron at a time, and record where each one lands."

**Guardrails.**
- Never state as fact that the electron "is a wave", "is in two places at once" or "goes through both slits". Say both slits' amplitudes contribute; what the electron does in between is exactly where the interpretations differ.
- One slit open: "a wide bright band in the middle with much fainter bands further out", never "a single smooth hump" or "the stripes vanish". Match a-one-vs-both.
- "Record" means any physical trace that can tell the slits apart (a detector, a scattered photon of short enough wavelength); no minds. A trace that only partly distinguishes the slits only partly fades the stripes.
- Don't claim nature has been proven fundamentally random; say the theory gives chances and nobody can predict the spot. No Bell's theorem.
- Decoherence explains why big objects show no stripes; it does not by itself settle the measurement problem.
- The amplitude is "a number that can be positive or negative; in the full theory a small clock hand (a complex number)". Clock hands are the sum-over-paths picture; don't call the electron's state "an arrow", which belongs to B.
- Name no experiments beyond the safe ones listed above; if unsure of a date, leave it out.

**Check question.** "**Check yourself:** At a spot on the screen that stays dark when both slits are open, what happens if you cover one slit: do electrons start landing there, or does it stay dark?" (Answer: they start landing there.)

### Strategy B: Two spots, three magnets

**First line of the candidate:** `<!-- strategy: B: two spots, three magnets (Stern–Gerlach sequences) -->`

**Experiments.**
1. *One magnet (Stern and Gerlach, 1922).* An oven boils silver; a narrow beam of silver atoms flies through a vacuum, between the poles of a magnet shaped so the field is much stronger near one pole than the other, onto a glass plate. Each silver atom is a tiny magnet (because of one of its electrons; the electron's magnetism is built in, not the result of anything spinning), and a non-uniform field pushes a tiny magnet up or down by an amount that depends on how it is oriented. Seen: the atoms land in two separate bands, one shifted up and one down, with nothing in between in the middle of the pattern; none land undeflected. Idealization: for a very narrow beam, two spots with an empty gap (real plates show two bands that join at the ends because the field varies across the beam).
2. *Two magnets, same axis.* Block the "down" band, send the "up" atoms through a second identical magnet. Seen: all come out "up" (ideal set-up; real counts very close).
3. *Two magnets, different axes.* Send the "up" atoms through a magnet turned 90°, so it sorts left/right. Seen: half left, half right, with no way to predict which an individual atom will do.
4. *Three magnets.* Take the "left" atoms and send them through a third magnet set like the first (up/down). Seen: half up, half down. The earlier "up" answer is gone.
Conditions: counts are averages over many atoms; sequences of this kind have been realized in several forms with atoms and with neutrons; the clean three-magnet version is the idealized textbook form of them.

**Classical candidates and what kills them.** Atoms that are tiny bar magnets pointing every which way: the push would take every value between the extremes, giving one continuous band; killed by the two separate bands in experiment 1. Atoms that aren't magnets at all: no deflection; killed by any deflection. Atoms carrying fixed labels (an up/down label and a left/right label) that each magnet merely reads off, like a sieve: consistent with experiments 1 to 3, killed by experiment 4, where the third magnet should have returned all atoms "up" and instead returned half. Not killed: models in which a magnet changes the atom as it sorts it; all the quantum models are of that kind.

**Models.** State vectors and measurement (primary); the wave-function view of the same magnet (secondary, shows what the "question" is physically); the four interpretations of what happens at the plate.

**Plan.** One-sentence answer, then experiment 1 with b-smear-vs-two, then experiments 2–4 as one story with b-three-magnets, each step's observation stated before any explanation. Dismantle the three classical candidates one observation each. Then the state-vector model in the globe picture: the atom's magnetic state is a point on a globe; a magnet set along some axis asks one question, "towards this axis's north pole or its south pole?", and there are only two answers ever, which is why there are two spots; the chance of each answer depends only on the angle between the atom's point and the magnet's axis: at the pole it is certain, 90° away it is 50/50, at the opposite pole it is zero; after the answer, the atom's point sits at the pole of the answer it gave (for an ideal magnet), which is why a repeat gives the same answer and why a left/right question wipes out the old up/down answer: the left/right poles are 90° from the up/down poles, so no point on the globe is at both. Then the wave-function view: the atom also has a wave of amplitude for where it is; in the magnet, the part tied to "north" is pushed up and the part tied to "south" is pushed down, so the wave splits into two separating packets, and the plate finds the whole atom in one of them with chances given by the squared sizes of the two parts; the magnet is not a black box, it ties the atom's magnetism to a position the plate can see. Then the interpretations in four short paragraphs about the moment of the dot, one pro and one con each, labelled. Relation: the two formulations are one theory, the first leaving the atom's position out, the second putting it in; the interpretations agree on every count in the figures. Zoom out: states as directions, questions with only a few answers, chances set by angles, answers that update the state and erase others; the same two-answer magnetism of the hydrogen nuclei in your body is what MRI scanners work with, and a qubit is exactly one of these globes.

**Model notes for the drafter.**
- *State vectors and measurement.* Why: the smallest structure that gives all four observations (exactly two answers, repeat gives the same, 90° gives 50/50, the old answer erased) and the chance rule has been tested at many angles; it works unchanged for any two-answer system (photon polarization, a qubit) and, with directions in a bigger abstract space, for every quantum system. Pros: compact, exact, general; the language of quantum computing. Cons: it treats "asking" as a primitive and says nothing about what the magnet and plate do physically; the globe works only for two-answer questions; it leaves the atom's position out.
- *Wave-function view.* Why: it shows how a measurement happens as ordinary motion up to the final dot: the magnet entangles magnetism with position, then the plate records position. Optional, if room: it predicts that if the two packets are steered back together before anything records them, the original state returns, which a sieve could never do; this has been done with atoms in recent years (say "in recent years", no date). Pros: unifies with everything else in quantum mechanics; makes "measurement" a physical process. Cons: more to hold in mind; the final "one dot, with these chances" is still an added rule, so both formulations share the same open question.
- *Copenhagen-style.* The state is a tool for predicting; the plate's dot is the result; before it, "up or down?" has no value the theory gives. Pro: minimal. Con: critics object that it never says what makes the plate a recorder and the magnet not.
- *Many-worlds.* Both packets persist; the plate's atoms join each; two branches, and "you" see one; the chances are the squared sizes. Pro: no added rule. Con: critics object that it is unclear why squared sizes are chances if both outcomes happen; supporters reply the wave equation is all there is.
- *Pilot-wave.* The atom always has an exact position and rides in one packet; which one depends on its exact starting position within the beam, so the outcome is determined but unpredictable in practice; the atom doesn't carry an up/down label, the guiding wave does the sorting. Pro: a clear picture of the dot. Con: the guidance acts instantly across distance for several atoms; the extension to light and high-energy physics is less settled.
- *Objective collapse.* The two packets persist until the plate, where the vast number of particles triggers a real, random localization within a tiny fraction of a second. Pro: a mechanism; testable. Con: new constants; no sign of collapse in experiments so far.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the physics of atoms, electrons and light, and its most basic lesson can be seen with a beam of silver atoms, a magnet and a glass plate: nature answers some questions with only a few possible answers, by chance, and asking one question can erase the answer to another."

**Guardrails.**
- Say "each silver atom is a tiny magnet" and attribute it to one of its electrons; never say the electron spins. Don't use the word "spin" as a thing that spins; "spin" may appear once as the name physicists use.
- The field must be described as non-uniform (stronger near one pole), otherwise there is no push.
- Experiment 1 is 1922 with silver; the sequences: say "realized in several forms with atoms and neutrons", don't attribute the three-magnet sequence to a named 1920s experiment.
- All counts are averages; 100%, 50% and 0% are for an ideal set-up.
- The chance rule in words: "depends only on the angle; certain at the pole, 50/50 at 90°, zero at the opposite pole". Don't write cos²(θ/2).
- The globe is exact for two-answer systems only; say so.
- Never use "interference", "stripes" or "cancellation"; the optional recombination sentence says "the original state returns".
- "Erased" means the state no longer carries that answer; don't say the magnet "disturbed" the atom clumsily (that is the ruled-out sieve-with-disturbance story told as if it were the quantum model).
- Don't claim the experiment rules out all hidden-label models; it rules out labels that are read off without change.

**Check question.** "**Check yourself:** Atoms come out of the X magnet's "left" exit and go into a third magnet that is also set along X. What fraction come out "left"?" (Answer: all of them, in an ideal set-up.)

### Strategy C: Sharp colours and the atom that shouldn't exist

**First line of the candidate:** `<!-- strategy: C: sharp colours and the atom that shouldn't exist (hydrogen spectrum, stability, Franck–Hertz) -->`

**Experiments.**
1. *Hydrogen's light.* A glass tube of hydrogen gas with a high voltage across it glows; send its light through a prism or grating and compare with the light of a glowing lamp filament. Seen: the filament gives a continuous rainbow; hydrogen gives four sharp visible lines, red at 656 nm, blue-green at 486 nm, blue-violet at 434 nm, violet at 410 nm, plus more lines in the ultraviolet and infrared, and darkness in between. The same pattern of lines comes from hydrogen in the Sun and in distant galaxies, shifted as a whole by the source's motion, which is how astronomers recognize hydrogen there. (Ordinary hydrogen; the heavy isotope's lines sit a tiny distance away.)
2. *Atoms don't collapse.* Hydrogen gas left alone keeps giving the same lines indefinitely; the atoms in a bottle today are as large and as alike as ever. Every ordinary hydrogen atom has the same size and the same lines.
3. *Franck–Hertz (1914).* Electrons are accelerated through mercury vapour and the current reaching a collector is measured as the voltage rises. Seen: the current drops sharply each time the voltage has risen by another 4.9 volts, and once the electrons have 4.9 eV the vapour gives off ultraviolet light of one wavelength, 254 nm. The electrons can lose energy to the atoms only in lumps of 4.9 eV. (Real curves are shifted by a volt or so as a whole and the drops are not to zero; the spacing is the point.)
4. Optional, one sentence: alpha particles fired at gold foil occasionally bounce straight back, so the atom's positive charge and nearly all its mass sit in a tiny nucleus.

**Classical candidates and what kills them.** The planetary atom (Newton's mechanics plus Maxwell's electromagnetism, which work flawlessly for planets, motors and radio): a circling charge radiates, so the electron should spiral into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second, giving off a smear of all colours on the way, and orbits could have any size so no two atoms would be alike; killed by 2 (no collapse), 1 (sharp lines, not a smear) and 2 again (identical atoms). Bohr's patched planetary atom (orbits allowed only at certain sizes, by decree): it reproduces hydrogen's lines, but it gives no reason for its rule, fails for helium and every atom beyond hydrogen, says nothing about how bright each line is, and its orbits contradict the full theory, in which the lowest state has no orbiting at all; killed by helium's spectrum and superseded. Mention that it is a stepping stone, not a mainstream model today.

**Models.** Schrödinger's standing patterns (primary); Heisenberg's tables (matrix mechanics), equivalent; sum over paths in one sentence; one short paragraph on what the cloud means (interpretations).

**Plan.** One-sentence answer, then experiment 1 with c-ladder-lines placed after the model is introduced (its right panel is the observation, its left panel the model), experiments 2 and 3 in words. Dismantle the planetary atom with its own predictions and the observation that kills each, then Bohr's patch and why it is not enough. Then Schrödinger's model: a bound electron is described by a pattern of amplitude around the nucleus, and the squared size of that pattern is the cloud of where the whole electron will be found if you look; like a guitar string, which can only vibrate in patterns that fit between its fixed ends, only certain patterns fit around the nucleus (c-string-fits), each with a definite energy, and together they form a ladder; bound the analogy at once (nothing is vibrating, the pattern is three-dimensional, the whole electron is always found at one spot, and hydrogen's rungs crowd together near the top instead of being evenly spaced). Colours: when the electron drops from one rung to a lower one, the atom emits one photon carrying exactly the energy gap, and a photon's energy fixes its colour (higher energy, bluer light, Planck's relation E = hf said in words); the 3-to-2 drop is the red line; Franck–Hertz is the same ladder seen without light: electrons can hand an atom only a whole rung-gap of energy, and mercury's first gap is 4.9 eV, exactly the energy of its 254 nm photon. Stability: there is a lowest rung because squeezing the pattern closer to the nucleus forces sharper bends, which cost energy of motion (right panel of c-string-fits), and the bottom rung is where that cost balances the nucleus's pull; with no rung below, there is nothing to fall to. Then Heisenberg's tables: start not from a wave but from what is seen, the frequencies and brightnesses of the lines, arranged in a table with a row and a column for each rung; the rules for combining such tables give the same ladder; shown equivalent to Schrödinger's picture in 1926. One sentence on the sum over paths as the third equivalent formulation, powerful for particle physics and clumsy for atoms. One short paragraph: what the cloud means is where interpretations differ (Copenhagen-style: a catalogue of chances for what you'd find; many-worlds: the electron's whole real state, and looking makes you part of the pattern; pilot-wave: the electron has an exact position every moment, distributed as the cloud across many atoms; objective collapse: nothing changes for single atoms), all with identical spectra. Relation: three equivalent formulations, chosen by convenience (Schrödinger's for atoms and molecules, Heisenberg's for anything with few states, paths for particle physics); interpretations change no line. Zoom out: matter comes in allowed states (ladders), chances (clouds) and wave-like patterns of amplitude; add one more quantum rule, that no two electrons can share a state, and the periodic table, chemical bonds, solids, conductors, transistors, LEDs and lasers follow; predictions tested to extraordinary precision, meaning still debated.

**Model notes for the drafter.**
- *Schrödinger's standing patterns.* Why: it produces the ladder instead of decreeing it; gives hydrogen's lines, their brightnesses, helium and every atom, molecules and solids; explains stability and why all atoms of a kind are alike (the same patterns fit around the same nucleus). Pros: pictures (clouds, orbitals) that chemists use daily; the workhorse of atomic and materials physics. Cons: for many electrons the pattern lives in a space with three dimensions per electron and exact computation becomes impossible beyond a handful; the rule for what you find when you look is an added rule.
- *Heisenberg's tables.* Why: historically first (1925), built directly from observed line frequencies and brightnesses; equivalent to Schrödinger's (shown 1926), so the same predictions; chosen when the system has only a few states (spins, qubits), where the tables are small and the wave picture is unnecessary. Pros: makes "allowed energies and transitions" the primary objects; no picture to misread. Cons: no picture to help intuition; cumbersome for positions and motion.
- *Sum over paths.* Equivalent; clumsy for bound states like hydrogen (a famous hard calculation); its strength is elsewhere.
- *Interpretations.* One sentence each, pros and cons compressed to a clause; if the pilot-wave "electron sits still in the lowest state" detail is used, say that what the standard theory calls energy of motion is, in this model, energy stored in the guiding wave's push, and label critics' and supporters' views.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the physics that explains what ordinary matter is and why it holds together, and the plainest evidence for it is light: make hydrogen gas glow, spread its light into a spectrum, and instead of a rainbow you get four sharp lines."

**Guardrails.**
- Classical collapse: "about a hundred-billionth of a second" (the estimate is about 1.6 × 10⁻¹¹ s).
- Excited rungs are not permanent: atoms drop down by emitting light, typically within billionths of a second; stability is about the bottom rung only.
- The lowest state has no orbiting (zero angular momentum about the nucleus), and the theory gives it a nonzero energy of motion; but don't assert "the electron is moving" as interpretation-free fact; say the state has no definite position and no definite speed. The cloud is where the whole electron is likely to be found if you look; avoid "smeared-out electron" and "the cloud is just our ignorance".
- "Every hydrogen atom is identical": say "every ordinary hydrogen atom"; the heavy isotope's lines are slightly shifted.
- Don't use absorption by cool hydrogen as an example (room-temperature hydrogen is H₂ molecules, and ground-state atoms absorb only ultraviolet lines).
- Starlight: astronomers recognize hydrogen by the pattern of lines, shifted as a whole; don't say the wavelengths are identical.
- Franck–Hertz: spacing 4.9 V; light at 254 nm; say "mercury vapour", keep the real-curve caveat.
- Bohr's model: present as a historical stepping stone that is not a mainstream model today; don't draw orbits as the real atom.
- The periodic table, bonds and solids need the exclusion principle; call it "one more quantum rule".
- Don't say how long a "quantum jump" takes or that it is instantaneous. Don't claim the atoms in your body have been intact since the Big Bang.
- Only relation: Planck's E = hf, said in words ("a photon's energy is proportional to its frequency, so the colour tells you the energy"); no other notation.
- Don't use slits or magnets.
