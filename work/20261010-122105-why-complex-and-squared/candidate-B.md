A hydrogen atom in a state of definite energy $E$ is stationary (nothing observable about it changes in time), yet the light it emits when it drops to energy $E'$ has frequency exactly $(E - E')/h$, which only works if the state carries the frequency $E/h$ in a form that leaves every observable constant. That is the whole answer to your first question in one line: an amplitude must be able to *turn* without changing its *length*. One real number per state cannot do that; a pair of real numbers, a clock hand with a length and a direction, can, and "complex number" is the name for a hand whose turning rule is multiplication. The answer to your second question is a conservation law: the equation that moves the hands keeps exactly one simple total fixed, the sum of the squared lengths, so that is the only candidate for "total chance $= 1$ at every moment". Below: the experiments; the classical pictures they kill; the models, with every derivation step written out; the limits of each argument; and what is still argued over.

**The experiments.** *(1) Spectral lines are differences.* Every frequency hydrogen emits is the difference of two numbers from one fixed list, the *terms* $R/n^2$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and $R = 3.29 \times 10^{15}$ Hz (Ritz's combination principle, 1908). Balmer-$\alpha$ is $R(1/4 - 1/9) = 4.57 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, the red line at 656 nm; Lyman-$\alpha$ is $R(1 - 1/4) = 2.47 \times 10^{15}$ Hz, at 122 nm. No line sits at a *sum* of terms: $R(1/4 + 1/9) = 1.19 \times 10^{15}$ Hz is not a hydrogen line. Franck and Hertz (1914) sent electrons through mercury vapour and found the current dropping every 4.9 V: an atom takes energy only in lumps, and the vapour then glows in the ultraviolet at 254 nm, whose $hf$ is 4.9 eV. Millikan (1916) confirmed $E = hf$ for the photoelectric effect. Together: the terms are energies divided by $h$, and an atom in a term is *stationary*: left alone in its lowest term it stays there, its charge distribution and its magnetic moment fixed. *(2) A spin in a steady magnetic field.* A proton's magnetic moment can point along the field or against it, with energies $E_+$ and $E_-$ (for 1 T they differ by $h \times 42.6$ MHz). In nuclear magnetic resonance (Bloch, 1946; Purcell, Torrey & Pound, 1946) a pulse tips the magnetization sideways; afterwards the sideways component precesses at $42.6$ MHz per tesla, the *Larmor frequency*, which equals $(E_+ - E_-)/h$, while the component along the field stays constant (idealization: times short compared with the relaxation times over which the sample settles back). *(3) Beats and Rabi flopping.* A short laser pulse puts a caesium atom into two nearby levels at once; the fluorescence that follows is modulated at exactly their difference frequency, and nothing is seen at the sum or at twice either level's frequency (Haroche, Paisner & Schawlow, 1973: quantum beats, used to measure level splittings). A two-level atom driven in a microwave cavity shows the chance of being in the upper level swinging smoothly between 0 and 1 and back (Brune et al., 1996: Rabi oscillation); the ammonia molecule's two lowest levels, $2A/h \approx 24$ GHz apart, give the microwave line of the first maser. *(4) Beams flow.* Electrons of momentum $p$ scattered from a nickel crystal show diffraction peaks at angles set by the wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ (Davisson & Germer, 1927; $1.67 \times 10^{-10}$ m at 54 eV; Thomson, 1927, with thin films), and the beam is a current: it carries charge into the detector. Idealizations throughout: isolated atoms, no damping, one wavelength, a long wave train as the limit of a long packet.

**What this rules out.** *Classical particles* carry no frequency and no wavelength: killed by (1) and (4). *A classical real wave* $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ gets wavelengths and difference-frequency beats, but its energy is spread out and divides at every splitter, while electrons and photons arrive whole (the two-slit dots; Grangier, Roger & Aspect, 1986); and it has no state that carries a frequency yet changes in nothing observable, since its square $\cos^2(\omega t)$ pulses at $2\omega$. *One real number per state*, combined and squared (your two-slit rule read literally): a stationary state of energy $E$ must either carry the frequency $E/h$ in its number, $c = \cos(Et/\hbar)$, and then the chance $c^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos(2Et/\hbar)$ pulses between 1 and 0 at twice the frequency: the chance of finding a proton along the field, which (2) says is constant, would dip to zero twice per period, and the total chance could not stay at 1; or carry no frequency at all, and then two levels superposed produce no beat and no precession, which (2) and (3) do show. **Model 1: the wave function and the Schrödinger equation.** *Intuition.* Every basis state (each term of the atom, each spin direction, each point in space) has a hand: a complex number $c = a + ib$, which you can read as the pair $(a, b)$, with length $|c| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and a direction. A state of definite energy $E$ has a hand turning clockwise at the angular rate $E/\hbar$ with fixed length; the chance of an outcome is the squared length of the hand for that outcome. *Why a turning hand is stationary.* Write $\theta = Et/\hbar$ and $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$. The squared length of $\psi = A e^{-i\theta}$ ($A$ real) is $\psi$ times its conjugate $\bar\psi = A e^{+i\theta}$:
$$|\psi|^2 = A(\cos\theta - i\sin\theta)\cdot A(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = A^2(\cos^2\theta - i^2\sin^2\theta) = A^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = A^2 .$$
Constant: the hand turns, the length does not. *Why two levels beat only at the difference.* Let level 1 have hand $c_1 = a\,e^{-i\omega_1 t}$ and level 2 have $c_2 = b\,e^{-i\omega_2 t}$, with $a, b$ real, $\omega_1 = E_1/\hbar$, $\omega_2 = E_2/\hbar$. Take any outcome whose amplitude gets an equal share from each level (for example, finding the atom in the combination state "(level 1 + level 2)$/\sqrt2$"; the amplitude to emit a photon in a given direction is of this type, with some fixed unequal shares, which changes only the depth of the beat). Its amplitude is $(c_1 + c_2)/\sqrt2$, and its chance is
$$\tfrac12|c_1 + c_2|^2 = \tfrac12(c_1 + c_2)(\bar c_1 + \bar c_2) = \tfrac12\big(|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 + c_1\bar c_2 + \bar c_1 c_2\big)
= \tfrac12\big(a^2 + b^2 + ab\,e^{-i(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t} + ab\,e^{+i(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}\big)$$
and since $e^{-i\phi} + e^{+i\phi} = 2\cos\phi$ this is $\tfrac12\big(a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos((\omega_2 - \omega_1)t)\big)$: one beat, at the difference frequency $(E_2 - E_1)/h$, exactly observation (3). Compare the real attempt, $c_1 = a\cos\omega_1 t$, $c_2 = b\cos\omega_2 t$:
$$(c_1 + c_2)^2 = a^2\cos^2\omega_1 t + b^2\cos^2\omega_2 t + 2ab\cos\omega_1 t\cos\omega_2 t
= \tfrac{a^2}{2}\big(1 + \cos 2\omega_1 t\big) + \tfrac{b^2}{2}\big(1 + \cos 2\omega_2 t\big) + ab\big[\cos(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t + \cos(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t\big],$$
using $\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)$ and $\cos x\cos y = \tfrac12[\cos(x - y) + \cos(x + y)]$. It contains $2\omega_1$, $2\omega_2$ and $\omega_1 + \omega_2$, frequencies that (1) and (3) do not show. *The spin, worked.* Energies $E_\pm = \pm\hbar\omega/2$, so $\omega = (E_+ - E_-)/\hbar$. A spin prepared along $+x$ has equal hands for up and down along the field: $c_+ = e^{-i\omega t/2}/\sqrt2$, $c_- = e^{+i\omega t/2}/\sqrt2$. Chance of "up": $|c_+|^2 = \tfrac12$, constant, as observed. The $+x$ state is itself the combination (up $+$ down)$/\sqrt2$, so the amplitude for "$+x$" is $(c_+ + c_-)/\sqrt2 = \tfrac12\big(e^{-i\omega t/2} + e^{+i\omega t/2}\big) = \cos(\omega t/2)$, and the chance is $\cos^2(\omega t/2) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos\omega t\big)$: it swings at the Larmor frequency $(E_+ - E_-)/h$; the chance of "$-x$", with amplitude $(c_+ - c_-)/\sqrt2 = -i\sin(\omega t/2)$, is $\sin^2(\omega t/2)$, and the two add to 1 at every $t$. That is precession with a constant longitudinal part, observation (2). (Feynman III §7-5 and Griffiths §4.4.2 do this calculation.)

*Flow: why a beam's wave function cannot be real.* In the position form of the model the hand at each point $x$ is $\psi(x, t)$ and the chance of finding the particle near $x$ is $|\psi|^2$ per unit length. The Schrödinger equation (one dimension, mass $m$, potential energy $V(x)$ real) is $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2 + V\psi$; it says "the rate at which the hand turns and changes is set by how sharply the wave bends and by the potential". Its conjugate (replace $i$ by $-i$ everywhere) is $-i\hbar\,\partial\bar\psi/\partial t = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,\partial^2\bar\psi/\partial x^2 + V\bar\psi$. Then, writing $\psi'$ for $\partial\psi/\partial x$,
$$\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = \bar\psi\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} + \psi\,\frac{\partial\bar\psi}{\partial t}
= \bar\psi\cdot\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi\Big] + \psi\cdot\frac{-1}{i\hbar}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bar\psi'' + V\bar\psi\Big].$$
The $V$ terms are $\frac{1}{i\hbar}(V\bar\psi\psi - V\psi\bar\psi) = 0$. What is left, with $1/i = -i$, is $\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\bar\psi\psi'' - \psi\bar\psi''\big)$, and because $(\bar\psi\psi' - \psi\bar\psi')' = \bar\psi'\psi' + \bar\psi\psi'' - \psi'\bar\psi' - \psi\bar\psi'' = \bar\psi\psi'' - \psi\bar\psi''$, this is
$$\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x},\qquad J = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\psi\bar\psi' - \bar\psi\psi'\big) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(\bar\psi\,\psi'\big),$$
using $\bar\psi\psi' - \psi\bar\psi' = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\psi')$. This is a continuity equation: chance is neither created nor destroyed, only carried from place to place by the *current* $J$. Now the point. If $\psi$ is real at some instant, $\bar\psi\psi' = \psi\psi'$ is real, its imaginary part is zero, and $J = 0$ everywhere: nothing flows. The mean momentum says the same: for a real $\psi$ that falls to zero far away, $\langle p\rangle = \int\bar\psi\,(-i\hbar\psi')\,dx = -i\hbar\int\psi\psi'\,dx = -i\hbar\big[\tfrac12\psi^2\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$. A real wave function describes something momentarily at rest on average. The electron beam of (4) is moving: its hand must be $\psi = A e^{ikx}$ with $k = 2\pi/\lambda = p/\hbar$, a hand that turns once per wavelength along the beam (idealization: a long packet, since a pure $e^{ikx}$ cannot be normalized). Check: $\bar\psi\psi' = \bar A e^{-ikx}\cdot ikAe^{ikx} = ik|A|^2$, so $J = (\hbar k/m)|A|^2 = v|A|^2$, density times velocity, the current a flowing beam needs. A standing wave $\cos kx$, two beams meeting head on, is real and has $J = 0$; a travelling one cannot be. (One more sign of the $i$: if $\psi$ were real at all times, the left side of the equation would be purely imaginary and the right side real, so both would vanish: a frozen wave with $H\psi = 0$, nothing else.)

*Why this model.* It is the one equation behind (1) to (4) and the rest of atomic physics: its definite-energy solutions are the terms, their hands turning at $E/\hbar$ give the differences-only spectrum and the beats, its magnetic term gives Larmor precession, and its plane waves give $\lambda = h/p$. *Pros.* Deterministic between measurements; the workhorse of chemistry and solid-state physics. *Cons.* The square is still a postulate here (Born, 1926, where the first paper's main text had the chance proportional to the amplitude and a footnote added in proof corrected it to the square); for several particles $\psi$ lives in a space with three coordinates per particle, not in ordinary space; and "complex" is a convention for "pairs of reals with the turning rule of multiplication": Stueckelberg (1960) showed that any such theory can be rewritten with twice as many real amplitudes plus one fixed operator that plays the role of $i$, and for a single system the two descriptions are the same theory. What the experiments force is that one real number per state is not enough and that the pairing rule is the one that turns hands at fixed length.

**Model 2: the sum over paths.** *Intuition.* Give every route from the start to the outcome a hand that turns by the route's action divided by $\hbar$ (for a free electron the position-dependent part is momentum times distance travelled, so the hand turns once per de Broglie wavelength along the route), add the hands tip to tail, square the length. *Why this model.* It is the same theory as model 1 in different mathematics (Feynman, 1948): the hand $e^{iS/\hbar}$ is the same $i$, and summing routes for a particle sitting in a definite-energy state reproduces the $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ turning. It adds no new prediction about the $i$ or the 2; it inherits both. *Pros.* The natural language for beams and scattering, and for seeing why heavy objects follow one path (hands near the classical path line up, the rest cancel). *Cons.* Clumsy for bound states, where model 1's stationary states are the right tool.

**Model 3 (established mathematics): why the exponent is 2.** The requirement: the chances of all the outcomes must add to 1 at every moment, whatever the state does. So ask what totals the equation keeps fixed. Take two levels with hands $c_1(t)$, $c_2(t)$. The general linear equation of motion is
$$i\hbar\,\dot c_1 = H_{11}c_1 + H_{12}c_2,\qquad i\hbar\,\dot c_2 = H_{21}c_1 + H_{22}c_2,$$
(the dot is $d/dt$), where the four numbers $H_{jk}$ form the energy matrix. Two conditions on them come from the experiments: $H_{11}$ and $H_{22}$ are real, because a single level's hand $c_1 = c_1(0)e^{-iH_{11}t/\hbar}$ must keep its length (an imaginary part $-i\Gamma$ would make $|c_1|^2$ decay as $e^{-2\Gamma t/\hbar}$, which is how one describes an *unstable* level, whose missing chance reappears in the decay products); and $H_{21} = \bar H_{12}$, which is the condition that the two energies of the coupled system come out real (a matrix with these two properties is called *Hermitian*). Now differentiate the squared length of $c_1$, using $\dot{\bar c}_1 = \overline{\dot c_1}$:
$$\frac{d|c_1|^2}{dt} = \bar c_1\dot c_1 + c_1\dot{\bar c}_1
= \bar c_1\frac{H_{11}c_1 + H_{12}c_2}{i\hbar} + c_1\frac{H_{11}\bar c_1 + \bar H_{12}\bar c_2}{-i\hbar}
= \frac{1}{i\hbar}\Big(H_{11}|c_1|^2 - H_{11}|c_1|^2 + H_{12}\bar c_1c_2 - \bar H_{12}c_1\bar c_2\Big)
= \frac{2}{\hbar}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big),$$
since $z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$ and $2i/i\hbar = 2/\hbar$. The same steps for $c_2$, with $H_{21} = \bar H_{12}$, give $\frac{d|c_2|^2}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\big(\bar H_{12}c_1\bar c_2 - H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big) = -\frac{2}{\hbar}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big)$. The two rates are equal and opposite:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\big(|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2\big) = 0 .$$
Whatever leaves level 1 arrives in level 2, measured in squared lengths. (For a wave function the same statement is $\frac{d}{dt}\int|\psi|^2dx = -[J]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$, from the continuity equation above with $J \to 0$ far away: Griffiths §1.4.) *Now the other powers.* Take the ammonia-type matrix $H_{11} = H_{22} = 0$, $H_{12} = H_{21} = -A$ with $A$ real and positive (Feynman III ch. 8–9), start in level 1, and try $c_1 = \cos(At/\hbar)$, $c_2 = i\sin(At/\hbar)$. Check by substitution: $i\hbar\dot c_1 = i\hbar\cdot\big(-\tfrac{A}{\hbar}\sin\tfrac{At}{\hbar}\big) = -iA\sin\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -A c_2$ ✓; $i\hbar\dot c_2 = i\hbar\cdot i\tfrac{A}{\hbar}\cos\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -A\cos\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -Ac_1$ ✓. (A *real* $c_2 = \sin(At/\hbar)$ fails the first check: the hand of level 2 is forced a quarter turn away from that of level 1. The chance $|c_2|^2 = \sin^2(At/\hbar)$ swinging between 0 and 1 is the Rabi flopping of observation (3).) Now compute the candidate totals $|c_1|^p + |c_2|^p = \cos^p\theta + \sin^p\theta$, $\theta = At/\hbar$, at $\theta = \pi/4$ where $\cos\theta = \sin\theta = 1/\sqrt2$: $2\cdot(1/\sqrt2)^p = 2^{1 - p/2}$.

| $\theta = At/\hbar$ | 0 | $\pi/8$ | $\pi/4$ | $3\pi/8$ | $\pi/2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $p = 1$: $\cos\theta + \sin\theta$ | 1 | 1.307 | 1.414 | 1.307 | 1 |
| $p = 2$: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| $p = 3$ | 1 | 0.845 | 0.707 | 0.845 | 1 |
| $p = 4$ | 1 | 0.750 | 0.500 | 0.750 | 1 |

All powers agree at $\theta = 0$, where the state is a single level; only $p = 2$ stays at 1 in between (any power below 2 overshoots, any power above 2 undershoots, worst at $\pi/4$). Since the molecule is always found in one level or the other, the chances must total 1 at every $t$, and among power rules only the square does. This is not a trick of one matrix: Banach (1932) and Lamperti (1958) proved that for $p \ne 2$ ($1 \le p < \infty$, two or more levels) the only linear maps that keep $\sum|c_k|^p$ fixed for *every* state are relabellings of the levels combined with turning each hand on the spot; none moves chance from one level to another gradually, as the ammonia equation, the Rabi oscillation and the Larmor precession all do. For $p = 2$ the length-keeping maps are exactly the unitary ones, and the Schrödinger equation generates them.

*Only among powers.* With just two levels a non-power rule can also total 1: $f(x) = x^2 + \varepsilon\,x^2(1 - x^2)(2x^2 - 1)$ applied to each hand's length. For a state $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, writing $C = \cos^2\theta$, $S = \sin^2\theta$ with $C + S = 1$:
$$f(\cos\theta) + f(\sin\theta) = C + S + \varepsilon\big[CS(2C - 1) + SC(2S - 1)\big] = 1 + \varepsilon\,CS\,\big[2C + 2S - 2\big] = 1 + 0 = 1 .$$
It also has $f(0) = 0$, $f(1) = 1$, and stays non-negative for $|\varepsilon| < 1$ (the factor $(1 - x^2)(2x^2 - 1)$ lies between $-1$ and $1/8$). So two levels alone cannot exclude it; measurements only push $\varepsilon$ below their error bars. Three levels do exclude it: for the state $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$, each hand has $x^2 = 1/3$ and $f = \tfrac13\big[1 + \varepsilon\cdot\tfrac23\cdot(-\tfrac13)\big] = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{2\varepsilon}{27}$, so the three chances total $1 - 2\varepsilon/9$, not 1, unless $\varepsilon = 0$. Gleason's theorem (1957) makes this general: for three or more mutually exclusive outcomes, if each outcome's chance depends only on the state and on that outcome's own direction (not on which other outcomes are measured alongside it) and the chances over every complete set of outcomes total 1, then the chance must be a squared shadow of the state, or a weighted average of such squares; adding that a state prepared in a level is found in it with certainty leaves the squared shadow itself. No power, no wiggle, no other rule survives. For two-level systems alone the theorem needs Busch's (2003) extension to imperfect measurements. *Why this account.* The exponent is the conserved quantity of the dynamics: among powers it is forced by the equation that the spectra, beats and precession establish, and among all rules by Gleason's consistency condition. *Pros.* It makes "why 2" a physical reason, conservation, not a fit. *Cons.* It assumes the dynamics is linear and the energy matrix Hermitian (motivated above, but assumptions); Gleason's non-contextuality is an assumption; and none of it says why there are chances at all.

**Where the interpretations stand** (all keep the $i$ and the square and agree on every count above; they differ on what the conserved density is a density *of*, and every position here is contested).

*Pilot-wave.* The particle always has a position and moves with velocity $v = J/|\psi|^2$ (current divided by density). Derivation of why the square is then stable: a cloud of particles with density $\rho(x, t)$ moving with velocity field $v(x, t)$ obeys $\partial\rho/\partial t = -\partial(\rho v)/\partial x$ (the number in a small interval changes only by what flows in across one end minus what flows out at the other). Put $\rho = |\psi|^2$: then $\rho v = J$ and the equation becomes $\partial|\psi|^2/\partial t = -\partial J/\partial x$, which is exactly the continuity equation derived above. So a cloud distributed as $|\psi|^2$ at one moment is distributed as $|\psi|^2$ for ever (*equivariance*). A cloud distributed as $|\psi| = \sqrt\rho$ is not: it would need $\partial\sqrt\rho/\partial t = -\partial(\sqrt\rho\,v)/\partial x$, but from the true equation $\partial\sqrt\rho/\partial t = \frac{1}{2\sqrt\rho}\,\partial\rho/\partial t = -\frac{\rho' v + \rho v'}{2\sqrt\rho}$, which differs from $-(\sqrt\rho\,v)' = -\frac{\rho' v}{2\sqrt\rho} - \sqrt\rho\,v'$ by $\tfrac12\sqrt\rho\,\partial v/\partial x$, zero only where the velocity field is uniform. So here the square is an equilibrium, like the settled distribution of a gas. Why the actual cloud is the $|\psi|^2$ one is argued from typicality (Dürr, Goldstein & Zanghì, 1992) or from relaxation (Valentini; simulations in Valentini & Westman, 2005), and critics debate whether "typical" explains or restates; the extension to photons is less settled.

*Copenhagen-style.* The hands are a tool for computing chances; the square is a postulate, with Gleason showing it is the only consistent one given the structure. Pro: minimal. Con: critics object that "measurement" is left undefined. *Many-worlds.* The hands are everything and never collapse; Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) derive the square from rules of rational betting plus the symmetries of branching, and Zurek (2003) from a symmetry of entangled states; critics (Barnum et al., 2000; Schlosshauer & Fine, 2005; Kent, 2010) object that the derivations assume what they prove or that "chance" is unclear when every outcome occurs; supporters reply that the assumptions are weaker than the rule. *Objective collapse.* The wave is real and localizes at random, and the square is written into the collapse law (Ghirardi, Rimini & Weber, 1986); Gisin (1989) showed that, given the standard description of entangled pairs, any other weighting would allow faster-than-light signalling, so there the square is forced by no-signalling. Con: new constants, and the predicted small departures from standard quantum mechanics have been searched for and not found so far (Bassi et al., 2013).

**A closing note on "complex" for pairs of systems.** For one system, pairs of reals with the right turning rule and complex numbers are the same theory. For two independently prepared systems the standard rule is that the joint hands are the products of the separate hands, and with *that* rule a theory using only real hands makes different predictions from complex quantum mechanics. Renou et al. (2021) designed a three-party, two-source Bell-type test: real-hand theories cannot score above 7.66, complex quantum mechanics predicts up to $6\sqrt2 \approx 8.49$; two experiments in 2022 (Li et al., with photons in an optical network; Chen et al., with superconducting qubits) exceeded 7.66 by many standard deviations. Caveats: the test assumes the two sources are independent, and it is not loophole-free. For your question, the experiments above already settle the single-system case: one real number per state cannot be stationary and carry a frequency at once.

*Sources:* Renou et al., *Nature* 600, 625 (2021); Li et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 128, 040402 (2022); Chen et al., *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 128, 040403 (2022).

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2 are one theory in two sets of mathematics, with the same $i$ (a hand that turns at $E/\hbar$ in time and once per wavelength in space) and the same square. Model 3 is mathematics about that theory: the square is the one total the equation conserves, so among power rules it is forced by the dynamics that spectra, precession, beats and flowing beams establish, and among all rules by Gleason's theorem with its assumptions named. The interpretations keep every number and differ on what the conserved density is: a tool for prediction, a branch weight, the equilibrium distribution of real particles, or the weighting in a collapse law; no experiment has told the first three apart, and objective collapse is a slightly different theory still being tested. So: the $i$ is "turn without changing length", forced by stationary states that carry a frequency; the 2 is "the total the turning keeps fixed", forced by the conservation that every other power breaks.
