Start from one fact about hydrogen. In the Schrödinger model of an isolated atom, a state of definite energy $E$ is *stationary*: nothing observable about it changes in time. Coupling to the light field gives an excited state a finite lifetime, and when it drops to a state of energy $E'$ the light it emits is centred at the frequency $(E - E')/h$, with a small natural width and a tiny recoil shift. Put the two together: the state must carry the frequency $E/h$ in some form, yet in a form that leaves every observable constant. That is the whole answer to your first question in one line: an amplitude must be able to *turn* without changing its *length*. One real number per state cannot do that. A pair of real numbers, a clock hand with a length and a direction, can, and "complex number" is the name for a hand whose turning rule is multiplication. The answer to your second question is a conservation law with a theorem behind it. The chances of all outcomes must total 1 at every moment; the equation that moves the hands shifts chance gradually from one outcome to another (between an atom's two levels in Rabi flopping, where a photon goes to the cavity and back; between the $+x$ and $-x$ outcomes in precession, while the chance of each energy level stays fixed); and among powers $p$ from 1 upwards, the only one for which such a gradual linear motion can keep $\sum|c|^p$ fixed is $p = 2$. Below: the experiments; the classical pictures they kill; the models, with every derivation step written out; the limits of each argument; and what is still argued over.

**The experiments.** *(1) Spectral lines are differences.* To better than one part in $10^4$, every frequency hydrogen emits is the difference of two numbers from one fixed list, the *terms* $R/n^2$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ and $R = 3.29 \times 10^{15}$ Hz (Ritz's combination principle, 1908). That figure names an idealization: looked at more finely, each term with $n \ge 2$ is a small cluster of terms (fine structure, about 11 GHz wide at $n = 2$, roughly one part in $10^5$ of the term; the Lamb shift, about 1 GHz at $n = 2$), and the $n = 1$ term is itself a pair 1.42 GHz apart (hyperfine structure, the source of the 21 cm radio line of hydrogen gas, which the list $R/n^2$ alone cannot give, since there a term would have to differ from itself). At every resolution so far reached the principle survives: every line is centred at the difference of two terms from the refined list. Balmer-$\alpha$ is $R(1/4 - 1/9) = 4.57 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, the red line at 656 nm; Lyman-$\alpha$ is $R(1 - 1/4) = 2.47 \times 10^{15}$ Hz, at 122 nm. No observed frequency needs a *sum* of terms to explain it: $R(1/4 + 1/9) = 1.19 \times 10^{15}$ Hz, for instance, is not a hydrogen line, and a sum of two terms coincides with a line only when it happens to equal a difference of two others. Franck and Hertz (1914) sent electrons through mercury vapour and found the current dropping every 4.9 V: an atom takes energy only in lumps, and the vapour then glows in the ultraviolet at 254 nm, whose $hf$ is 4.9 eV. Millikan (1916) confirmed $E = hf$ for the photoelectric effect. Together: the terms are energies divided by $h$, fixed only up to a common shift, since only differences are ever measured. An atom in its lowest term, left alone, stays there indefinitely, its charge distribution and its magnetic moment fixed; an atom in a higher term stays for a while (1.6 ns for the state that emits Lyman-$\alpha$) and then drops, emitting a line at the difference of the two terms. *(2) A spin in a steady magnetic field.* A proton in a field has two energy levels, one for its magnetic moment measured along the field and one for it measured against; "along" is the lower (the energy of a moment $\boldsymbol\mu$ in a field $\mathbf B$ is $-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$), and at 1 T the two differ by $h \times 42.6$ MHz. In nuclear magnetic resonance (Bloch, 1946; Purcell, Torrey & Pound, 1946; pulsed form: Hahn, 1950) a radio-frequency pulse tips the magnetization of a sample sideways; afterwards the sideways component precesses at 42.6 MHz per tesla, the *Larmor frequency*, which is the level difference divided by $h$, while the component along the field stays constant (idealization: times short compared with the relaxation times over which the sample settles back). *(3) Beats and Rabi flopping.* A short laser pulse puts caesium atoms into a superposition of two nearby levels (hands for both); the fluorescence that follows is modulated at the difference frequencies of the levels excited together, and no other modulation frequency was found (Haroche, Paisner & Schawlow, 1973: quantum beats, used to measure level splittings). An atom crossing a superconducting microwave cavity and exchanging a photon with it shows the chance of being in its upper level oscillating smoothly in time (Brune et al., 1996: Rabi oscillation); in the ideal case, on resonance and without losses, the swing is from 0 to 1 and back, and the real experiment sees the oscillation with reduced, decaying contrast. A pair of inversion levels of the ammonia molecule, $2A/h \approx 24$ GHz apart, gives the microwave line of the first maser. *(4) Beams flow.* Electrons of momentum $p$ scattered from a nickel crystal show diffraction peaks at angles set by the wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ (Davisson & Germer, 1927; $1.67 \times 10^{-10}$ m at 54 eV; Thomson, 1927, with thin films), and the beam is a current: it carries charge into the detector. Idealizations throughout: isolated atoms, no damping, exact resonance, one wavelength, a long wave train as the limit of a long packet.

**What this rules out.** *Classical particles.* A classical electron orbiting a nucleus would radiate at its orbit frequency and its whole-number multiples, and that frequency would drift continuously as the electron lost energy and spiralled inward: not a fixed list of differences, and not a stable atom; (1) kills it. Particles also have no wavelength; (4) kills them again. *A classical real wave* $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ gets wavelengths and difference-frequency beats, but its energy is spread out and divides at every splitter, while electrons and photons arrive whole (the two-slit dots; Grangier, Roger & Aspect, 1986). Note what the classical wave does *not* get wrong: a frictionless pendulum or a circularly polarized light wave carries a frequency while its energy, or its intensity, stays constant, and it manages that by using two real numbers at once (position and velocity; two field components a quarter cycle out of step), which is the same move the complex hand will make. What kills it is whole arrivals, not its arithmetic. *One real number per state*, combined and squared (your two-slit rule read literally). A stationary state of energy $E$ must either carry the frequency $E/h$ in its number, $c = \cos(Et/\hbar)$, and then the chance $c^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos(2Et/\hbar)$ pulses between 1 and 0 at twice the frequency: the chance of finding the proton's moment along the field, which (2) says is constant, would dip to zero twice per period, and the total chance could not stay at 1; or carry no frequency at all, and then two levels superposed produce no beat and no precession, which (2) and (3) do show. There is a second way to see the first branch fail: its pulsing at $2E/h$, and for two levels at $(E_1 + E_2)/h$, would change if you moved the zero of energy, which (1) leaves free, whereas every measured frequency is a difference and does not. Worked numbers for the attempt, with $\hbar\omega = E$:

| $Et/\hbar$ | 0 | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/2$ | $3\pi/4$ | $\pi$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| one real number $\cos(Et/\hbar)$ | 1 | 0.707 | 0 | −0.707 | −1 |
| its square, "chance of along" | 1 | 0.5 | 0 | 0.5 | 1 |
| hand $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$: squared length | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

What survives: a pair of real numbers per state, a hand that turns at the rate $E/\hbar$ and keeps its length.

**Model 1: the wave function and the Schrödinger equation.** *Intuition.* Every basis state (each term of the atom, each of the two spin states along a chosen axis, each point in space) has a hand: a complex number $c = a + ib$, which you can read as the pair $(a, b)$, with length $|c| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and a direction. A state of definite energy $E$ has a hand turning at the angular rate $E/\hbar$ with fixed length (clockwise for $E > 0$; the zero of energy is a convention, so the sense of turning is too, and only the turning of one hand relative to another is ever observed); the chance of an outcome is the squared length of the hand for that outcome. *Why a turning hand is stationary.* Write $\theta = Et/\hbar$ and $e^{-i\theta} = \cos\theta - i\sin\theta$. The squared length of $\psi = A e^{-i\theta}$ ($A$ real) is $\psi$ times its conjugate $\bar\psi = A e^{+i\theta}$ (the conjugate of a hand is its mirror image in the horizontal axis, $i$ replaced by $-i$):
$$|\psi|^2 = A(\cos\theta - i\sin\theta)\cdot A(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = A^2(\cos^2\theta - i^2\sin^2\theta) = A^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = A^2 .$$
Constant: the hand turns, the length does not. *Why two levels beat only at the difference.* Let level 1 have hand $c_1 = a\,e^{-i\omega_1 t}$ and level 2 have $c_2 = b\,e^{-i\omega_2 t}$, with $a, b$ real, $\omega_1 = E_1/\hbar$, $\omega_2 = E_2/\hbar$. Take any outcome whose amplitude gets an equal share from each level (for example, finding the atom in the combination state "(level 1 + level 2)$/\sqrt2$"; the amplitude to emit a photon in a given direction is of this type, with fixed shares that need not be equal, which changes the depth of the beat and can shift its timing but not its frequency). Its amplitude is $(c_1 + c_2)/\sqrt2$, and its chance is
$$\tfrac12|c_1 + c_2|^2 = \tfrac12(c_1 + c_2)(\bar c_1 + \bar c_2) = \tfrac12\big(|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 + c_1\bar c_2 + \bar c_1 c_2\big)
= \tfrac12\big(a^2 + b^2 + ab\,e^{-i(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t} + ab\,e^{+i(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}\big)$$
and since $e^{-i\phi} + e^{+i\phi} = 2\cos\phi$ this is $\tfrac12\big(a^2 + b^2 + 2ab\cos((\omega_2 - \omega_1)t)\big)$: one beat, at the difference frequency $(E_2 - E_1)/h$, exactly observation (3). Compare the real attempt, $c_1 = a\cos\omega_1 t$, $c_2 = b\cos\omega_2 t$:
$$(c_1 + c_2)^2 = a^2\cos^2\omega_1 t + b^2\cos^2\omega_2 t + 2ab\cos\omega_1 t\cos\omega_2 t
= \tfrac{a^2}{2}\big(1 + \cos 2\omega_1 t\big) + \tfrac{b^2}{2}\big(1 + \cos 2\omega_2 t\big) + ab\big[\cos(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t + \cos(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t\big],$$
using $\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)$ and $\cos x\cos y = \tfrac12[\cos(x - y) + \cos(x + y)]$. It contains $2\omega_1$, $2\omega_2$ and $\omega_1 + \omega_2$, frequencies that (1) and (3) do not show and that would move with the zero of energy. *The spin, worked.* Field along $z$; "along" means the proton's moment, and so its spin, along $+z$. Energies $E_{\rm a} = -\hbar\omega/2$ (along, the lower level) and $E_{\rm b} = +\hbar\omega/2$ (against), so $\omega = (E_{\rm b} - E_{\rm a})/\hbar > 0$ is the Larmor angular frequency. Each hand turns at its own level's rate, $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, so a spin prepared along $+x$, which has equal hands for the two levels, has $c_{\rm a} = e^{+i\omega t/2}/\sqrt2$ and $c_{\rm b} = e^{-i\omega t/2}/\sqrt2$. Chance of "along": $|c_{\rm a}|^2 = \tfrac12$, constant, as observed. The $+x$ state is itself the combination (along $+$ against)$/\sqrt2$, so the amplitude for "$+x$" is $(c_{\rm a} + c_{\rm b})/\sqrt2 = \tfrac12\big(e^{+i\omega t/2} + e^{-i\omega t/2}\big) = \cos(\omega t/2)$, and the chance is $\cos^2(\omega t/2) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \cos\omega t\big)$: it swings at the Larmor frequency $(E_{\rm b} - E_{\rm a})/h$; the chance of "$-x$", with amplitude $(c_{\rm a} - c_{\rm b})/\sqrt2 = i\sin(\omega t/2)$, is $\sin^2(\omega t/2)$, and the two add to 1 at every $t$. That is precession with a constant longitudinal part, observation (2). (Feynman III §7-5 and Griffiths §4.4.2 do this calculation.)

*Flow: why a beam's wave function cannot be real.* In the position form of the model the hand at each point $x$ is $\psi(x, t)$ and the chance of finding the particle near $x$ is $|\psi|^2$ per unit length. The Schrödinger equation (one dimension, mass $m$, potential energy $V(x)$ real) is $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2 + V\psi$; it says "the rate at which the hand turns and changes is set by how sharply the wave bends and by the potential". Its conjugate (replace $i$ by $-i$ everywhere) is $-i\hbar\,\partial\bar\psi/\partial t = -(\hbar^2/2m)\,\partial^2\bar\psi/\partial x^2 + V\bar\psi$. Then, writing $\psi'$ for $\partial\psi/\partial x$,
$$\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = \bar\psi\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} + \psi\,\frac{\partial\bar\psi}{\partial t}
= \bar\psi\cdot\frac{1}{i\hbar}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi\Big] + \psi\cdot\frac{-1}{i\hbar}\Big[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\bar\psi'' + V\bar\psi\Big].$$
The $V$ terms are $\frac{1}{i\hbar}(V\bar\psi\psi - V\psi\bar\psi) = 0$. What is left, with $1/i = -i$, is $\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\bar\psi\psi'' - \psi\bar\psi''\big)$, and because $(\bar\psi\psi' - \psi\bar\psi')' = \bar\psi'\psi' + \bar\psi\psi'' - \psi'\bar\psi' - \psi\bar\psi'' = \bar\psi\psi'' - \psi\bar\psi''$, this is
$$\frac{\partial|\psi|^2}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x},\qquad J = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\big(\psi\bar\psi' - \bar\psi\psi'\big) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(\bar\psi\,\psi'\big),$$
using $\bar\psi\psi' - \psi\bar\psi' = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\psi')$, where $\mathrm{Im}\,z$, the *imaginary part* of $z = x + iy$, is $y$. This is a continuity equation: chance is neither created nor destroyed, only carried from place to place by the *current* $J$. Now the point. If $\psi$ is real at some instant, $\bar\psi\psi' = \psi\psi'$ is real, its imaginary part is zero, and $J = 0$ everywhere: nothing flows, and the chance distribution is frozen for that instant, as a thrown ball is at rest at the top of its flight (and, like the ball, it need not stay so: the equation gives a real $\psi$ an imaginary part at once, and a real packet starts spreading). The electron beam of (4) is moving: its hand must be $\psi = A e^{ikx}$ with $k = 2\pi/\lambda = p/\hbar$, a hand that turns once per wavelength along the beam (idealization: a long packet, since a pure $e^{ikx}$ cannot be normalized). Check: $\bar\psi\psi' = \bar A e^{-ikx}\cdot ikAe^{ikx} = ik|A|^2$, so $J = (\hbar k/m)|A|^2 = v|A|^2$, density times velocity, the current a flowing beam needs. A standing wave $\cos kx$, two beams meeting head on, is real and has $J = 0$; a travelling one cannot be. (One more sign of the $i$: if $\psi$ were real at all times, the left side of the equation would be purely imaginary and the right side real, so both would vanish: a frozen wave with $H\psi = 0$, nothing else.)

*Why this model.* It is the one equation behind (1) to (4) and the rest of atomic physics: its definite-energy solutions for hydrogen give the terms $R/n^2$ (the fine structure needs relativistic corrections and spin, the Lamb shift needs quantum electrodynamics, and each refined level is still a stationary state with a turning hand), their hands turning at $E/\hbar$ give the differences-only spectrum and the beats, its magnetic term gives Larmor precession, and its plane waves give $\lambda = h/p$. *Pros.* Deterministic between measurements; the workhorse of chemistry and solid-state physics. *Cons.* The square is still a postulate here (Born, 1926, where the first paper's main text had the chance proportional to the amplitude and a footnote added in proof corrected it to the square); for several particles $\psi$ lives in a space with three coordinates per particle, not in ordinary space; and "complex" is a convention for "pairs of reals with the turning rule of multiplication": Stueckelberg (1960) showed that any such theory can be rewritten with twice as many real amplitudes plus one fixed operator that plays the role of $i$, and for a single system the two descriptions are the same theory. What the experiments force is that one real number per state is not enough and that the pairing rule is the one that turns hands at fixed length.

**Model 2: the sum over paths.** *Intuition.* Give every route from the start to the outcome a hand that turns by the route's action divided by $\hbar$; for a beam of fixed energy the hand turns by (momentum $\times$ distance)$/\hbar$ along the route, once per de Broglie wavelength. Add the hands tip to tail, square the length. *Why this model.* It is the same theory as model 1 in different mathematics (Feynman, 1948): the hand $e^{iS/\hbar}$ is the same $i$, and summing routes for a particle sitting in a definite-energy state reproduces the $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ turning. It adds no new prediction about the $i$ or the 2; it inherits both. *Pros.* The natural language for beams and scattering, and for seeing why heavy objects follow one path (hands near the classical path line up, the rest cancel). *Cons.* Clumsy for bound states, where model 1's stationary states are the right tool.

**Model 3 (established mathematics): why the exponent is 2.** The requirement: the chances of all the outcomes must add to 1 at every moment, whatever the state does. So ask what totals the equation of motion keeps fixed. Take two basis states with hands $c_1(t)$, $c_2(t)$, and call them state 1 and state 2. (In Feynman's ammonia example used below they are "nitrogen above the plane of the three hydrogens" and "nitrogen below it"; they are not the molecule's energy levels, which are the two stationary combinations worked out below.) The general linear equation of motion is
$$i\hbar\,\dot c_1 = H_{11}c_1 + H_{12}c_2,\qquad i\hbar\,\dot c_2 = H_{21}c_1 + H_{22}c_2,$$
(the dot is $d/dt$), where the four numbers $H_{jk}$ form the energy matrix; *linear* means each rate is a fixed combination of the hands, so that the sum of two solutions is a solution, which is what lets amplitudes add and cancel. Two conditions on the $H_{jk}$. $H_{11}$ and $H_{22}$ are real, because with the coupling switched off ($H_{12} = H_{21} = 0$) state 1 is a stationary state of energy $H_{11}$, with hand $c_1 = c_1(0)e^{-iH_{11}t/\hbar}$, and must keep its length (an imaginary part $-i\Gamma$ would make $|c_1|^2$ decay as $e^{-2\Gamma t/\hbar}$, which is how one describes an *unstable* level, whose missing chance reappears in the decay products). And $H_{21} = \bar H_{12}$. Be clear about the status of this one: it is an assumption, not something the spectra force. Real energies alone do not require it: the matrix with $H_{11} = H_{22} = 0$, $H_{12} = 1$, $H_{21} = 4$ has the real energies $\pm 2$, yet under it $|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2$ does not stay fixed: started in state 1, its solution is $c_1 = \cos(2t/\hbar)$, $c_2 = -2i\sin(2t/\hbar)$ (substitute to check: $i\hbar\dot c_1 = -2i\sin(2t/\hbar) = c_2$ and $i\hbar\dot c_2 = 4\cos(2t/\hbar) = 4c_1$), so the total is $\cos^2(2t/\hbar) + 4\sin^2(2t/\hbar) = 1 + 3\sin^2(2t/\hbar)$, swinging between 1 and 4. What $H_{21} = \bar H_{12}$ does is exactly to keep the total squared length fixed, as the next lines show; a matrix with both properties is called *Hermitian*, and the standard theory assumes its energy matrices are. Differentiate the squared length of $c_1$, using $\dot{\bar c}_1 = \overline{\dot c_1}$:
$$\frac{d|c_1|^2}{dt} = \bar c_1\dot c_1 + c_1\dot{\bar c}_1
= \bar c_1\frac{H_{11}c_1 + H_{12}c_2}{i\hbar} + c_1\frac{H_{11}\bar c_1 + \bar H_{12}\bar c_2}{-i\hbar}
= \frac{1}{i\hbar}\Big(H_{11}|c_1|^2 - H_{11}|c_1|^2 + H_{12}\bar c_1c_2 - \bar H_{12}c_1\bar c_2\Big)
= \frac{2}{\hbar}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big),$$
since $z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$ and $2i/i\hbar = 2/\hbar$. The same steps for $c_2$, with $H_{21} = \bar H_{12}$, give $\frac{d|c_2|^2}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\big(\bar H_{12}c_1\bar c_2 - H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big) = -\frac{2}{\hbar}\,\mathrm{Im}\big(H_{12}\bar c_1c_2\big)$. The two rates are equal and opposite:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\big(|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2\big) = 0 .$$
Whatever leaves state 1 arrives in state 2, measured in squared lengths. (For a wave function the same statement is $\frac{d}{dt}\int|\psi|^2dx = -[J]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$, from the continuity equation above with $J \to 0$ far away: Griffiths §1.4.) On its own this is circular as an argument for the square: the condition we assumed is the one that conserves it. What is not circular is the comparison with the other powers, which needs a solution to look at. *Now the other powers.* Take Feynman's ammonia matrix $H_{11} = H_{22} = 0$, $H_{12} = H_{21} = -A$ with $A$ real and positive (Feynman III ch. 8–9): state 1 is nitrogen above the plane, state 2 nitrogen below. Its energy levels are the stationary combinations: with $c_2 = c_1$ the equation gives $i\hbar\dot c_1 = -Ac_2 = -Ac_1$, a hand turning at the rate of energy $-A$; with $c_2 = -c_1$ it gives $i\hbar\dot c_1 = +Ac_1$, energy $+A$; the two are $2A$ apart, the inversion doublet of (3), and their chances, $\tfrac12$ each for a molecule started in state 1, never change. Start in state 1 and try $c_1 = \cos(At/\hbar)$, $c_2 = i\sin(At/\hbar)$. Check by substitution: $i\hbar\dot c_1 = i\hbar\cdot\big(-\tfrac{A}{\hbar}\sin\tfrac{At}{\hbar}\big) = -iA\sin\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -A c_2$ ✓; $i\hbar\dot c_2 = i\hbar\cdot i\tfrac{A}{\hbar}\cos\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -A\cos\tfrac{At}{\hbar} = -Ac_1$ ✓. (A *real* $c_2 = \sin(At/\hbar)$ fails the first check: the hand of state 2 is forced a quarter turn away from that of state 1. The chance $|c_2|^2 = \sin^2(At/\hbar)$ swinging between 0 and 1 is the nitrogen passing through the plane and back; it has the same mathematical form as the Rabi flopping of observation (3), where the two coupled states are "atom in its upper level, cavity empty" and "atom in its lower level, one photon in the cavity".) Now compute the candidate totals $|c_1|^p + |c_2|^p = \cos^p\theta + \sin^p\theta$, $\theta = At/\hbar$, at $\theta = \pi/4$ where $\cos\theta = \sin\theta = 1/\sqrt2$: $2\cdot(1/\sqrt2)^p = 2^{1 - p/2}$.

| $\theta = At/\hbar$ | 0 | $\pi/8$ | $\pi/4$ | $3\pi/8$ | $\pi/2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $p = 1$: $\cos\theta + \sin\theta$ | 1 | 1.307 | 1.414 | 1.307 | 1 |
| $p = 2$: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| $p = 3$ | 1 | 0.845 | 0.707 | 0.845 | 1 |
| $p = 4$ | 1 | 0.750 | 0.500 | 0.750 | 1 |

All powers agree at $\theta = 0$, where all the hand is in state 1; only $p = 2$ stays at 1 in between (any power below 2 overshoots, any power above 2 undershoots, worst at $\pi/4$). Since the nitrogen is always found on one side of the plane or the other, the chances must total 1 at every $t$, and under this equation only the square does. But this equation is Hermitian and keeps the square by construction, so a sceptic can ask for a different equation, built to keep a different power fixed. The theorem of Banach (1932) and Lamperti (1958) says there is none worth having: for $p \ne 2$ ($1 \le p < \infty$, two or more states) the only linear maps that keep $\sum_k|c_k|^p$ fixed for *every* state are relabellings of the basis states combined with turning each hand on the spot. A relabelling moves chance between outcomes all at once or not at all; none moves it gradually, as the ammonia inversion (between nitrogen-up and nitrogen-down), the Rabi oscillation (between the atom's two levels, the photon going to the cavity and back) and the Larmor precession (between the $+x$ and $-x$ outcomes, while the chance of each energy level stays fixed) all do. (The one escape, $p = 1$ with hands that are never negative, is ordinary probability, where nothing cancels, so no beat and no dark fringe is possible.) For $p = 2$ the length-keeping linear maps are exactly the unitary ones, and every Hermitian equation generates a continuous family of them. So among power rules, gradual transfer of chance leaves only the square.

*Only among powers.* With just two outcomes a non-power rule can also total 1: $f(x) = x^2 + \varepsilon\,x^2(1 - x^2)(2x^2 - 1)$ applied to each hand's length. For a state $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, writing $C = \cos^2\theta$, $S = \sin^2\theta$ with $C + S = 1$:
$$f(\cos\theta) + f(\sin\theta) = C + S + \varepsilon\big[CS(2C - 1) + SC(2S - 1)\big] = 1 + \varepsilon\,CS\,\big[2C + 2S - 2\big] = 1 + 0 = 1 .$$
It also has $f(0) = 0$, $f(1) = 1$, and stays non-negative for $|\varepsilon| < 1$ (the factor $(1 - x^2)(2x^2 - 1)$ lies between $-1$ and $1/8$). So two outcomes alone cannot exclude it; measurements only push $\varepsilon$ below their error bars. Three outcomes do exclude it: for the state $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$, each hand has $x^2 = 1/3$ and $f = \tfrac13\big[1 + \varepsilon\cdot\tfrac23\cdot(-\tfrac13)\big] = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{2\varepsilon}{27}$, so the three chances total $1 - 2\varepsilon/9$, not 1, unless $\varepsilon = 0$. Gleason's theorem (1957) makes this general: for three or more mutually exclusive outcomes, if each outcome's chance depends only on the state and on that outcome's own direction (not on which other outcomes are measured alongside it) and the chances over every complete set of outcomes total 1, then the chance must be a squared shadow of the state, or a weighted average of such squares; adding that a state prepared in a level is found in it with certainty leaves the squared shadow itself. No power, no wiggle, no other rule survives. For two-level systems alone the theorem needs Busch's (2003) extension to imperfect measurements. *Why this account.* It ties the exponent to the dynamics: the equation that the spectra, beats and precession establish moves chance gradually between outcomes, and a gradual linear motion can keep a power-total fixed only for the power 2; Gleason's consistency condition then removes the rules that are not powers. *Pros.* It makes "why 2" a physical reason, conservation under gradual change, not a fit. *Cons.* The Hermitian condition that makes the square the conserved total is assumed, so the ammonia calculation illustrates rather than proves, and the theorem that does prove it assumes linearity and gradual transfer; Gleason's non-contextuality is an assumption; and none of it says why there are chances at all.

**Where the interpretations stand** (all keep the $i$ and the square and agree on every count above; they differ on what the conserved density is a density *of*, and every position here is contested).

*Pilot-wave.* The particle always has a position and moves with velocity $v = J/|\psi|^2$ (current divided by density). Why the square is then stable: a cloud of particles with density $\sigma(x, t)$, each moving with the velocity $v(x, t)$ of its place, obeys $\partial\sigma/\partial t = -\partial(\sigma v)/\partial x$ (the number in a small interval changes only by what flows in at one end minus what flows out at the other). Put $\sigma = |\psi|^2$: then $\sigma v = J$ and this is exactly the continuity equation derived above. So a cloud distributed as $|\psi|^2$ at one moment is distributed as $|\psi|^2$ for ever (*equivariance*). A cloud distributed as $|\psi|$ is, in general, not. To compare fairly it must be normalized, $\sigma = \sqrt\rho/N(t)$ with $\rho = |\psi|^2$ and $N = \int\sqrt\rho\,dx$, because $\int\sqrt\rho\,dx$ changes in time while $\int\rho\,dx$ does not. From the true equation $\partial\rho/\partial t = -(\rho v)' = -\rho' v - \rho v'$ (primes are $x$-derivatives),
$$\frac{\partial\sqrt\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt\rho}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -\frac{\rho' v}{2\sqrt\rho} - \tfrac12\sqrt\rho\,v' = -(\sqrt\rho\,v)' + \tfrac12\sqrt\rho\,v',$$
using $(\sqrt\rho\,v)' = \rho' v/(2\sqrt\rho) + \sqrt\rho\,v'$. Integrating over $x$ (the transport term integrates to zero) gives $dN/dt = \tfrac12\int\sqrt\rho\,v'\,dx$, and so for $\sigma = \sqrt\rho/N$:
$$\frac{\partial\sigma}{\partial t} = -(\sigma v)' + \tfrac12\,\sigma\,\big(v' - \langle v'\rangle\big),\qquad \langle v'\rangle = \int\sigma\,v'\,dx .$$
The extra term vanishes only when $\partial v/\partial x$ is the same everywhere the cloud is, a pure stretch, as for one freely spreading Gaussian packet; for two overlapping packets it is not, and the $|\psi|$ cloud drifts away from $|\psi|$ (figure), while the $|\psi|^2$ cloud never does. So here the square is an equilibrium, like the settled distribution of a gas. Why the actual cloud is the $|\psi|^2$ one is argued from typicality (Dürr, Goldstein & Zanghì, 1992) or from relaxation (Valentini; simulations in Valentini & Westman, 2005), and critics debate whether "typical" explains or restates; the extension to photons is less settled.

*Copenhagen-style.* The hands are a tool for computing chances; the square is a postulate, with Gleason showing it is the only consistent one given the structure. Pro: minimal. Con: critics object that "measurement" is left undefined. *Many-worlds.* The hands are everything and never collapse; Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (2012) derive the square from rules of rational betting plus the symmetries of branching, and Zurek (2003) from a symmetry of entangled states; critics (Barnum et al., 2000; Schlosshauer & Fine, 2005; Kent, 2010) object that the derivations assume what they prove or that "chance" is unclear when every outcome occurs; supporters reply that the assumptions are weaker than the rule. *Objective collapse.* The wave is real and localizes at random, and the square is written into the collapse law (Ghirardi, Rimini & Weber, 1986); Gisin (1989) showed that, given the standard description of entangled pairs, any other weighting would allow faster-than-light signalling, so there the square is forced by no-signalling. Con: new constants, and the predicted small departures from standard quantum mechanics have been searched for and not found so far (Bassi et al., 2013).

**A closing note on "complex" for pairs of systems.** For one system, pairs of reals with the right turning rule and complex numbers are the same theory. For two independently prepared systems the standard rule is that the joint hands are the products of the separate hands, and with *that* rule a theory using only real hands makes different predictions from complex quantum mechanics. Renou et al. (2021) designed a three-party, two-source Bell-type test and computed that real-hand theories cannot score above 7.66, while complex quantum mechanics predicts up to $6\sqrt2 \approx 8.49$; two experiments in 2022 (Li et al., with photons in an optical network; Chen et al., with superconducting qubits) exceeded 7.66 by many standard deviations. Caveats: the test assumes the two sources are independent, and it is not loophole-free. For your question, the experiments above already settle the single-system case: one real number per state cannot be stationary and carry a frequency at once.

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2 are one theory in two sets of mathematics, with the same $i$ (a hand that turns at $E/\hbar$ in time and once per wavelength in space) and the same square. Model 3 is mathematics about that theory: the square is the total a Hermitian equation conserves, and the theorem says that a linear motion which moves chance gradually can conserve no other power's total, so among power rules the 2 is forced by the gradual transfers of chance between outcomes that precession, Rabi flopping and the ammonia inversion show; among all rules it is forced by Gleason's theorem, with its assumptions named. The interpretations keep every number and differ on what the conserved density is: a tool for prediction, a branch weight, the equilibrium distribution of real particles, or the weighting in a collapse law; no experiment has told the first three apart, and objective collapse is a slightly different theory still being tested. So: the $i$ is "turn without changing length", forced by stationary states that carry a frequency; the 2 is "the total that gradual turning keeps fixed", the only power a gradual linear motion can conserve.
