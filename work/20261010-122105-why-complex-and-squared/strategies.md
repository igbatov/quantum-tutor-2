# Strategies: why is the wave function complex, and why is the chance its *squared* size?

Learner: intermediate level requested explicitly (algebra, complex numbers as pairs of reals / clock hands, 2-vectors, 2×2 matrices, derivatives; no Dirac operators or Hilbert-space theory). Follow-up to two earlier answers: the two-slit "what is QM" answer (final-A, 2026-10-08: "combine amplitudes first, square after", 1+1=2→4, +1−1=0→0) and the conceptual "why exactly the square" run (2026-10-09; finalists A "Pythagoras at the polarizer" and C "three slits, add and subtract"; the student judge picked A by a slight margin). The judge's notes from that run say what this learner values: a one-line reason up front that can be checked with a calculator; arithmetic done in front of them; honest caveats about the limits of each argument; every lab term defined (they lost footing on "heralded", "wave plate", "Stern–Gerlach", "unitary"). This run must not repeat the conceptual content at length: it must add the mathematics, with every derivation step written out.

The question has two parts and every strategy must answer both:
1. **Why complex?** Why one amplitude is a pair of real numbers (a clock hand with a length and a direction) rather than one real number.
2. **Why the square?** Why the chance is the squared length of the hand, not the length, not the fourth power, not some other function.

## Audit of the quick answer's claims

| Claim in the quick answer | Verdict | Correct form to use |
|---|---|---|
| "Complex because phase must turn continuously (interferometer phase shifts)" | Right idea, wrong as a slogan. A real 2-vector can be turned continuously (that is what the polarizer answer did), and a classical real wave cos(ωt + φ) has a continuous phase. | "A device that acts on *one arm only* and leaves that arm's own count unchanged can only multiply the arm's amplitude by a number of size 1. If the amplitude were one real number, that number could only be +1 or −1, and the exits could only show the two extreme patterns. The exits move smoothly as the plate tilts, so the arm's amplitude must have a direction that can turn without its length changing: a pair of reals, i.e. a complex number of modulus 1, e^{iφ}." (Strategy A.) |
| Neutron 4π spinor rotation as evidence for complexness | Misplaced. Rauch et al. (1975) and Werner et al. (1975) show the interference term changes sign after a 2π spin rotation and returns after 4π. A sign flip is available to real amplitudes too. It is evidence that the *sign* of an amplitude is physical relative to another amplitude, and of spin-½ structure, not of complex numbers. | Drop, or one line labelled as evidence for relative sign, not for i. |
| Stationary states e^{−iEt/ħ} | Correct. | A state of definite energy E must carry the frequency E/h (Bohr–Ritz: emitted frequencies are differences of fixed terms; Planck–Einstein E = hf) yet be stationary (nothing observable changes; a spin along a magnetic field stays along it). A single real number carrying a frequency, cos(Et/ħ), has a size that pulses; a real number with no time dependence gives no beats. A hand turning at fixed length does both. (Strategy B, with the beat algebra shown.) |
| Plane wave e^{i(kx−ωt)} vs cos | Correct, and sharpenable. | For any real, normalizable ψ(x), the mean momentum is zero: ⟨p⟩ = −iħ∫ψψ′dx = −iħ[ψ²/2] = 0. A beam with the measured de Broglie wavelength h/p moves, so its wave function cannot be real at any instant. (Strategy B.) |
| "Complex = pairs of reals for one system" | Correct (Stueckelberg 1960): any complex-amplitude theory can be rewritten with twice as many real numbers plus one fixed rule that pairs them. For a single system the two descriptions are the same theory. | Say it this way in every strategy; "complex is forced" means "one real number per outcome is not enough, and the pairing rule is that of complex multiplication". |
| Real QM ruled out for networks (Renou et al., Nature 600, 625 (2021); Li et al. PRL 128, 040402 (2022); Chen et al. PRL 128, 040403 (2022)) | Correct with caveats. What is ruled out is a theory with real amplitudes *and* the standard rule that independent sources give a product state. The test is a three-party, two-source Bell-type score: real-amplitude theories cannot exceed 7.66; complex quantum mechanics predicts up to 6√2 ≈ 8.49; both experiments (one with photons, one with superconducting qubits; verify which is which before naming) exceeded 7.66 by many standard deviations. Not loophole-free; assumes the two sources are independent. | Use only as a closing note on composite systems, with these caveats, and quote measured scores only if verified. |
| Three-slit Sorkin test, I₃ = 0 (Sinha et al., Science 329, 418 (2010)) | Correct, with the idealization that each slit's amplitude is the same whether or not the other slits are open. Standard QM itself predicts a small nonzero leftover from looped paths (Sawant et al. PRL 113, 120406 (2014); measured with plasmonic slits by Magaña-Loaiza et al., Nat. Commun. 7, 13987 (2016)). The 2010 bound: leftover below about 10⁻² of the two-path interference. | Strategy C, with the complex algebra of |a+b+c|² written out and the idealization named. |
| Beam splitter: 2·(1/√2)^p = 2^{1−p/2} = 1 only for p = 2 | Correct arithmetic but circular on its own: that the two amplitudes are 1/√2 is itself read off the square rule. | Non-circular version: whatever the power p, the splitter-plus-plate family is a *continuous* family of linear maps that moves photons between exits and keeps every photon; for p ≠ 2 no such family exists (Banach–Lamperti). Show the 2^{1−p/2} line as a worked instance of the obstruction, not as the proof. (Strategy A.) |
| Banach–Lamperti | Correct: in n ≥ 2 dimensions, for 1 ≤ p < ∞ and p ≠ 2, the only linear maps that preserve the p-norm of *every* vector are permutations of the components combined with sign or phase changes of each component (Banach 1932; Lamperti 1958). For p = 2 they are the orthogonal/unitary maps. Aaronson's version adds: p = 1 with non-negative amplitudes and sum-preservation (not norm-preservation) is classical probability with stochastic matrices. | State the theorem with its conditions; show a 2×2 worked obstruction; do not claim to prove it. |
| Gleason's theorem | Correct: dimension ≥ 3; the assumption is that each outcome's chance depends only on that outcome's direction and the state (non-contextuality) and that chances over any complete orthogonal set add to 1; the conclusion is that chances are Tr(ρP), i.e. squared shadows or averages of them; certainty for the prepared direction picks the pure-state square. Dimension 2 needs Busch (2003) with generalized measurements. | Any strategy may use it as the closer for non-power rules; always name the assumptions. |
| Pilot-wave equivariance | Correct: ρ = |ψ|² obeys the same continuity equation as a cloud of particles moving with v = J/|ψ|², so a |ψ|²-distributed ensemble stays |ψ|²-distributed; |ψ| or |ψ|⁴ do not. Why the actual ensemble is |ψ|² is the contested part (typicality, Dürr–Goldstein–Zanghì 1992; relaxation, Valentini). | Strategy B shows the three-line derivation. |
| Zurek envariance; Deutsch–Wallace; "disputed" | Correct labelling. Critics: Barnum et al. Proc. R. Soc. A 456, 1175 (2000); Schlosshauer & Fine, Found. Phys. 35, 197 (2005); Kent (2010). | Label contested in every strategy; Strategy C shows the equal-amplitude swap step. |

Traps for every candidate (skill section 0):
- Never write "real numbers can't turn continuously". Write "one real number per arm/outcome".
- For two outcomes, "the square is the only rule that keeps the total at 1" is true *only among powers*: f(x) = x² + ε·x²(1−x²)(2x²−1) also satisfies f(cos θ) + f(sin θ) = 1, f(0) = 0, f(1) = 1, f ≥ 0 for |ε| < 1. Show it, then show it fails for three outcomes at (1,1,1)/√3, then cite Gleason.
- A lossless 50/50 beam splitter can be written with real entries (the matrix (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]]). The i is forced by the one-arm phase plate, not by the splitter.
- "A stationary state does not pulse" is not directly observable at optical frequencies (10¹⁵ Hz). Use a spin in a static magnetic field (the component along the field is constant; the transverse component precesses at the Larmor frequency, which is (E₊ − E₋)/h), where it is observed, and the consistency argument (a pulsing size would break the total of 1).
- Every lab term defined on first use: heralded photon, beam splitter, phase plate, wave plate, polarizing splitter, Hermitian, unitary, norm.
- Idealizations named each time: lossless optics, perfect detectors, one wavelength, independent slit amplitudes, plane waves as the limit of long packets.
- The learner wants an illustration per section, but the pipeline allows two figure requests per candidate. Use the two figures for the two load-bearing points and give every other section a small table of worked numbers as its picture.

### Strategy A: One arm, one clock hand (interferometer and unitary matrices)

**Experiments.** (1) A Mach–Zehnder interferometer with single photons: a half-silvered mirror (beam splitter) divides the beam into two arms, two mirrors bring them back together on a second beam splitter, and a detector sits at each of the two exits. Photons are sent one at a time (heralded: produced in pairs so that detecting one announces the other). In one arm a thin glass plate can be tilted, which lengthens the optical path in that arm by a controllable amount. Grangier, Roger & Aspect (1986) did this with heralded single photons and saw fringes of visibility close to 100% (about 98%; verify). (2) The same with neutrons: a single-crystal interferometer whose arms are separated by centimetres, with an aluminium plate rotated in one arm (Rauch, Treimer & Bonse 1974). Observed, in the ideal lossless case: (a) each photon is counted at one exit, never both; (b) as the plate tilts, the fraction at exit 1 swings smoothly between 100% and 0% and back, repeating every time the extra path grows by one wavelength, and the fraction at exit 2 is the mirror image; (c) the two exits together account for every photon at every tilt; (d) a detector placed in the arm with the plate counts half the photons whatever the tilt, and with the other arm blocked each exit receives one quarter, independent of the tilt. Idealizations: lossless splitters and plates, perfect detectors, one wavelength.

**Classical candidates ruled out.** Tiny balls: a plate could delay a ball but could not move it from one exit to the other; no fringes. Classical real wave: gets (b) and (c) as energy fractions and has a continuous phase, but divides each pulse's energy between the exits, so weak pulses would fire both detectors together; killed by (a) (the 1986 anticorrelation measurement). A rule with *one real number per arm* that combines and squares (the two-slit answer's rule read literally): the plate acts on arm 1 alone and keeps its count (d), so it can only multiply that arm's number by +1 or −1, giving only the two extreme patterns, never the smooth sweep of (b). What survives: a pair of reals per arm, a hand that the plate turns by an angle set by the extra path.

**Models.** *Model 1: state vectors and unitary matrices (standard quantum mechanics for a two-path system).* Intuition: the photon's state is a pair of complex numbers (u, v), one per arm; a lossless optical element is a 2×2 matrix acting on the pair; the chance of an exit is the squared modulus of its entry. Derivations, every step: the splitter matrix H = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]] (real; show that it keeps |u|²+|v|²); the plate P = [[z,0],[0,1]] with |z| = 1 from (d); input (1,0) → H → (1,1)/√2 → P → (z,1)/√2 → H → ((z+1)/2, (z−1)/2); with z = e^{iφ}: |z+1|² = (cos φ + 1)² + sin²φ = 2 + 2cos φ, so P₁ = cos²(φ/2), P₂ = sin²(φ/2), sum 1 for every φ; with z = ±1 only (0 or 1); blocked arm gives (z/2, z/2), chance ¼ each. Why this model: one matrix picture gives (a)–(d) and the fringe period. Pros: the matrix picture is the whole theory in miniature (qubits, spin, interferometers); cons: the square is a postulate here (Born 1926, the footnote), and "lossless ⇒ unitary" already assumes the square. *Model 2: sum over paths.* Every route gets a hand turning with path length (for photons) or action/ħ (for electrons): the plate adds turning to every route through arm 1; the same two-exit algebra results. Equivalent to model 1; use it when thinking about routes. *Model 3 (established mathematics): why the exponent is 2.* Suppose the chance is |amplitude|^p and devices act linearly. A lossless device must keep Σ|c|^p = 1 for every input. Banach–Lamperti: for p ≠ 2 the only such linear maps are relabellings of the exits with sign or phase changes; none moves a photon from one exit to the other *gradually*. Worked obstruction: for p = 4 the "splitter" would have to be 2^{−1/4}[[1,1],[1,−1]]; it keeps the 4-norm of (1,0) (2·2^{−1} = 1) but sends the unit-4-norm input 2^{−1/4}(1,1) to √2·(1,0), whose fourth-power total is 4, not 1; for p = 1 the analogous map sends (1,−1)/2 to (0,0), losing the photon; only p = 2 keeps every input. The 2^{1−p/2} line at the 50/50 setting is shown as the instance. Then the two-outcome wiggle (so "only among powers"), and Gleason (dimension ≥ 3, non-contextuality) as the closer for arbitrary rules; Busch 2003 for two dimensions. *Interpretations* (contested, short, labelled): Copenhagen (postulate), many-worlds (Deutsch–Wallace, Zurek; critics), pilot-wave (equilibrium), objective collapse (built into the collapse law; Gisin's no-signalling reason). *Closing on complex:* for a single system the pair-of-reals description is the same theory (Stueckelberg 1960); for independent sources combined, real amplitudes with the product rule fail a 2021 network test (Renou et al.; two 2022 experiments), with caveats.

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2: one theory; model 3 is mathematics about the rule, forcing the exponent among powers from continuity and, with Gleason, among all rules; the interpretations keep the i and the square and differ on what the hand is.

**Figures.** `a-interferometer-counts`: fractions at exit 1 (cos²(φ/2)) and exit 2 (sin²(φ/2)) against the plate phase φ from 0 to 4π, their sum flat at 1, the one-arm-blocked level flat at ¼, and the two "real-number" possibilities marked as isolated points at φ = 0 and π; inset: the two arm hands at φ = 0, π/2, π. Notice: the smooth sweep, the flat sum, and that one real number per arm could only give the two marked points. `a-pnorm-circles`: the unit curves |x|^p + |y|^p = 1 for p = 1 (diamond), 2 (circle) and 4 (rounded square), with the point (1,0) and its images under rotations by 15°, 30°, …, 90°: the rotated points stay on the circle but leave the diamond (1.41 at 45°) and the rounded square (0.5 at 45°), rejoining only at 90°. Caption: a rotation is one continuous linear map; the theorem says no other continuous family works for p ≠ 2 either.

**Opening sentence.** "Put a thin glass plate in one arm of an interferometer and send photons through one at a time: as you tilt the plate, the share of photons leaving by each exit sweeps smoothly from all to none and back, while a counter placed in that arm alone registers the same number whatever the tilt."

**Suggested check.** With the plate set so that every photon leaves by exit 1, you block the other arm: what fraction now reaches exit 1? Work it with the matrices.

(2021); Li et al. and Chen et al. (2022); interpretation sources as in the previous run's finals.

### Strategy B: Clocks that keep their length (stationary states, beats, flow, and a conservation law)

**Experiments.** (1) Spectral lines: every hydrogen line frequency is a difference of two fixed numbers (the terms R/n²; Ritz combination principle), never a sum; Franck–Hertz (1914) shows atoms take energy in fixed amounts and Millikan (1916) confirms E = hf for photons, so the terms are energies/h. (2) A spin in a static magnetic field (nuclear magnetic resonance, Bloch 1946; neutron polarimetry): the component along the field is constant in time; the transverse component precesses at the Larmor frequency, which equals (E₊ − E₋)/h for the two spin energies. (3) Quantum beats: a short pulse puts an atom into a superposition of two nearby levels; the fluorescence is modulated at exactly the difference frequency (Haroche, Paisner & Schawlow 1973); or Rabi oscillation of a two-level atom (Brune et al. 1996). (4) Electron diffraction: a beam of electrons of momentum p shows the wavelength λ = h/p (Davisson & Germer 1927; Thomson 1927), and it is a beam: it flows into the detector. Idealizations: isolated atoms, no damping, monochromatic beams, plane waves as the limit of long packets.

**Classical candidates ruled out.** Classical particles have no frequency or wavelength (killed by 1 and 4). A classical real wave cos(kx − ωt) has nodes and a size that pulses at 2ω, divides its energy, and has no stationary state that carries a frequency (killed by whole arrivals and by 2). A quantum rule with one real number per state faces a dichotomy: either the number carries the frequency, c = cos(Et/ħ), and then c² pulses (for a spin along the field the chance of "up" would dip below 1 twice per period, which (2) does not show, and the total chance could not stay 1), or it carries no frequency and then two levels superposed give no beat and no precession (killed by 2 and 3).

**Models.** *Model 1: the wave function and the Schrödinger equation.* Intuition: each basis state has a hand; a state of energy E has a hand turning at rate E/ħ with fixed length. Derivations: |A e^{−iEt/ħ}|² = A² (write z z̄ out); two levels, |a e^{−iω₁t} + b e^{−iω₂t}|² = a² + b² + 2ab cos((ω₂ − ω₁)t) (difference only), against the real attempt (a cos ω₁t + b cos ω₂t)² which contains 2ω₁, 2ω₂ and ω₁ + ω₂ as well (expand with product-to-sum); for the spin, c₊ = e^{−iωt/2}/√2, c₋ = e^{+iωt/2}/√2 gives chance of +z constant at ½ and chance of +x = cos²(ωt/2): precession at ω = (E₊ − E₋)/ħ with constant longitudinal part. Flow: from iħ∂ψ/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∂²ψ/∂x² + Vψ and its conjugate, derive ∂|ψ|²/∂t = −∂J/∂x with J = (ħ/m) Im(ψ̄ ∂ψ/∂x), every step; for real ψ, J = 0, and ⟨p⟩ = −iħ∫ψψ′dx = −iħ[ψ²/2] = 0: a real wave function describes something momentarily at rest; a beam with λ = h/p needs e^{ikx}, for which J = (ħk/m)|A|² = v|A|². Why this model: it is the equation behind (1)–(4) and all of atomic physics. Pros/cons as usual (Born rule a postulate; configuration space for many particles). *Model 2: sum over paths.* The hand of a path turns by (action)/ħ; the e^{iS/ħ} is the same i, and for a stationary state the sum reproduces e^{−iEt/ħ}; equivalent. *Model 3 (established mathematics): why 2.* The total chance must be 1 at every moment; the equation conserves exactly one simple total. Discrete form: iħ ċ₁ = H₁₁c₁ + H₁₂c₂, iħ ċ₂ = H₂₁c₁ + H₂₂c₂ with H₂₁ = H̄₁₂ and H₁₁, H₂₂ real (Hermitian: the condition that energies are real); compute d/dt(|c₁|² + |c₂|²) term by term and show it is zero. Continuous form: d/dt∫|ψ|²dx = −[J]₋∞^∞ = 0 (Griffiths §1.4). Then the ammonia-type example H = [[0,−A],[−A,0]]: c₁ = cos(At/ħ), c₂ = i sin(At/ħ) (verify by substitution), so |c₁|^p + |c₂|^p = cos^p + sin^p, equal to 1 at t = 0 and to 2^{1−p/2} at At/ħ = π/4: constant only if p = 2. The two-outcome wiggle f(x) = x² + εx²(1−x²)(2x²−1) (show it sums to 1 for any (cos θ, sin θ)), then show it fails for three levels at (1,1,1)/√3, then Gleason (dimension ≥ 3, non-contextuality) for any rule. *Interpretations:* pilot-wave with the derivation shown: ρ = |ψ|² and v = J/ρ satisfy ∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρv)/∂x = 0, which is also the equation for the density of a cloud of particles moving with velocity v; so a cloud distributed as |ψ|² stays so (equivariance); a cloud distributed as |ψ| does not (one-line check); why the actual cloud is |ψ|² is contested (typicality; relaxation). Copenhagen, many-worlds, objective collapse: short, labelled, with the Gisin no-signalling sentence. *Closing on complex:* Stueckelberg's pairs of reals; the 2021 network test with caveats.

**How they relate.** Models 1 and 2: one theory; model 3: the exponent is the one conserved total, forced among powers by the dynamics and among all rules by Gleason; interpretations differ on what the conserved density is a density *of*.

**Figures.** `b-stationary-vs-cos`: top, |ψ|² at one point against time for a single state of definite energy under the hand rule (flat at 1) and under a real cos rule (pulsing between 0 and 2 at twice the frequency); bottom, the same for an equal superposition of two levels with ω₂ = 1.1 ω₁ under both rules: the hand rule gives only the slow beat at ω₂ − ω₁, the real rule adds fast pulsing. Notice: the hand rule's single state is stationary and its pair beats only at the difference frequency. `b-rabi-norms`: |c₁|² = cos²(At/ħ) and |c₂|² = sin²(At/ħ) over one period, and Σ|c_n|^p for p = 1, 2, 3, 4 on the same axis: p = 1 rises to 1.41, p = 3 dips to 0.71, p = 4 to 0.5, p = 2 flat at 1. Notice: only the square stays at 1.

**Opening sentence.** "A hydrogen atom in a state of definite energy E is stationary (nothing observable about it changes in time), yet the light it emits when it changes to energy E′ has frequency exactly (E − E′)/h, which only works if the state carries the frequency E/h in a form that leaves every observable constant."

**Suggested check.** A spin prepared along +x in a field along z: using c₊ = e^{−iωt/2}/√2 and c₋ = e^{+iωt/2}/√2, show that the chance of +z stays ½ while the chance of +x oscillates, and find its frequency.

### Strategy C: Three ways to be fifty-fifty, and three slits (counting what nature allows)

**Experiments.** (1) Polarization sorters: a polarizing beam splitter (sends horizontal, H, out of one face and vertical, V, out of the other); the same turned by 45° (diagonal D and anti-diagonal A); and a circular sorter: a quarter-wave plate (a crystal slab that delays one polarization component by a quarter of a period relative to the other) followed by a polarizing splitter, which sends right-circular (R) and left-circular (L) light to different exits. Single heralded photons prepared as H, D or R by the matching element. Observed (ideal lossless optics): H photons: 100/0 at H/V, 50/50 at D/A, 50/50 at R/L; D photons: 50/50 at H/V, 100/0 at D/A, 50/50 at R/L; R photons: 50/50 at H/V, 50/50 at D/A, 100/0 at R/L. Each photon leaves whole by one exit. Beth (1936) measured the angular momentum carried by circularly polarized light, ±ħ per photon in the single-photon reading. The spin twin: a spin-½ particle sorted along z, x or y shows the same 100/50/50 table (one paragraph; sequential sorting with atoms is the textbook idealization, realized with neutron and atom polarimetry). (2) The three-slit test (Sinha et al. 2010): slits A, B, C opened singly, in pairs and all together; the leftover P_ABC − P_AB − P_AC − P_BC + P_A + P_B + P_C measured spot by spot; consistent with zero, bounded below about 10⁻² of the two-path interference; later five-path (Kauten et al. 2017) and molecule (Cotter et al. 2017) versions. Idealizations: lossless optics; each slit's amplitude unchanged by whether the others are open.

**Classical candidates ruled out.** Classical real light waves have circular polarization (two components a quarter period apart) and pass the 100/50/50 table as energy fractions, but divide every pulse's energy; killed by whole arrivals (Grangier, Roger & Aspect 1986). A rule with one real amplitude per exit: states are unit arrows (cos θ, sin θ); chance at H is cos²θ, at D is cos²(θ − 45°); a 50/50 bet at both needs cos²θ = ½ and cos²(θ − 45°) = ½, i.e. θ = 45° or 135° and then cos²(θ − 45°) = 1 or 0: impossible. So one real number per exit allows only two mutually fifty-fifty sorters; nature has three. (Hidden-pointer particle models are not what this kills; they are a separate story and are not excluded for a single two-outcome system.)

**Models.** *Model 1: two-component complex state vectors.* H = (1, 0), V = (0, 1), D = (1,1)/√2, A = (1,−1)/√2, R = (1, i)/√2, L = (1, −i)/√2. Chance of exit e for state s is |⟨e|s⟩|² with the conjugate on the left entry: show ⟨D|R⟩ = (1 + i)/2, |…|² = ½; ⟨H|R⟩ = 1/√2; ⟨R|L⟩ = (1 + (−i)(−i))/2 = 0, so R and L are perfectly distinguishable. The quarter-wave plate is the matrix diag(1, i): a quarter turn of one component's hand is literally multiplication by i, so the i is a physical operation (a delay of a quarter period). Picture: all states as points on a sphere (Poincaré/Bloch): real arrows form one great circle (H, D, V, A), and the third sorter's states R and L are the two points off it. Why this model: the full table from one rule; pros: the same two-vector describes spin-½ and qubits; cons: the square is a postulate. *Model 2: amplitudes for alternatives and the square of a sum.* |a + b + c|² = (a+b+c)(ā+b̄+c̄) = |a|² + |b|² + |c|² + (ab̄ + āb) + (ac̄ + āc) + (bc̄ + b̄c), each bracket 2Re(ab̄): only single and pair pieces. Pairs: |a+b|² = |a|² + |b|² + 2Re(ab̄). Hence pairs added minus singles added equals the triple: leftover zero, every step shown; for the p-th power the expansion has pieces involving all three, so the leftover is not zero: numbers at (1,1,−1): p = 1 gives 1 − 2 + 3 = 2; p = 2: 1 − 4 + 3 = 0; p = 3: 1 − 8 + 3 = −4; p = 4: 1 − 16 + 3 = −12; at (1,1,1): p = 1: 0 (the plain size passes where hands align), p = 2: 0, p = 3: 6, p = 4: 36. Sorkin's hierarchy: a rule of degree p has interference up to order p; the measured absence of third order is the signature of degree 2. Fine print, after the arithmetic and short: looped paths give a small nonzero leftover within the square rule itself (2014 calculation; 2016 measurement). *Model 3 (established mathematics): the structural theorems.* Gleason: with three or more directions, non-contextual chances that add to 1 over every orthogonal set are quadratic in the state, with certainty for the prepared direction giving the squared shadow; two-dimensional case needs Busch (2003). Counting for pairs of systems (Wootters 1990): with complex amplitudes a two-photon polarization state has 15 parameters, exactly what local measurements and their correlations supply (3 + 3 + 9); with one real number per amplitude it has 9 but only 8 are reachable locally (2 + 2 + 4), so one parameter of the pair would be invisible to the parts; show the counts. The 2021 network test (Renou et al.; two 2022 experiments) turns this into a measurable score (real bound 7.66, complex prediction up to 6√2 ≈ 8.49), with the caveats in the audit. *Interpretations* (contested, labelled): many-worlds with Zurek's swap step shown for equal amplitudes: in (|a⟩|A⟩ + |b⟩|B⟩)/√2, swapping a↔b on the system can be undone by swapping A↔B on the partner, so whatever the chances are they cannot depend on which is which: ½ each; unequal cases by fine-graining the partner (sketch); critics (Schlosshauer & Fine; Barnum et al.) object that fine-graining assumes what is proved. Copenhagen, pilot-wave, objective collapse short, with the Gisin sentence.

**How they relate.** Model 1 and model 2 are the same rule (shadows of a vector; sum of path hands); model 3 says the exponent and the pairing are what consistency across all sorters and all groupings allows; the three-sorter count forces the i for one system, the parameter count and the network test force it for pairs, the three-slit test and Gleason force the 2; the interpretations agree on every count and differ on meaning.

**Figures.** `c-three-fifty-fifty`: left, for real unit arrows at angle θ from 0° to 180°, the chances cos²θ (sorter H/V) and cos²(θ − 45°) (sorter D/A) against θ, with the ½ line: the first curve meets ½ at 45° and 135°, the second at 0°, 90° and 180°, never at the same θ; right, the Poincaré sphere with H/V, D/A and R/L as three axes, the real arrows drawn as the great circle through H, D, V, A, and R and L marked at the poles off that circle, with the two "fifty-fifty" great circles (one for each linear sorter) crossing exactly at R and L. Notice: no point on the real circle is on both fifty-fifty circles. `c-three-slit-leftover`: ideal far-screen three-slit amplitudes (equal slits of width w, centres at −d, 0, d with d = 4w; amplitude of slit j at screen position x proportional to sinc(πwx/λL)·exp(2πi d_j x/λL)); four panels of the leftover across ±3 stripe spacings for chance = |amplitude|^p with p = 1, 2, 3, 4, each panel labelled with its largest value: p = 2 flat zero; p = 1 non-negative, zero only at isolated points; p = 3 and p = 4 swinging. Caption names the independent-slit idealization.

**Opening sentence.** "There are three ways for a photon to be an even bet at a polarization sorter, and an arrow made of one real number per exit can supply only two of them; that is the shortest experimental route to the i, and a three-slit experiment is the shortest route to the 2."

**Suggested check.** Is there a polarization state that gives 50/50 at all three sorters? Use the sphere picture, and say what unpolarized light is.
