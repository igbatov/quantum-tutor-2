Quantum mechanics is the physics of light and matter at the scale of atoms and smaller (in principle, of everything), and two surprises sit at its heart. Even when you know everything the theory lets you know about something, it usually predicts only the odds of what a measurement will show, and measuring is not a passive glance: it typically leaves the thing in a new state. Three polarizing filters, like polarized sunglass lenses, show both; predict each step before reading on.

First, three facts:

  • Viewed as a wave, light vibrates sideways as it travels. The direction of that vibration is its polarization: picture an arrow across the beam. Ordinary light is a mix of all directions.
  • A perfect polarizing filter has a built-in direction: it passes light polarized along it and blocks light polarized at right angles to it.
  • Light also comes in photons, indivisible packets of energy. A photon gets through a filter whole or not at all.

1. Predict: Ordinary light meets a vertical filter. How much gets through?

What happens: Half, and every photon that made it is now vertically polarized.

2. Predict: Those vertical photons meet a second filter. What if it is vertical? Horizontal? Tilted 45°?

What happens: All pass a vertical filter, none pass a horizontal one, and half pass at 45°. The photons were prepared identically, yet no one can predict which will pass; quantum mechanics gives the 50% and nothing more. Whenever a photon's arrow and a filter are 45° apart, half pass.

3. Predict: Maybe a photon that just passed a 45° filter is secretly vertical or horizontal; we just don't know which. Then what fraction would pass a second 45° filter?

What happens: A hidden mix of vertical and horizontal photons would give half, since each kind is 45° from the filter. But all of them pass. So a 45° photon is not a vertical or horizontal one in disguise; 45° is a definite state of its own.

Still, its arrow can be built from a vertical piece and a horizontal piece, each about 0.71 long, as north-east is some north plus some east. That combination is a superposition: not "both at once", not "one of the two, unknown", but one definite direction described using two others. (A vertical arrow is equally a superposition of the two diagonals, 45° and 135°.) Where the compass picture breaks: a 45° photon never gets partly through a vertical filter. It passes whole or not at all, with a chance equal to its vertical piece squared: $0.71 \times 0.71$, about $0.5$. That piece, the arrow's shadow along the filter, is an example of an amplitude, the theory's basic ingredient: chances are amplitudes squared.

4. The puzzle. Predict: Crossed filters, vertical then horizontal, let nothing through. Slip a 45° filter between them. Does even less light get out, or more?

What happens: More! One-eighth of the original light gets out. Half passes the vertical filter, and half of that passes the 45° one. Those photons leave as 45° photons, with no memory of having been vertical, so the horizontal filter passes half of them: $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac18$.

If filters merely read labels the photons already carried, an extra filter could only block more light. So a filter, like any measurement, is not a passive reader: every photon that passes comes out along the filter's direction, whatever its past. (A classical wave of bright light also gives one-eighth. The quantum part is that it holds photon by photon: a photon can't be split, so the wave's fraction becomes each photon's chance. Whole photons with wave-like odds: that is wave–particle duality.)

"Vertical or horizontal?" and "which diagonal?" are incompatible questions: a photon with a sure answer to one is 50/50 on the other, however perfect the filters, because no state has sure answers to both. Position and momentum (roughly, where a particle is and how it moves) are another such pair: that is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, a fact about states, not clumsy instruments.

One more rule takes all this far beyond filters: when something can happen by two routes that nothing tells apart, the theory adds their amplitudes before squaring, and amplitudes, unlike chances, can be negative, so they can cancel. That is why electrons sent one at a time through two slits build up stripes on a screen, stripes that vanish when anything records which slit each took. Inside an atom, the nucleus's pull hems in an electron's wave of amplitudes (one for each place it might be found), and a hemmed-in wave has only certain stable patterns, each with its own energy. That is why each element glows in its own set of colors, and it underlies chemistry, lasers and computer chips. Everyday objects never show such stripes: air and light keep recording where they are, and their waves have unimaginably short wavelengths.

Nothing here needed a person: "observation" means any physical interaction that leaves a record, such as a filter absorbing a photon, not a mind. The predictions are among the best tested in science; what is "really" happening when a measurement gives one result, and what the quantum state (our arrow) is, are still debated among rival interpretations (the "measurement problem"), and recording by the surroundings doesn't settle it.

So quantum mechanics is about this: at the smallest scales it predicts odds, computed from amplitudes that can add or cancel, and measuring a thing generally changes it.
