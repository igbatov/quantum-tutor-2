# Exam answers (learner, after reading candidate B only)

**1.** Quantum mechanics is the physics of light and matter at the size of atoms and smaller. It describes things like electrons with waves that tell you the odds of finding them in each place. When one of those waves is hemmed in, like an electron held by the nucleus, it only has certain steady patterns, so the energy comes in fixed steps ("quanta"), the way a guitar string only plays certain notes. It's different from balls and planets in two ways. The theory only gives you odds, not exact outcomes. And the waves can add up or cancel each other out. Big objects follow the same rules too, but their energy steps are too tiny to notice, and their surroundings keep "recording" where they are, which washes out the cancelling.

**2.** I think detector B would start clicking some of the time. The explanation's two-slit example works the same way. Each electron lands as a single dot, but the dots form stripes because the waves from the two slits add up in some places and cancel in others. So with both routes open, I'm guessing the waves from the two routes cancel at B, which is why B never clicks, and add up at A. If you block one route, only one wave gets through, so there's nothing for it to cancel against at B. Then B can click. Some photons will also just get stopped by the block. I can't tell from what I read exactly how the photons that get through would split between A and B. I'd also guess that if you left both routes open but recorded which route each photon took, the cancelling would go away and B would click as well, since the explanation says recording which slit makes the stripes vanish. The explanation used electrons, not photons, but it said the same wave rules apply to light.

**3.** I disagree. The explanation said the uncertainty principle is "a fact about waves, not about clumsy measuring tools." The electron is described by a wave. If you squeeze the wave into a small space to pin down where the electron is, it has to wiggle more tightly, so its motion (momentum) gets less sharply defined. So better instruments wouldn't help. You can't have both a sharply pinned-down position and a sharply defined motion at once. I'm not completely sure why "wiggling tightly" means the motion is spread out rather than just faster. The explanation stated it but didn't really show that step.

**4.** No. It gives the odds for each detector. The explanation said what really happens between measurements, and what the wave itself actually is, are still argued about between rival "interpretations." So it's not settled. Physicists agree on the predictions, which have been tested very precisely, but not on the story of what the photon is "really doing" when nobody is checking.
