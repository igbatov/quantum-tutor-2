# Exam answers (after reading candidate C only)

**1.** Quantum mechanics is the physics of light and matter at the scale of atoms and smaller. Everyday physics would tell you exactly what a ball or planet will do, but quantum mechanics usually only gives you the odds: two photons prepared exactly the same way can still do different things at a filter, and nobody can say which one will get through. The other big difference is that measuring something isn't just looking at it. It changes the thing. A photon that passes a filter comes out lined up with that filter, whatever it was before.

**2.** I think detector B will start clicking some of the time once one route is blocked. The explanation said that when something can happen by two routes and nothing tells them apart, you add the amplitudes before squaring. Amplitudes can be negative, so they can cancel. My guess is that with both routes open, the amplitudes for "go to B" from the two routes cancel out exactly, which is why B never clicks. Blocking one route leaves only one way through, so there's nothing left to cancel with. The chance of reaching B is then that single route's amplitude squared, which isn't zero. It's like the two-slit stripes: they vanish when you know which slit was used. Some photons will also just be stopped by the block, so fewer reach either detector overall. I can't tell from what I read exactly how often B clicks. I'd guess something like half of the photons that get through, but I'm not sure.

**3.** I don't agree. The explanation said uncertainty is "a fact about states, not clumsy instruments." Even with perfect filters, a photon that's surely vertical is 50/50 on which diagonal it is, because no state has sure answers to both questions. Position and momentum are a pair like that, so no state has an exact position and an exact speed at the same time, however gentle the instrument is. It's true that measuring changes things (the filter resets the photon), but that's not the reason you can't know both. There just isn't a state with both answers to find out. I can't tell from what I read exactly how this works for position and momentum, beyond it being "another such pair."

**4.** No. Quantum mechanics gives the odds for each detector, and those predictions are very well tested. But what is "really" happening when nobody checks the route, and what the quantum state actually is, are still argued over between rival interpretations. That's called the measurement problem, and it isn't settled. The one thing I'd add is that "checking" doesn't need a person. Any interaction that leaves a record counts, and if something did record the route, the cancellation would go away.
