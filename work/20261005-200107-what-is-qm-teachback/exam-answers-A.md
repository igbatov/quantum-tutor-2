# Exam answers (after reading candidate A only)

**1.** Quantum mechanics is the physics of light and matter, and you notice it most with really tiny things like atoms and electrons. What's different is that it usually can't tell you exactly what will happen, only the odds of each result. And it works those odds out from things called amplitudes, which act like waves and can add up or cancel each other. So something like an electron always shows up whole, as one particle in one spot, but where it tends to land follows a striped wave pattern. A ball or a planet doesn't do that: you can just say where it is and where it's going. (Also, inside atoms this wave stuff means only certain fixed energies are allowed, and that's where the "quantum" name comes from.)

**2.** I think detector B will start clicking some of the time once one route is blocked. My reasoning: with both routes open and nothing recording which way the photon went, you add the amplitudes for the two routes. B never clicking must mean the two amplitudes cancel exactly at B, like the dark stripe in the two-slit experiment. If you block one route there's only one way left, so there's nothing for it to cancel against, and some photons should reach B. It's the same as covering one slit, where a dark spot starts getting hits. Opening a second route can make a result *less* likely, so taking one away can make it more likely.
Also, the photons that hit the block are stopped, so fewer photons reach either detector overall. How the ones that get through split between A and B (half and half, or something else) I can't tell from what I read.

**3.** I disagree. The explanation said the uncertainty principle isn't caused by clumsy instruments. It's built into the waves. To make a wave squeezed into a small spot (so position is sharp), you have to combine lots of different wavelengths, and different wavelengths mean different momenta. So an electron with a sharply defined position has to have a spread of momenta, and since momentum is mass times velocity, that means a spread of speeds too. Better engineering wouldn't fix that, because it isn't about bumping the electron. (It's a bit like the two-slit part, where the text said even records too gentle to knock the electron off course still wipe out the stripes, so "bumping" isn't the real explanation there either.) I'm less sure exactly how big the trade-off is. The text didn't give a number or formula, so I can't tell how precisely you could know both.

**4.** No. Quantum mechanics gives the odds very precisely (the text said its predictions are among the most precisely tested in science), but it doesn't settle what the photon is "really doing" when nobody checks. Saying "it goes both ways at once" claims more than the theory does. Whenever you do check, you find it on one route, whole. What it does unchecked is exactly what the different interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree about, so no, it isn't settled. There's also the related open question of why each particle ends up at one particular spot, which is called the "measurement problem." The text didn't say what the different interpretations actually claim, so I can't tell you which ones exist or what they say.
