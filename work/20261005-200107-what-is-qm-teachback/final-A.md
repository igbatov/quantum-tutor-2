Quantum mechanics is the physics of light and matter; its effects are most obvious at the scale of atoms and below. Its central idea is this: the theory usually predicts only the odds of each possible result, and it computes those odds from wave-like quantities, called amplitudes, that can add up or cancel out. One real experiment shows what that means: fire electrons, one at a time, at a wall with two narrow slits.

Behind the wall, a screen flashes wherever an electron lands. What would you expect? If electrons were tiny balls, each would pass through one slit or the other, so with both slits open you would just get the hits from one slit plus the hits from the other: a smooth spread, no stripes. If each electron were a ripple, like a wave on a pond, it would wash over the whole screen at once, faintly, rather than land as one flash.

What you see fits neither picture. Each electron lands whole, as a single dot, at a spot nobody can predict. But as thousands of dots pile up, they form bright stripes, where many land, separated by dark stripes, where almost none do. The theory predicts this pattern precisely, but not where the next dot will land.

Now the decisive detail. Pick a spot in the middle of a dark stripe near the center. With only one slit open, electrons land there often. Open the second slit as well and, in an ideal setup, none land there at all. A second way through made arriving *less* likely, which is impossible if the chances for the two routes simply add.

Quantum mechanics uses a different rule, the heart of the whole theory. For each route to a spot, the theory gives an amplitude, a quantity that behaves like a wave: it has a size, and along the route it runs through up-and-down cycles, one for every wavelength of distance. (An electron's wavelength is a tiny length that shrinks as its momentum, its mass times its velocity, grows.) For routes that nothing tells apart, **you add the amplitudes, and the chance of landing at that spot is the size of the total, squared.**

At the center of the screen both routes are equally long, so their amplitudes arrive in step and reinforce: a bright stripe. A little to one side, one route is half a wavelength longer, so they arrive out of step and cancel: a dark stripe. A whole wavelength longer: bright again. When an electron's state carries amplitudes for several possibilities like this, it is called a *superposition*. Unlike a pond wave, though, an amplitude is not a wave of any material, and you never detect part of an electron.

Next, try to learn which slit each electron uses. If anything records the route (even a single particle of light that bounces off the electron and carries that information away), the stripes vanish: you get just the sum of what each slit gives alone. **Routes that can be told apart add their chances, not their amplitudes**, so nothing cancels. And it isn't just that the detector knocks the electron off course: records far too gentle for that erase the stripes too. What matters is that a record exists, not that a person reads it.

So tiny things arrive whole, like particles, while the amplitudes that set their odds spread and interfere like waves; the older name for this is wave–particle duality.

The same rules reach far beyond slits. Inside an atom, the electron has an amplitude for each place it could be found, and the nucleus's pull hems this wave of amplitudes in, so only certain steady patterns fit, each with one fixed energy. These fixed amounts of energy, or "quanta", gave the theory its name. When the electron drops to a lower pattern, the atom emits light whose color is fixed by the energy difference, so each element glows in its own set of sharp colors. There is a lowest pattern with nothing below it, so an atom can't keep shedding energy: atoms are stable. These patterns underlie chemistry, lasers, LEDs and computer chips. The same wave picture explains the uncertainty principle: a wave squeezed into a small region must combine many wavelengths, which cancel everywhere else, and different wavelengths mean different momenta. So an electron with a sharply defined position has a spread of momenta: a trade-off built into the waves, not caused by clumsy instruments.

Stripes have also been seen with atoms and, in similar experiments, with molecules of 60 carbon atoms and larger. Everyday objects don't show them: air and light constantly record where they are, and their huge momentum makes their wavelengths far smaller than an atomic nucleus.

Two popular pictures to drop. "Observation" needs no mind: in quantum mechanics it means any physical interaction that leaves a record. And "it goes through both slits" or "it's in two places at once" claims more than the theory does: check, and you always find one whole electron at one slit; what it does unchecked is exactly what interpretations of quantum mechanics disagree about. The predictions are settled: they are among the most precisely tested in science. Still argued about is why each electron ends up at one particular spot (the "measurement problem"), which constant recording by the surroundings does not by itself explain.

In one line: in quantum mechanics, things arrive as whole particles, but the odds of every result come from amplitudes that add or cancel like waves, and hemmed-in waves allow only certain energies.
