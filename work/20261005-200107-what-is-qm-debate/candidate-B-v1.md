Quantum mechanics is the theory of how matter and light behave at the scale of atoms (and, as far as we know, at every scale), and the quickest way to see what it's about is a question classical (pre-quantum) physics gets spectacularly wrong: why do atoms exist at all? Its answer is that an electron is described not as a tiny ball on a track but by a wave of possibilities, and a wave trapped around a nucleus can only take certain shapes, so the atom can only have certain energies, including a lowest one it can never fall below.

**What classical physics predicts.** Picture the atom most of us met at school: a tiny, heavy nucleus with an electron circling it like a planet. Add a law that every radio transmitter confirms: an electric charge that changes speed or direction gives off electromagnetic waves (light and radio waves are both kinds), losing energy. A circling electron changes direction constantly, so it should radiate nonstop and spiral into the nucleus within about a hundred-billionth of a second. Nothing in this picture picks out particular colours, so glowing hydrogen should give off a smooth smear of them, and since orbits can be any size, no two hydrogen atoms would need to match.

Three facts flatly contradict this:

- Atoms are stable: atoms in the oldest rocks have lasted billions of years.
- All hydrogen atoms are identical: same size, same colours.
- Glowing hydrogen shines in a few sharp colours, not a smear. In visible light there are just four: red, blue-green, blue and violet (wavelengths 656, 486, 434 and 410 nanometres). Neon's own sharp colours are what make neon signs glow red-orange.

**Step 1: a wave of possibilities.** Quantum mechanics drops the planet picture and describes the electron by a wave spread around the nucleus (its technical name is the wavefunction). The wave's strength at each place sets the chance of finding the electron there, yet any detection finds one whole electron at one spot. The fuzzy "clouds" in pictures of atoms are maps of those chances.

**Step 2: a trapped wave can only take certain shapes.** Think of a guitar string: pinned at both ends, its steady vibration patterns must fit a whole number of half-waves along it, so it plays only certain notes. Unlike the string, the electron's wave isn't a vibration of any material, and the atom's patterns are three-dimensional, held in by the nucleus's electric pull, not by pinned ends. But the key result carries over.

Each allowed pattern has a definite energy, so the atom has a ladder of allowed energies with gaps between the rungs. When the electron drops from a higher rung to a lower one, the energy difference leaves as a single packet of light, a photon. A photon's energy $E$ and its frequency $f$ (how many times per second its wave oscillates, which your eye sees as colour) are linked by Planck's constant $h$, a tiny fixed number:

$$E = hf.$$

In words: a fixed energy gap means a fixed frequency, so one sharp colour, and bigger drops give higher frequencies, towards blue and violet. Every hydrogen atom has the same ladder, hence the same colours.

**Step 3: there is a bottom rung, with nothing below it.** On it the electron has nowhere lower to drop, so it can't radiate energy away, and the atom can't collapse. Why must there be a bottom? Squeezing the electron's wave closer to the nucleus forces it into shorter wavelengths, and in quantum mechanics a shorter wavelength means more momentum, so more energy of motion. The nucleus's pull draws the electron in; the cost of squeezing pushes back. Their balance sets the size of every hydrogen atom: roughly a ten-billionth of a metre across. This is the uncertainty principle at work, and notice that no measurement is involved: it's a property of waves, not of clumsy measuring.

**Zooming out**, quantum mechanics is about a handful of big ideas:

- **Quantization:** anything confined, from atoms to electrons trapped in tiny crystals, has a ladder of allowed energies.
- **Amplitudes and probability:** the wave is made of numbers called amplitudes, and unlike chances, amplitudes can cancel. When something can happen in several ways and nothing records which way it went, quantum mechanics adds the amplitudes for those ways, not their chances, and only then turns the total into a chance by squaring its size. That's why electrons sent one by one through two slits build up bright and dark stripes. The theory predicts the chances exactly, but not where any single electron will land.
- **Uncertainty:** a wave can't be both tightly located and of a single wavelength, so position and momentum are never both sharp.
- **Entanglement:** particles can share one joint wave, linking their measurement results in ways no plan agreed in advance could produce, yet never in a way that sends a message.

These rules explain chemistry and make transistors, lasers and LEDs possible; in big, warm objects their strange effects wash out into everyday behaviour. Their predictions are among the best-tested in science. What is still debated is what the wave "really is" (a physical thing, a summary of information, or something else) and how a measurement ends up with one definite result. Those are questions of interpretation, and the main interpretations make the same predictions.
