# Critique of candidate A ("One electron at a time")

Written by the author of candidate B ("The atom that shouldn't exist"). The learner is at conceptual level, this is their first question, and the profile is empty.

## Bottom line

A is accurate almost line by line. It also teaches the core of quantum mechanics better than B does: amplitudes add, and only then are they squared into chances.

It will let this learner down in two places:
- **A figure legend** states as fact something the text carefully leaves open.
- **The closing map** asserts, with no reason given, the results that tie quantum mechanics to ordinary matter: discrete energies, a lowest level, stable atoms and sharp colours. The opening promises that "the rule" explains these, but the answer never shows how.

There are smaller gaps too. "Wavelength" and "photon" arrive undefined, and the measurement problem is never named.

All of this is fixable. The fixes add roughly 80 words, and about half can be recovered by trimming. That matters because A already runs to about 950 words against the plan's 600–800 (B runs over too).

## Problems, in priority order

**1. [major] A figure legend overclaims about paths.**
- In `a-one-vs-two` the dotted curve is labelled "if each electron took one slit (chances simply add)".
- The text gets this exactly right: "it can't be that each electron just takes one slit *while the other slit makes no difference to it*." That qualifier is what makes the sentence true. In pilot-wave (de Broglie–Bohm) theory, every electron does pass through one slit, and the bands still form because the guiding wave passes through both.
- The legend drops the qualifier. The figure then tells the learner that the experiment proves electrons don't take one slit. That contradicts A's own closing paragraph, which says "whether the electron takes a definite path between source and screen" is still debated.
- Learners remember figures longer than sentences.

*Fix:* label the curve "if the two slits' chances simply added" (or "... as when a detector records the slit"). Keep the final caption to that wording.

**2. [major, teaching] The everyday payoff is promised, then only asserted.** The opening says the rule "is behind ... atoms glowing in sharp colours". The quantization bullet never shows how, and it gives no reason for any of its three claims.

- **Why only certain patterns?** "Amplitudes confined to a small space ... have only certain steady patterns" is a bare statement. There is no "the wave has to fit its space" and no standing-wave image. The learner can repeat the claim but can't reason with it; they couldn't predict what shrinking the space would do.
- **Why a floor, and why it matters.** "including a lowest one it can't fall below (which is why atoms are stable)" is the biggest everyday payoff in the whole answer, and it sits in a parenthesis.
  - The learner is never told that atoms *should* be unstable. Pre-quantum physics has an orbiting electron radiate and spiral in within about a hundred-billionth of a second. Without that contrast, the claim lands as trivia.
  - The floor itself is never explained, although A's own uncertainty bullet holds the ingredients.
- **Why one colour?** "each drop from one value to a lower one gives off light of one sharp colour" gives no link from a fixed energy to a fixed colour.
  - Light coming in packets (photons, the "quanta" behind the theory's name) never appears.
  - "Photons" shows up once, undefined, in a list.

*Fix (about +40 words net):*
- Move the uncertainty bullet above quantization.
- Replace the second sentence of the quantization bullet with something like: "Squeezing an electron's wave closer to the nucleus forces shorter wavelengths, which means more momentum and more energy of motion; that cost balances the nucleus's pull at a lowest level. Pre-quantum physics predicted the electron would spiral in within a hundred-billionth of a second; this floor is why it doesn't. Each drop to a lower level releases one packet of light, a photon, and the packet's energy sets its colour."
- Optionally, for the first point, borrow one bounded clause: "a confined wave must fit its space, as a guitar string can only play certain notes", with its limit stated.

*Declared bias:* this is my candidate's whole route, so weigh this item accordingly. Still, the standard here is the skill's "explain why, not just that", and A's own opening makes the promise.

**3. [minor] "An electron's wavelength" arrives undefined.** The uncertainty bullet depends on it, but so far the text has only said that the arrow "turns like a clock hand as the route gets longer".

*Fix:* add one clause to the arrow paragraph: "the distance over which it makes one full turn is the electron's wavelength, and the faster the electron, the shorter that is." This also anchors the squeezing argument in item 2.

**4. [minor] The text never says why "one at a time" matters.** A first-timer's natural way out is "maybe the electrons jostle each other into bands". Sending them one at a time is what rules that out, but A never says so.

*Fix:* add "...so the bands can't come from electrons pushing on each other." Also, "only one is ever in flight" is slightly too absolute; "one at a time" is exact.

**5. [minor] "So the chance is 4."** The paragraph sets up "1 unit of chance" and then drops the unit. "The chance is 4" then reads as a probability above 1, which will snag exactly the careful reader A is courting.

*Fix:* write "4 units of chance".

**6. [minor] The measurement problem is never named, and one bullet quietly picks an interpretation.**
- "the amplitudes change in a fully predictable way until an outcome is recorded" states the textbook collapse account as fact.
- Yet the close offers "branches of a larger reality", a view in which the amplitudes never stop changing predictably.
- The chair sentence correctly explains why big things show no bands. It does not explain why one definite outcome occurs. With that open question unnamed, a learner may conclude that "records" settle everything.

*Fix:*
- Rewrite the probability bullet as: "The theory predicts chances exactly, and the amplitudes change over time in a fully predictable way. Only which outcome gets recorded is left to chance, and nobody has found a way to predict it better."
- Add to the "Still debated" list: "...and how a recording ends up with one definite outcome. The main interpretations make the same predictions."

**7. [minor] "each with its own energy".** In hydrogen several patterns share each energy: four at the second level, ignoring spin.

*Fix:* "each with a definite energy" is correct and costs nothing.

**8. [minor] The key step has no picture, and the simulated figure isn't labelled as simulated.** Both figures show the screen. Neither shows the arrows, yet "Chain the arrows tip to tail" is the step a first-timer is most likely to lose.

*Fix (within the two-figure limit):*
- Add two small insets to `a-one-vs-two`:
  - at x = 0, two aligned unit arrows (total 2, so 4 units);
  - at x = 0.5, two opposite arrows (total 0).
- The text calls this "a real experiment", so the `a-dot-buildup` caption should say the dots are simulated.

**9. [optional]**
- **"even 60-atom molecules".** This is true (buckyballs, 1999), but it reads as the current frontier. Interference has since been shown with molecules of roughly 2,000 atoms (2019). Update it, or just say "large molecules".
- **"nature doesn't add the chances ... It adds quantities called amplitudes".** This casts amplitudes as nature's own bookkeeping, while the close lists "a tool for tracking information" as a live option. "Quantum mechanics says: don't add the chances, add amplitudes" is neutral and costs nothing.
- **Late definition of "amplitudes".** The word appears about 250 words before the arrow paragraph makes it concrete. A pointer such as "(you'll meet them as arrows in a moment)" keeps a first-timer from bouncing off the abstraction.

*Budget:* these trims save about 35 words and offset part of the additions:
- merge the "Predict" and "Common sense" paragraphs;
- fold "Here is the rule at work in a real experiment" into the next sentence;
- cut the water-wave paragraph to two sentences.

## What A does better than B (conceded)

1. **The heart of the subject.** A makes the idea that unlocks interference, tunnelling and quantum computing emerge from data. It then works the idea with numbers: 1 + 1 gives 4, 1 − 1 gives 0, and the average is 2, so interference only moves probability around. B states the same rule in one abstract bullet ("only then turns the total into a chance by squaring its size") and never shows it at work. B also gives the double slit only one sentence. For "what is quantum mechanics about", this is A's decisive advantage.
2. **Measurement.** A properly defuses the myth that a conscious observer is needed. Anything that records which slit counts, nobody has to read the record, and the bands vanish. B never says what a measurement is, so the pop-science misconception a first-timer most likely brings goes unaddressed.
3. **Why everyday things look classical.** A gives a mechanism: "why never with a chair? Largely because big, warm things constantly leave records". B's "their strange effects wash out" is a bare assertion.
4. **"Both slits at once."** A handles this myth precisely and without taking sides between interpretations. B doesn't touch it.
5. **Engagement.** The predict moment makes the learner commit to a guess before the surprise. B has nothing like it before the check question.
6. **Entanglement wording.** A's "no answers agreed in advance and carried by each particle" states exactly what Bell's theorem rules out: local, pre-agreed answers. B's "no plan agreed in advance" is looser and can be read as ruling out every hidden-variable theory. B should adopt A's wording.
7. **Probability.** A's bullet is more complete. It says the amplitudes evolve predictably, only single outcomes are left to chance, and nobody has found a better way to predict them.
8. **Figure and check question.**
   - `a-dot-buildup` shows the one fact no wave analogy can carry: single, whole detections building a statistical pattern.
   - The check question tests the core rule with a genuine transfer step (unequal arrows). It can be answered from the text alone, and A needs no equation at all.

## Shared weaknesses (not a reason to prefer either)

- Both entanglement bullets are abstract and give no example.
- Neither says that the correlations only show up when the two sets of results are compared.
- Both run over the length target.

**Overall:** as they stand, A would probably leave this learner with the more transferable core idea, and B with a better sense of why quantum mechanics matters to the world they live in. With items 1 and 2 fixed, A would cover most of what B does better while keeping its own advantages.
