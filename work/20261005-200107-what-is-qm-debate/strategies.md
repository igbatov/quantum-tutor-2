# Strategies: "Describe what quantum mechanics is about"

**Learner.** Conceptual level by default. The profile is empty because this is the first question of the session, so there are no recorded misconceptions, interests or earlier style choices to build on. **Mode:** new-question, a broad overview. **Setup:** debate, two strategies.

**What any good answer has to deliver here:**
1. The honest core in the first two sentences. Quantum mechanics is the theory of matter and light at the scale of atoms (and, as far as we know, of everything; its strangeness is just hidden at everyday scales). It predicts probabilities, which it computes from amplitudes that can add or cancel, and confined systems get discrete energies.
2. One real phenomenon that classical physics can't explain, worked through concretely.
3. A short map of the big ideas (amplitudes and interference, probability, quantization, uncertainty, entanglement) and why they matter.
4. A clear line between established physics and interpretation.

Length: about 600–800 words, at most one equation, no notation beyond it, no headings.

## Lenses considered

1. **Mechanism-first.** State the rules up front. A system is described by an amplitude for each possible outcome. When an outcome can come about in several ways that leave no record, their amplitudes add and can cancel. The probability is the squared size of the total. Then show interference, discrete energies and uncertainty following from these rules, using a photon passing two half-silvered mirrors (Mach–Zehnder) as the example. **Fit 3/5.** This is the most honest compression of the theory. But rules handed over before any phenomenon feel arbitrary to a first-time conceptual learner, and the interferometer needs its own setup. *Not chosen as a route.* Both chosen strategies end at these rules.

2. **Experiment-first.** Electrons are fired one at a time at two slits. Each lands as a single dot at an unpredictable spot, yet thousands of dots build bright and dark bands, and at a dark band opening the second slit *lowers* the number of arrivals. The data force the amplitude rule out rather than the text announcing it. **Fit 5/5.** It is a real experiment, repeated many times, with no prerequisites. It is the canonical "heart of quantum mechanics". It also lets the explanation defuse the two most common pop-science pictures ("in two places at once", "a conscious observer changes reality") at exactly the point where they come up. **Chosen (Strategy 1).**

3. **Geometric.** Treat photon polarization as an arrow in a plane. A polarizing filter asks "are you along my axis?" and passes the photon with probability equal to the squared cosine of the angle. The example is the three-filter trick with polarized sunglasses. **Fit 3/5.** It is tangible and the cleanest two-state system. But for an overview it shrinks quantum mechanics to a single yes/no property. The three-filter effect also has a classical wave explanation, so the genuinely quantum part (indivisible photons, each passing at random) is hard to make convincing at this level. Its arrows would also duplicate Strategy 1's representation. *Rejected for now.* It would be a strong choice for a later follow-up on measurement or superposition.

4. **Classical-contrast.** Start from the picture most people carry from school, an electron circling a nucleus like a planet, and add a well-tested classical law: a charge that changes speed or direction radiates and loses energy. Classical physics then predicts that the atom collapses in about $10^{-11}$ s, glowing in a smear of all colours on the way, and that atoms have no fixed size. In reality atoms are stable and identical, and they glow in sharp colours specific to each element. Quantum mechanics' fix explains all three: a confined wave of possibility can only take certain shapes. **Fit 4/5.** It grounds quantum mechanics in why the everyday world exists (solid matter, the colours of neon signs, chemistry). It also replaces the planetary-atom picture a conceptual learner most likely holds. The risk is density (radiation law, spectra, energy ladder, $E = hf$), so the classical setup must stay short. **Chosen (Strategy 2).**

5. **Analogy-led.** Carry the whole answer on one bounded analogy: guitar-string harmonics for discrete energies, or noise-cancelling headphones for amplitudes cancelling. **Fit 3/5.** Each analogy is honest about one feature. But no wave analogy can show the defining quantum fact: every detection finds one whole particle, at one place, at random. An answer built on one analogy therefore makes quantum mechanics look like "just waves". *Rejected as a route.* The guitar string is used inside Strategy 2 and water waves inside Strategy 1, each with its limits stated on the spot.

6. **Worked-example-first.** Open with the arrow arithmetic. Take two routes that each give 1 unit of chance on their own: together they give 4 when their arrows line up and 0 when the arrows point opposite ways, averaging 2. **Fit 3/5.** The arithmetic is gentle, but opening a "what is it about" answer with a calculation gives the learner no reason to care yet. *Rejected as a route.* It becomes the key step inside Strategy 1.

7. **Historical.** Tell the story in order: Planck's light packets (1900), Einstein's photoelectric effect (1905), Bohr's atom (1913), de Broglie's matter waves (1924), then Heisenberg, Schrödinger and Born (1925–26). **Fit 2/5.** It is a good story, but it leads with history instead of the core idea and tends to become a list of names and dates. It also passes through Bohr's orbits, which the learner would then have to unlearn. *Rejected.* Strategy 2 borrows only the puzzle that drove the history (why are atoms stable?), without the chronology.

8. **Question-led.** Follow Feynman's sequence: predict the pattern for bullets through two slits, then for water waves, then for electrons, revealing each answer in turn. **Fit 4/5.** It is engaging, but a written answer can't wait for the learner's reply. With the same example it would also produce nearly the same text as Strategy 1, so the two count as one strategy. *Merged:* Strategy 1 keeps a single "predict before you read on" moment.

9. **Formal derivation.** Hilbert space, operators and the Schrödinger equation. **Fit 1/5.** The learner is at conceptual level, this is the first question, and they haven't signalled any maths. *Rejected.*

10. **Misconception-first.** Open by naming the pop-science pictures (in two places at once, consciousness creates reality, "everything is random") and replace each one. **Fit 3/5.** Nothing suggests this learner holds these pictures, so leading with them risks talking down. Myth-busting also delays the positive picture the question asks for. *Rejected as a route.* Each chosen strategy instead defuses likely wrong pictures where they naturally come up. Strategy 1 handles "through both slits" and "observer = a person". Strategy 2 handles "electron = tiny planet" and "uncertainty = clumsy measurement".

## Choice

| | Strategy 1: One electron at a time | Strategy 2: The atom that shouldn't exist |
|---|---|---|
| Route | Experiment-first: start from data and let the rule emerge | Classical-contrast: a confident classical prediction, its failure, the quantum fix |
| Example | Single electrons at two slits | The hydrogen atom: why it's stable, and its sharp colours |
| Representation | Dots on a screen; amplitudes as arrows added tip to tail | Energy ladder; standing waves (bounded guitar-string analogy) |
| Face of QM it leads with | Probability, interference, what "measurement" means | Quantization, the stability of matter, why QM matters |
| Wrong picture it defuses | "Goes through both slits", "observer = a conscious person" | "Electron = tiny planet on an orbit", "uncertainty = clumsy measurement" |

These are the two highest-fit lenses, and they share almost nothing: route, example, representation and emphasis all differ. Both end with the same short map of the big ideas, so either one alone answers the question.

Expected weak spots, for the critique round:
- **Strategy 1** can read like a clever puzzle with no stake in the everyday world. Its closing map must tie the rule to atoms and technology.
- **Strategy 2** can run long and leave probability and interference as an afterthought. Its closing map must state the amplitude rule plainly.

In revision, borrow sentences, not routes.

**Accuracy rules for both drafts:**
- Quantum mechanics is not "only for small things". As far as we know it describes everything; its effects are hidden for large, warm objects.
- Say an electron is "described by" a wave. Every detection finds one whole particle at one place, and the wave's intensity gives the probability.
- "Observation" or "measurement" means any physical record of the outcome, never a conscious mind.
- Don't say "everything is random". The theory predicts probabilities exactly, and the amplitudes themselves change in a fully predictable way. Only individual outcomes are a matter of chance, and no one has found a way to predict them better.
- Keep interpretations labelled as interpretations. What happens between source and screen, or what the wave "really is", is debated; the predictions are not.
- Don't give precision records or dates you aren't sure of. "Tested to extraordinary precision" is enough.

### Strategy 1: One electron at a time

**Lens:** experiment-first, with one "predict before you read on" moment borrowed from the question-led lens.

**Plan.** Open with the rule (see the opening sentence below). Add one sentence saying this single rule is behind wave patterns made by particles, the sharp colours of atoms, and the fact that the theory predicts chances rather than certainties. Then go straight to the experiment and let the rule fall out of it.
- **Setup, in plain words.** A source sends electrons so slowly that only one is in the apparatus at a time. They head for a barrier with two narrow slits, and behind it a screen flashes where each electron lands.
- **The data.** Every electron arrives as one dot at one spot, never a smear and never half an electron. Where the next dot lands is unpredictable, but thousands of dots build bright and dark bands (figure 1).
- **Predict before you read on.** With one slit open you get a single broad patch. If you open the second slit too, what happens at a given spot? Common sense says more ways in means more hits. In fact, at a dark band, opening the second slit drops the hits almost to zero (figure 2).
- **Careful conclusion.** Whatever is going on, it can't be that each electron takes one slit and the other slit makes no difference, because then the chances would simply add.
- **The rule, in arrow language.** Each route to a spot comes with an arrow, its amplitude. The arrow turns like a clock hand as the route gets longer, so two routes of different lengths can arrive pointing different ways. Add the arrows tip to tail; the chance is the length of the total, squared.
- **Work the numbers.** Use two arrows of length 1 (each slit alone gives 1 unit of chance). Lined up, the total is 2 and the chance is 4, twice the 2 you'd get by adding chances. Pointing opposite ways, the total is 0 and the spot stays dark. Averaged over bright and dark bands the chance comes back to 2, so interference moves probability around rather than creating or destroying it.
- **One bounded analogy.** Water waves through two gaps make the same bands. State the limit at once: a water wave is spread-out stuff that would nudge every point of the screen at once, while each electron registers whole, at one place. The wave behaviour lives in the chances.
- **The which-path twist.** Suppose something leaves a record of which slit an electron used: a detector at the slits, or a photon bouncing off the electron in a way that reveals the slit. Then the routes become distinguishable, you add chances instead of arrows, and the bands vanish. "Observation" means a physical record, not a conscious person.
- **Zoom out: what quantum mechanics is about.** A five-line map, each line tied back to the arrow rule:
  - amplitudes and interference (seen with photons, atoms and even large molecules too);
  - predictions are probabilities, exact as probabilities, while individual outcomes are, as far as anyone has found, unpredictable;
  - confined amplitude-waves fit only certain patterns, which gives atoms their energy ladders and sharp colours;
  - a wave can't be both sharply located and have a single wavelength, which is the uncertainty principle;
  - entangled particles share one set of amplitudes, giving correlations that no pre-arranged answers could produce, yet no way to send messages.
- **Reach.** One line: chemistry, transistors, lasers, MRI.
- **Close.** One or two sentences: these rules and their predictions are among the best-tested in science, but what the arrows "really are" (a physical wave, a record of information, branches of a larger reality) is still debated.

**Example.** Electrons sent one at a time through two slits, building a banded pattern dot by dot. Worked numbers: arrows of 1 + 1 give a chance of 4 (bright), 1 − 1 gives 0 (dark), and the average is 2.

**Figure requests (two):**
- `a-dot-buildup`, placed right after the data are described. Four panels side by side, each a dark rectangle standing for the detector screen, showing simulated arrival dots for 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 electrons. Draw the horizontal position $x$ at random from $P(x) \propto \cos^2(\pi x)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2(x/5)$, with $\mathrm{sinc}(u) = \sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)$, over $-6 \le x \le 6$; $x$ is measured in fringe spacings. Draw the vertical position uniformly at random. Use small white dots and put the electron count in each panel title; no axis ticks needed. What to notice: the first few dots look scattered at random, bands emerge as the count grows, and every electron arrived as a single dot.
- `a-one-vs-two`, placed at the "predict" moment. A line plot of relative chance of arrival against position $x$ (same units, $-6$ to $6$), with three curves:
  - thin dashed: one slit open (either one), $S(x) = \mathrm{sinc}^2(x/5)$;
  - dotted: "if each electron took one slit and chances simply added", $2S(x)$;
  - solid: "both slits open, what is actually seen", $4S(x)\cos^2(\pi x)$.

  Mark the dark band at $x = 0.5$ with a vertical line labelled "one slit open: about 1; both open: 0". Use a plain-words legend. What to notice: at a dark band, opening the second slit lowers the chance to zero; at a bright band the chance reaches 4, twice the dotted curve; on average the solid curve follows the dotted one.

**Opening sentence.** "Quantum mechanics is the physics of electrons, atoms and light, and at its heart is one strange rule: when something can happen in more than one way and nothing records which way it took, nature doesn't add up the chances of each way; it adds up quantities called amplitudes, which can reinforce or cancel each other, and only then turns the total into a chance."

**Watch out for:**
- Don't state as fact that the electron "goes through both slits" or "is in two places at once". Don't flatly say it "doesn't go through one slit" either, because in some interpretations it does, guided by a wave. Say that the theory assigns no path, only amplitudes for both routes, and that a picture where each electron takes one slit and the other slit makes no difference can't produce the bands.
- For which-path, say "anything that leaves a record of which slit", not "any interaction at all". A disturbance that doesn't reveal the slit leaves the bands in place.
- The chances in the arrow example are relative ("units of chance"). Keep the 4 / 0 / average 2 bookkeeping consistent.
- Real dark bands are *nearly* dark; ideal ones are fully dark.
- If you cite the experiment, say only this: it has been done with electrons one at a time (including through two real slits), and the same bands appear for photons, neutrons, atoms and molecules as large as 60-atom buckyballs. Give no other names or dates unless certain.

**Check-yourself idea.** "If one slit were made narrower, so fewer electrons get through it than through the other, would the dark bands still be completely dark? (Picture adding a long arrow and a short arrow.)" Answer: no. Unequal arrows only partly cancel, so the dark bands turn dim grey rather than black.

### Strategy 2: The atom that shouldn't exist

**Lens:** classical-contrast. It borrows the stability puzzle from the historical lens and one bounded analogy from the analogy-led lens.

**Plan.** Open with the core idea (see the opening sentences below).
- **The classical expectation, in five sentences or fewer.** Build it carefully enough that its failure is sharp. Start from the picture most people meet at school: a tiny heavy nucleus with an electron circling it like a planet. Add a law of classical physics confirmed by every radio transmitter: a charge that changes speed or direction gives off electromagnetic waves (light is one kind) and loses energy. Together they predict that the electron spirals into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second, glowing in a continuous smear of colours on the way. And because an orbit's size depends on how it started, no two hydrogen atoms would need to be alike.
- **Three facts that break it.**
  - Atoms last for billions of years; you are made of them.
  - Every hydrogen atom is the same.
  - Glowing hydrogen gives off only a few sharp colours: four visible ones, at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm. Astronomers use this fingerprint to find hydrogen in stars, and the same effect is why neon signs glow red-orange and sodium streetlamps glow yellow.
- **The quantum replacement, in three steps.**
  1. **A wave of possibility.** The electron is not a little ball on a track. It is described by a wave of possibility spread around the nucleus. How strong the wave is at a place gives the chance of finding the electron there if you look, and when you look you always find one whole electron at one spot.
  2. **A trapped wave can only take certain shapes.** Introduce the guitar string: pinned at both ends, it can only vibrate with a whole number of half-waves along it, so it plays only certain notes. State the limits immediately. The string is a vibration of real material, while the electron's wave is not a vibration of anything and only gives chances. And the atom's patterns are three-dimensional clouds held in by the nucleus's pull, not a string with pinned ends. Each allowed pattern has its own energy, so the atom has a ladder of allowed energies with gaps between them. When the electron drops from a higher rung to a lower one, the difference leaves as one packet of light, a photon. The one equation, $E = hf$, says that a fixed energy gap means a fixed frequency, which the eye sees as one exact colour. That is why the lines are sharp and the same in every hydrogen atom (figure).
  3. **There is a bottom rung and nothing below it,** which is why the atom doesn't collapse. Explain why without equations. Squeezing the electron's wave closer to the nucleus forces it into shorter wavelengths, and in quantum mechanics a shorter wavelength means more momentum and so more energy of motion. The nucleus's pull wants the electron closer and the squeezing cost pushes back. The balance sets the size of every hydrogen atom, roughly a tenth of a nanometre across. Name this as the uncertainty principle at work: a property of waves, not of clumsy measurement.
- **Zoom out: what quantum mechanics is about in general.** A short map:
  - quantization (confined things have discrete energies);
  - amplitudes and probability (these waves of possibility can add and cancel, as the famous double-slit experiment shows, and the theory predicts chances rather than certainties);
  - uncertainty;
  - entanglement (one line, including "no messages").
- **Reach.** One line: why atoms bond into molecules, the periodic table, transistors, lasers, LEDs.
- **Close.** One or two sentences separating the well-tested predictions from the still-debated question of what the wave "really is".

**Example.** The hydrogen atom. Classical physics says it collapses in about $10^{-11}$ s. In fact it is a stable atom about 0.1 nm across that glows only at 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm, and each of those lines is one drop between two rungs of its energy ladder.

**Figure request (one):**
- `b-ladder-and-lines`, placed right after step 2. Two panels.
  - **Left:** hydrogen's energy ladder. Draw horizontal lines at $E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV for $n = 1$ to $6$, with the y-axis labelled "energy (eV)" running from $-14$ to $0$. Label the $n = 1$ line "lowest rung: nothing below it". Draw downward arrows from $n = 3, 4, 5, 6$ to $n = 2$, each coloured by the light it emits and labelled with its wavelength: 656 nm (red), 486 nm (blue-green), 434 nm (blue), 410 nm (violet).
  - **Right:** two horizontal strips over a wavelength axis from 380 to 700 nm. The top strip is a continuous rainbow labelled "classical expectation: a smear of all colours". The bottom strip is black with four thin bright lines at 410, 434, 486 and 656 nm, labelled "what hydrogen actually emits".

  What to notice: each sharp colour is one drop between two rungs, bigger drops give bluer light, and the bottom rung is why the atom can't collapse.

**Opening sentences.** "Quantum mechanics is the theory of how matter and light behave at the scale of atoms, and the quickest way to see what it's about is a question classical physics gets spectacularly wrong: why do atoms exist at all? Its answer is that an electron isn't a tiny ball on a track but is described by a wave of possibilities, and a wave trapped around a nucleus can only take certain shapes, so the atom can only have certain energies, including a lowest one it can never fall below."

**Watch out for:**
- Don't use "orbit" for the quantum picture. If Bohr appears at all, it should only be as a superseded stepping stone.
- Don't say the electron "is smeared out" as a cloud of charge. The cloud is a map of where you'd find it.
- Say the electron "drops" between rungs, not that it makes "instantaneous jumps".
- The size argument is a rough balance ("roughly a tenth of a nanometre across"), not a derivation.
- The classical collapse time for hydrogen is about $1.6 \times 10^{-11}$ s; write it as "about a hundred-billionth of a second".
- Keep the classical setup to five sentences or fewer.
- The only equation is $E = hf$. Define every symbol: $E$ is the photon's energy, $h$ is Planck's constant (a tiny fixed number), and $f$ is the frequency, which the eye sees as colour.

**Check-yourself idea.** "Quantum dots are tiny crystals that trap electrons, and manufacturers set their colour by choosing their size. Would a smaller dot glow redder or bluer?" Answer: bluer. More squeezing raises the energies and widens the gaps, so each photon carries more energy and has a higher frequency.
