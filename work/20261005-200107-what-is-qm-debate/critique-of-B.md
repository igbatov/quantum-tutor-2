# Critique of candidate B: "The atom that shouldn't exist"

From the author of candidate A ("One electron at a time"). Learner: conceptual level, first question of the session, empty profile.

**Overall.** B is accurate. I found no physics errors. The collapse time, the atom's size, the four Balmer wavelengths, the $-13.6/n^2$ eV ladder and the 122 nm line in the figure request all check out. B also avoids the usual traps: it never calls the quantum atom an "orbit", never says the electron is smeared out, never has it jump instantly and never blames uncertainty on clumsy measurement. Its weaknesses are emphasis and two missing "why"s. Both can be fixed in a few sentences without changing the route. **If you fix only two things, fix items 1 and 2.**

## Where B will fail this learner

**1. [major] The defining idea of quantum mechanics is told, not shown, and the opening leaves it out.**

- **The opening covers only quantization.** The first two sentences are what a learner keeps if they read nothing else. Chance and probability don't appear. "Wave of possibilities" is the only hint.
- **The amplitude rule arrives late and unsupported.** B's own map lists it as one of the big ideas, yet it first appears in the zoom-out as a bare statement. Its only support is "That's why electrons sent one by one through two slits build up bright and dark stripes."
  - That sentence names an experiment the learner has never been shown.
  - It doesn't say why "one by one" matters.
  - It leaves out the punchline: at a dark stripe, opening a second slit *lowers* the number of hits.
  - The word "interference" never appears.
- **The atom story never uses cancelling amplitudes.** It runs on four facts taken on trust:
  - the electron is described by a wave of chances;
  - each trapped pattern has one energy;
  - light leaves as photons with $E = hf$;
  - a shorter wavelength means more momentum.

  (A's closing bullets also assert things. The difference is that A's main body gets its one rule from data.) A conceptual reader can finish B thinking quantum mechanics means "electrons are waves, so atoms have harmonics". That is true as far as it goes. It misses what no classical wave reproduces: each detection is a single, whole particle, and the chances come from amplitudes that can cancel.

*Fix within B's route:*
- (a) In the opening, add "...and the theory predicts only the chances of where it will be found."
- (b) Replace the name-drop with the punchline: "Fire electrons one at a time at two narrow slits: each lands as a single dot, yet the dots build up stripes, and at a dark stripe, opening the second slit *lowers* the number of hits, because the amplitudes for the two ways cancel. This adding and cancelling is called interference."

**2. [major] The step from "certain shapes" to "certain energies" is asserted, and the guitar analogy, taken at face value, undercuts the bottom rung.**

- **The limit that matters isn't stated.** B gives two limits of the string analogy: the electron's wave isn't a vibration of material, and the atom is 3-D and held by electric pull. It doesn't give the limit its argument rests on.
- **Why it matters.** A string's allowed patterns fix its *notes*, not its energy. You can play the lowest note as softly as you like, with energy close to zero. A learner who carries that over will wonder why the electron's lowest pattern can't hold less energy too. But a lowest energy is B's whole answer to "why do atoms exist?". Step 3 answers the doubt only implicitly, through energy coming from wavelength.
- **Where the gap is.** "But the key result carries over. Each allowed pattern has a definite energy" skips exactly this step.
- **Fix.** One accurate sentence closes the gap. It can replace "But the key result carries over": "Unlike a string, which can play the same note softly or loudly, the electron's wave can't be made 'quieter' or 'louder', since it always stands for exactly one electron; its energy is set by its shape alone, so each allowed shape has exactly one energy."

## Overstatements and precision slips (minor, each a cheap fix)

- **Opening: "can only take certain shapes".** A trapped wave can be any mix of the allowed patterns. Only the *steady* patterns are restricted, and only they have one definite energy. B already says "steady" for the string. Say it for the electron too: "can only settle into certain steady shapes".
- **Entanglement bullet.** "No plan agreed in advance could produce" leaves out distance and locality, which are what make the result remarkable. Bell's theorem rules out instructions *carried by each particle*, not every hidden mechanism. Suggest: "however far apart they are measured, their results are linked in ways no plan agreed in advance and carried by each particle could produce, yet never in a way that sends a message."
- **Close: "Those are questions of interpretation, and the main interpretations make the same predictions."**
  - How a measurement ends with one definite result is the measurement problem, which is an *open question*. Most proposed answers are interpretations with identical predictions. But spontaneous-collapse models change the theory slightly and are being tested.
  - "Measurement" is never defined, so a reader raised on pop science will hear "someone looking".
  - Suggest: "...how a measurement (any interaction that leaves a physical record, not a person looking) ends up with one definite result. These are open questions: most proposed answers are interpretations that make identical predictions, though a few would change the theory slightly and are being tested."
- **"In big, warm objects their strange effects wash out into everyday behaviour."** This sits oddly beside B's own thesis that quantum effects are *why* everyday matter is stable and coloured. Say which effects wash out, and why: "interference effects wash out, largely because big, warm things constantly leave records of their position in the air and light around them."
- **"Nothing in this picture picks out particular colours."** This asserts where a mechanism would cost little. As the electron spirals in, it circles faster and faster, so the colour it gives off sweeps smoothly. (Nit.)
- **"In visible light there are just four."** This is the textbook convention, but fainter Balmer lines at 397 and 389 nm sit at the violet edge of vision. "Four bright ones" is safe. (Nit.)

## Clarity for this learner (minor)

- **The opening is too dense.** It is two sentences of about 50 words each. Between them they carry scale, "every scale", the classical failure, the question, the wave, trapping, shapes, energies and the bottom rung. Split each in two.
- **The uncertainty principle is used before it is stated.** Step 3 says "This is the uncertainty principle at work", but the principle's content only arrives in the zoom-out. Put the statement in Step 3: "a wave squeezed into a small space must mix many wavelengths, including short ones, so it can't have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum". Then cut the zoom-out bullet to a pointer back to Step 3. That frees words for item 1(b).
- **The ladder can rebuild the school "shells" picture.** Many learners bring Bohr's orbits from school. A ladder of rungs reads easily as orbits at different heights, which would quietly rebuild the planetary atom B sets out to retire. One clause helps: "each rung is a whole cloud pattern, not a track at a set distance."

## What B does better than A (conceded)

1. **Stakes.** "Why do atoms exist at all?" is a stronger hook for a first question than dots on a screen. From the first line it ties quantum mechanics to the learner's own body, to chemistry and to neon signs. A's link to everyday matter is one bullet and one closing sentence.
2. **It replaces the wrong pictures this learner most likely holds.** It breaks the planetary atom from school with three checkable facts, and it corrects the reading of the cloud as a smeared-out electron. A addresses neither.
3. **Quantization and uncertainty are shown working, not listed.**
   - B uses real wavelengths and a ladder figure that maps each colour to one drop.
   - It gets the atom's size from the squeezing trade-off. That shows uncertainty involves no measurement far more convincingly than A's one-line bullet on how waves trade location against wavelength.
   - By comparison, A's quantization bullet is a bare assertion.
4. **Lower abstraction load.** A guitar string is familiar. A's tip-to-tail arrow arithmetic is the most abstract step in either candidate and the likeliest place for this learner to stall.
5. **A more precise close.** B names the measurement problem and says the interpretations share predictions. A only gestures at "the meaning". I would borrow this.
6. **A transfer check question.** The quantum-dot colour question asks the learner to apply the idea somewhere new, not to re-run the worked example.

## Worth borrowing from A (sentences, not route)

- The double-slit punchline and the word "interference" (item 1).
- "A physical record, not a conscious mind" and the "why never with a chair?" line, for the measurement and wash-out fixes.

**Length.** A and B are both about 900 words. The two major fixes add about 85 words, so make them first. Of the minor fixes, the definition of measurement and the entanglement qualifier matter most for accuracy. The rest are optional if length is tight.
