Quantum mechanics is the physics of electrons, atoms and light (and, as far as anyone knows, of everything else too). At its heart is one strange rule: when something can happen in more than one way and nothing records which way it took, nature doesn't add the chances of each way. It adds quantities called *amplitudes*, which can reinforce or cancel each other, and only then turns the total into a chance. This rule is behind particles making wave patterns, atoms glowing in sharp colours, and a theory that predicts chances rather than certainties.

Here is the rule at work in a real experiment. A source sends out electrons so sparsely that only one is ever in flight. Each heads for a barrier with two narrow slits, and behind it a screen flashes where the electron lands. Every electron arrives as one dot at one spot, never a smear and never half an electron. Where the next dot lands is unpredictable, yet thousands of dots build up bright and dark bands.

Predict before you read on. With one slit open, the dots form a single broad patch. Open the second slit too: does a given spot get more hits, or fewer?

Common sense says more, since there are now two ways to get there. Yet at the spots that become dark bands, opening the second slit drops the hits almost to zero.

So it can't be that each electron just takes one slit while the other slit makes no difference to it: then the chances would simply add, and a second slit could only add hits. Nor has anyone seen an electron "go through both slits at once": a detector at the slits always finds the whole electron at just one of them, and then, as you'll see, the bands vanish. Instead of a path, quantum mechanics gives a rule.

Each route to a spot comes with an arrow, its amplitude, which turns like a clock hand as the route gets longer. Chain the arrows tip to tail, like the legs of a walk; the chance of landing at that spot is the squared length of the arrow from start to finish. Give each slit's arrow length 1, so either slit alone gives 1 unit of chance. Lined up, the walk ends 2 from the start, so the chance is 4: twice the 2 you'd get by adding chances. Pointing opposite ways, the walk ends where it began: chance 0, a dark spot. Moving along the screen makes one route steadily longer than the other, so the arrows swing in and out of line: bright, dark, bright. This adding and cancelling is called interference. Averaged across the bands the chance comes back to 2, so interference only moves probability around; it never creates or destroys it.

Water waves through two gaps in a harbour wall make similar bands, calm where crest meets trough. But water is spread-out stuff that rocks the whole shore at once, while each electron lands whole, at one spot. The wave behaviour lives in the amplitudes, seen only in where electrons tend to land.

Now add anything that records which slit each electron used: a detector, say, or light bouncing off the electron in a way that reveals its slit. The routes become distinguishable, so you add chances instead of arrows, and the bands vanish into one smooth patch. Nobody has to read the record; it only has to exist. That is what "observation" means in quantum mechanics: a physical record, not a conscious mind. Interference has been seen with photons, neutrons, atoms and even 60-atom molecules, so why never with a chair? Largely because big, warm things constantly leave records of their position in the air and light around them.

Besides interference, the other main themes of quantum mechanics come from the same amplitudes:

- **Probability.** The theory predicts chances exactly, and the amplitudes change in a fully predictable way until an outcome is recorded. Only single outcomes are left to chance, and nobody has found a way to predict them better.
- **Quantization.** Amplitudes confined to a small space, like an electron held by a nucleus, have only certain steady patterns, each with its own energy. So an atom's energy can take only certain values, including a lowest one it can't fall below (which is why atoms are stable), and each drop from one value to a lower one gives off light of one sharp colour.
- **Uncertainty.** A wave with one exact wavelength goes on forever, so a confined wave must mix many wavelengths. Since an electron's wavelength fixes its momentum (mass times velocity), no electron can have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum: a fact about waves, not clumsy measuring.
- **Entanglement.** Two particles can share one set of amplitudes that can't be split into a set for each. Measured far apart, their results can be correlated in ways that no answers agreed in advance and carried by each particle could reproduce, yet no message can be sent this way.

This isn't just lab physics: the same rules explain how atoms bind into molecules, and they made possible transistors (in every computer chip), lasers, LEDs and MRI scanners.

The predictions themselves are among the best-tested in science. Still debated is the meaning: what the arrows really *are* (a physical wave, a tool for tracking information, or branches of a larger reality), and whether the electron takes a definite path between source and screen.
