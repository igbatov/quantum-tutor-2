Quantum mechanics is the physics of electrons, atoms and light (and, as far as anyone knows, of everything else too). It predicts chances rather than certainties, and at its heart is one strange rule for working them out. When something can happen in several ways and nothing records which way it took, you don't add the chances of each way: you add quantities called amplitudes (you'll meet them as arrows shortly), which can reinforce or cancel each other, and only then turn the total into a chance. This rule is behind particles making wave patterns, and behind atoms being stable and glowing in sharp colours.

In a real experiment, a source sends electrons one at a time toward a barrier with two narrow slits, and behind it a screen flashes where each one lands. Every electron arrives as one dot at one spot, never a smear and never half an electron. Where the next dot lands is unpredictable, yet thousands of dots build up bright and dark bands. Coming singly, the electrons can't be jostling each other into bands.

Predict before you read on. With one slit open, the dots form a single broad patch. Open the second slit too: does a given spot get more hits, or fewer?

Common sense says more, since there are now two ways to get there. Yet at the spots that become dark bands, opening the second slit drops the hits almost to zero.

So it can't be that each electron just takes one slit while the other slit makes no difference to it: then the chances would simply add, and a second slit could only add hits. Nor has anyone seen an electron "go through both slits at once": a detector at the slits always finds a whole electron at one slit, and then the bands vanish. Instead of a path, quantum mechanics gives a rule.

Each route to a spot comes with an arrow, its amplitude, which turns like a clock hand as the route gets longer. The extra length for one full turn is the electron's wavelength, which is shorter for faster electrons. Chain the arrows tip to tail, like the legs of a walk; the chance of landing at that spot is the squared length of the arrow from start to finish. Give each slit's arrow length 1, so either slit alone gives 1 unit of chance. Lined up, the walk ends 2 from the start, and 2 squared is 4 units: twice the 2 you'd get by adding chances. Pointing opposite ways, the walk ends where it began: 0 units, a dark spot. Moving along the screen makes one route steadily longer than the other, so the arrows swing in and out of line: bright, dark, bright. This adding and cancelling is called interference. Averaged across the bands, the chance comes back to 2 units: interference only moves probability around.

Water waves through two gaps in a harbour wall make similar bands, calm where crest meets trough. But water is spread-out stuff, while each electron lands whole, at one spot: the wave lives in the amplitudes, seen only in where electrons tend to land.

Now add anything that records which slit each electron used, such as light bouncing off it in a way that reveals the slit. The routes become distinguishable, so you add chances instead of arrows, and the bands vanish into one smooth patch. Nobody has to read the record; it only has to exist. That is what "observation" means in quantum mechanics: a physical record, not a conscious mind. Interference has been seen with photons, atoms and even 2,000-atom molecules, so why never with a chair? Largely because big, warm things constantly leave records of their position in the air and light around them.

Besides interference, the other main themes of quantum mechanics come from the same amplitudes:

  • Probability. The theory predicts chances exactly, and the amplitudes change over time in a fully predictable way. Only which outcome gets recorded is left to chance, and nobody has found a way to predict it better.
  • Uncertainty. A wave with one exact wavelength goes on forever, so a confined wave must mix many wavelengths. Since an electron's wavelength fixes its momentum (mass times velocity), no electron can have both a sharp position and a sharp momentum. That is the uncertainty principle, a fact about waves, not clumsy measuring.
  • Quantization. Near a nucleus, an electron's amplitudes form a trapped wave, which can hold steady only in certain patterns that fit its trap, each with a definite energy. There is a lowest one: squeezing the wave closer forces in shorter wavelengths, so more energy of motion, a cost that balances the nucleus's pull. Pre-quantum physics predicted that atoms would collapse, their electrons spiralling in within about a hundred-billionth of a second; this floor is why they don't. A drop to a lower energy releases the difference as one packet of light, a photon, whose energy sets its colour: one sharp colour per kind of drop.
  • Entanglement. Two particles can share one set of amplitudes that can't be split into a set for each. Measured however far apart, their results can be correlated in ways no answers agreed in advance and carried by each particle could reproduce. Yet the link shows up only when the two lists of results are compared, so no message can be sent this way.

These rules explain how atoms bind into molecules, and they made possible transistors (in every computer chip), lasers, LEDs and MRI scanners. Their predictions are among the best-tested in science.

Still debated is what the theory means: what the arrows really are (a physical wave, a tool for tracking information, or branches of a larger reality), whether the electron takes a definite path, and why each recording shows just one outcome (the "measurement problem"). The main interpretations make identical predictions, so experiments can't decide among them; a few proposals that would slightly change the theory are being tested.
