# Strategies: "why the square", answered by the frequency-operator route

Mode: another-angle (the three previous answers to "why complex and why squared" have not been chosen; the learner asked for the list of other routes and picked the frequency-operator one). Level override: intermediate (algebra, complex numbers as pairs/hands, 2-vectors, 2×2 matrices, sums; derivatives tolerated; no Dirac notation unless defined in one line, since kets, "density matrix", "trace" and "Bloch ball" cost footing last time). From the previous judgement: this learner values a one-line reason up front that a calculator can check, arithmetic done in front of them, every lab term defined on first use, honest caveats about what each argument assumes, and sources per section. The previous finals already used the Mach–Zehnder with a phase plate (A), spectra/precession/ammonia (B), and polarization sorters plus three slits (C); none of those should be the lead experiment here.

The route is fixed. All three strategies explain the same argument: take N identical copies of a two-outcome system; the observable that reads the relative frequency of one outcome has mean |c|² for every N and a spread that shrinks like 1/√N; in the N → ∞ construction the N-copy state is an eigenstate of the frequency operator with eigenvalue |c|²; with the certainty rule for eigenstates (eigenvalue–eigenstate link), the long-run frequency is therefore |c|², with no probability rule put in by hand. They differ in experiment, in which piece of the mathematics is the spine, in the order and framing of the models, and in the figures.

## Route facts (checked; every candidate draws on these)

Notation for all candidates: a two-outcome system with amplitudes c₀ (outcome 0) and c₁ (outcome 1), |c₀|² + |c₁|² = 1; p := |c₁|². N independently prepared copies: the N-copy state is a list of 2^N amplitudes, one per string s of 0s and 1s, with entry c₀^(N−m) c₁^m where m = number of 1s in s (product rule for independent preparations; name it as an assumption). The frequency "counter" F_N multiplies the entry of string s by m(s)/N (a diagonal matrix in the string basis); its possible readings (eigenvalues) are 0, 1/N, …, 1; its sure-bet lists (eigenvectors) are lists with all nonzero entries at one value of m. Note: p is in general not one of the readings at finite N (e.g. p = 0.1 at N = 4), and Ψ_N is never an eigenvector at finite N when 0 < p < 1.

1. Weights. The squared length of the entries with m ones adds to w_m = C(N,m) p^m (1−p)^(N−m), where C(N,m) = N!/(m!(N−m)!) is the number of strings with m ones. Σ_m w_m = (p + 1 − p)^N = 1 (binomial theorem; uses |c₀|² + |c₁|² = 1).
2. Shortest-length computation (Born-free linear algebra). ‖(F_N − λ)Ψ_N‖² = Σ_m w_m (m/N − λ)². Operator trick: F_N = (1/N) Σ_k P^(k), with P^(k) the "is copy k in outcome 1?" matrix (diag(0,1) on copy k, P² = P). ⟨P^(k)⟩ = p; ⟨P^(j) P^(k)⟩ = p² for j ≠ k (product state), = p for j = k. So ⟨F_N⟩ = p and ⟨F_N²⟩ = (1/N²)[N p + N(N−1) p²] = p² + p(1−p)/N. Hence ‖(F_N − λ)Ψ_N‖² = ⟨F_N²⟩ − 2λ⟨F_N⟩ + λ² = (λ − p)² + p(1−p)/N: shortest at λ = p = |c₁|², shortest squared length p(1−p)/N. Here ⟨A⟩ means the squared-length-weighted average Σ_s |Ψ_s|² A_s for a diagonal A (define it as a mathematical quantity; do not call it an expectation value until the Born reading is introduced).
3. N = 2 by hand: Ψ₂ = (c₀², c₀c₁, c₁c₀, c₁²) for strings 00, 01, 10, 11; readings (0, ½, ½, 1); ‖(F₂ − λ)Ψ₂‖² = λ²(1−p)² + 2(½−λ)² p(1−p) + (1−λ)² p². At λ = p this is p(1−p)/2 (algebra: p(1−p)[2p(1−p) + 2(¼ − p + p²)] = p(1−p)[½] ). With p = ¼: 3/32 = 0.09375. With p = ½: 1/8.
4. Deviant part. For any ε > 0, the squared length of the part of Ψ_N on strings with |m/N − p| > ε is at most p(1−p)/(N ε²) (Chebyshev-type: on those strings (m/N − p)² > ε², so ε² × deviant weight ≤ Σ_m w_m (m/N − p)² = p(1−p)/N). The exact tail is far smaller (exponentially small in N). Numbers for p = ¼, ε = 0.05: bound 75/N → 0.075 at N = 10³, 0.0075 at 10⁴, 7.5×10⁻⁵ at 10⁶.
5. Spread of the reading: √(p(1−p)/N). p = ¼: 0.433 (N=1), 0.217 (4), 0.0433 (100), 0.00433 (10⁴). p = ½: 0.5/√N: 0.158 (10), 0.05 (100), 0.0158 (1000). Count variance N p(1−p): 25 at N = 100, p = ½ (Itano's curve: zero at p = 0 and 1, maximum at ½).
6. Where the 2 enters: through the squared lengths. If the "size" of a list were measured with exponent q instead (weights v_m = C(N,m)|c₀|^{q(N−m)}|c₁|^{qm}), the ratio v_{m+1}/v_m = ((N−m)/(m+1))·|c₁|^q/|c₀|^q shows the dominant strings have frequency f_q = |c₁|^q/(|c₀|^q + |c₁|^q), which equals |c₁|² only at q = 2 (and the weights total 1 only at q = 2). With c₀ = √3/2, c₁ = ½: f₁ = 0.366, f₂ = 0.25, f₃ = 0.161, f₄ = 0.10. Reading: the exponent the argument delivers is the exponent of the norm the formalism uses to decide which part of a list is negligible; the argument moves "why |c|²?" to "why is the norm the 2-norm?", which the earlier answers tied to conservation under continuous change (only the 2-norm has continuous lossless maps) and to Gleason. Label this as our reading; it is in the spirit of Squires and of Caves & Schack.
7. Equal amplitudes hide the exponent: for |c₀| = |c₁| every q gives f_q = ½. The exponent is visible only for unequal amplitudes; say so where the 50/50 splitter appears.
8. Everett's measure (1957): a branch weight m(a) depending only on |a|, additive under regrouping: if branches with amplitudes a_i (i in a group) are merged into one normalized branch with amplitude a′ = √(Σ|a_i|²), require m(a′) = Σ m(a_i). Put m(a) = g(|a|²): g(Σ x_i) = Σ g(x_i), Cauchy's equation; for a non-decreasing g the only solutions are g(x) = k x; so m = k|a|², and Σ|a_i|² = 1 fixes k = 1. The 2 enters at a′ = √(Σ|a_i|²), the 2-norm normalization of the merged branch (with a q-norm normalization the same steps give k|a|^q). Branch counting (one unit per branch) violates additivity: merging two branches into one changes the count from 2 to 1. Critics (Kent 1990; Barrett 1999): additivity and "depends on amplitude only" are what one assumes if the measure is already being treated as a probability; whether merged branches are one world or two is the disputed question.
9. Many-worlds frequency version (Everett 1957; DeWitt 1970; Graham 1973): after N measurements, the branches whose frequency differs from p by more than ε ("maverick" branches) have total weight ≤ p(1−p)/(Nε²) → 0; DeWitt: "the mathematical formalism of the quantum theory is capable of yielding its own interpretation". Objections: measure zero is not non-existence (those branches, and the observers in them, exist); at finite N nothing is zero; by branch number, most branches have frequency near ½ whatever p is (for N = 20, p = 0.8: by count the peak is at m = 10; by weight at m = 16); supporters of many-worlds themselves largely abandoned the frequency route for decision theory (Deutsch 1999; Wallace 2012) and envariance (Zurek 2003).
10. Branch counting numbers for N = 4, p = 0.9: weight of branches with m ≤ 2: 0.0001 + 0.0036 + 0.0486 = 0.0523; their number: 1 + 4 + 6 = 11 of 16 = 0.6875. For N = 3, p = 0.8: group weights by frequency 0, ⅓, ⅔, 1: 0.008, 0.096, 0.384, 0.512 (total 1; weight per single branch 0.008, 0.032, 0.128, 0.512).
11. Infinite copies (Farhi, Goldstone & Gutmann 1989): the infinite tensor product (von Neumann 1939) is non-separable; product lists built from different single-copy states are orthogonal in it, because the per-copy overlap raised to the N-th power goes to zero unless its size is 1: polarizer angles 30° and 31° have per-photon overlap cos 1° = 0.99985, and after 10⁵ photons the overlap is 0.99985^(10⁵) = e^(−15.2) ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁷. FGG define F_∞ on a suitable domain and show Ψ_∞ = ψ^{⊗∞} is an eigenvector with eigenvalue |c₁|²; the sense is the one their construction defines (strong limit on that domain). Ochs (1977) and Gutmann (1995) give the mathematical law-of-large-numbers versions.
12. Critiques to state in every candidate, labelled as contested: (i) the eigenvalue–eigenstate link (certainty for eigenstates) is itself the probability-1 case of the Born rule, so the argument derives the quantitative rule from its qualitative case plus the norm; (ii) Squires (1990): dropping the remainder "because its norm is zero" assumes that zero norm means zero probability, which is a probability statement about the norm, and for finite N (every real experiment) one needs "small norm ⇒ rarely happens", which is the Born rule's substance; (iii) Caves & Schack (2005), title: "Properties of the frequency operator do not imply the quantum probability postulate": the N → ∞ limit requires choosing which lists count as close (a topology), and that choice (the Hilbert-space norm) is where the rule enters; the frequency operator's properties alone do not force |c|²; (iv) the limit lives in a non-separable space with no finite-N counterpart; (v) in the many-worlds reading, branch counting versus weighting is unsettled by the mathematics. Supporters' replies: the assumption used is only certainty-for-eigenstates plus continuity in the norm, weaker than the full quantitative rule (Hartle; FGG; Gutmann 1995); and any rule that respects the Hilbert-space structure must be |c|² anyway (Gleason), so the argument shows the formalism is consistent with exactly one long-run frequency. The honest summary for the learner: the argument shows that, read with certainty-for-eigenstates and the 2-norm, the formalism can assign long-run frequencies to only one exponent, 2; it does not show that norms are probabilities, and that step is where every derivation of the Born rule has to assume something.
13. What it does and doesn't establish (for the closing section of each candidate): does: the formalism singles out |c|² as the only consistent long-run frequency; the exponent is the norm's exponent; no extra probability primitive beyond certainty. Doesn't: give finite-N probabilities without an extra rule; explain why norm connects to chance; avoid the infinite idealization; settle any interpretation. Relations: Gleason (non-contextual additive chances, dim ≥ 3 ⇒ squared shadows); envariance (Zurek 2003: swap symmetry of entangled states; equal case shown in the previous C); decision theory (Deutsch 1999, Wallace 2012: rational betting plus branching symmetries); pilot-wave typicality (Dürr, Goldstein & Zanghì 1992: a law-of-large-numbers argument in which the measure is picked out because the dynamics preserves it, the closest cousin of the frequency argument); objective collapse (square in the law; Gisin's no-signalling constraint). All give identical predictions for every count; what decides is which assumption one finds weaker.

## Experiments available (what is observed, with conditions)

E1. Single photons at a polarizer and polarizing beam splitter (define both; heralded source defined again, each candidate stands alone). Fraction transmitted follows cos²θ (Malus's law, 1809, for classical light; at the single-photon level in teaching laboratories, e.g. Beck's single-photon labs; Dehlinger & Mitchell 2002 for cos² curves with photon pairs) to within the few-per-cent imperfections of real polarizers and detectors. The fraction's run-to-run scatter shrinks as 1/√N. Whole arrivals: Grangier, Roger & Aspect 1986 (α = 0.18 ± 0.06 against ≥ 1 for a classical wave).
E2. Beam-splitter quantum random number generator (Jennewein et al. 2000; Stefanov et al. 2000): single photons at a 50/50 splitter, one bit per click; the fraction of 1s converges to ½ up to a small bias from unequal detector efficiencies and splitter ratio, which is measured and removed. Equal amplitudes: every exponent agrees here (fact 7), so this is a check, not evidence for the square.
E3. Quantum projection noise (Itano et al. 1993, Phys. Rev. A 47, 3554): trapped ions prepared in a superposition of two levels with the upper-level share p set by the length of a radio-frequency or laser pulse; read out by state-dependent fluorescence (a laser makes an ion glow if it is in one level, not if in the other); the number found in one level fluctuates from run to run with variance N p(1−p), zero at p = 0 and 1 and largest at p = ½; done with a single mercury ion measured repeatedly and with groups of beryllium ions (quote no ion numbers unless verified); photon-counting noise separated out. A classical picture in which each ion is partly in each level and glows a fixed fraction predicts no such fluctuation.
E4. Rabi flopping in a single trapped ion: fraction found in the upper level against pulse length follows sin²(Ωt/2) (ideal: resonant, no decoherence), each point the fraction over a few hundred to a few thousand repetitions with binomial error bars (Leibfried et al., Rev. Mod. Phys. 75, 281 (2003) for the method; Monroe et al. 1995 / Meekhof et al. 1996 for curves).
E5. Electron build-up (Tonomura et al. 1989, Am. J. Phys. 57, 117; earlier Merli, Missiroli & Pozzi 1976): electrons one at a time through an electron biprism (define), frames at 10, 100, 3000, 20 000 and 70 000 electrons; the fraction landing in any chosen stripe settles to a definite value; on average fewer than one electron in flight at a time. Bach et al. 2013 (New J. Phys. 15, 033018) with masked slits.
E6. Stern–Gerlach (Gerlach & Stern 1922): silver atoms through an inhomogeneous magnet split into two bands; the modern two-outcome spin sorter (define in one sentence where used).

### Strategy A: Count at the polarizer (the counter's shortest length is at |c|²)

**Experiments.** E1: heralded single photons through a polarizer at angle θ to the transmitting axis of a polarizing beam splitter, a detector at each exit; the transmitted fraction follows cos²θ and the reflected fraction sin²θ (ideal lossless optics, perfect detectors, one photon at a time); the run-to-run scatter of the fraction over N photons shrinks as 1/√N (worked numbers for θ = 30°, reflected share p = ¼: ±0.043 at N = 100, ±0.0043 at N = 10⁴); whole arrivals from the 1986 anticorrelation test. E2: the beam-splitter random-number generator, fraction → ½ with a measured, removable bias; noted as the case where equal amplitudes make every exponent agree.

**Classical candidates ruled out, and by what.** Classical wave: energy fraction cos²θ gives the right mean but divides each pulse, so faint light would fire both detectors together at least as often as chance; killed by the anticorrelation test. Tickets (each photon carries a pre-set exit): matches any single-photon frequency by construction, explains nothing about why cos²θ, and is not ruled out by counts alone (say so); Bell tests constrain it for pairs. Ordinary probability with p supplied from outside: consistent with every count, silent on where p comes from; the quantum argument tries to read p off the state.

**Models.** *Model 1: the Born rule as a postulate* (the two-component state (cos θ, sin θ), chance = squared length of the component; the law of large numbers then gives the 1/√N scatter as the binomial spread, Feynman I ch. 6). Why: one rule fits every count. Pros: minimal, no infinite limits. Cons: a postulate; says nothing about why 2. *Model 2: the frequency operator (Finkelstein 1963, Hartle 1968, FGG 1989).* Intuition: write the whole N-photon state as a list, one entry per string of T/R outcomes, entries multiplied (product rule); the counter reads m/N on each string; ask which single number the list is "closest to being certain about". Derivation, every step: N = 1, 2, 3 lists and readings in tables; the shortest-length computation ‖(F_N − λ)Ψ_N‖² = (λ − p)² + p(1−p)/N via the operator trick (define ⟨·⟩ as the squared-length-weighted average, a mathematical quantity); N = 2 by hand with p = ¼ (3/32); the Chebyshev bound on the deviant part; the limit and the certainty rule; then the which-norm check (fact 6, with the f_q table). Why this model: built to get the rule out of the formalism with only certainty-for-eigenstates. Pros: no probability primitive; shows the 2 is the norm's 2; the finite-N spread is the measured scatter (under the Born reading). Cons (contested, labelled): infinite limit; finite-N gap; Squires's and Caves–Schack's circularity charge; non-separable limit space; the certainty rule is the probability-1 case of what is derived. *Model 3: the many-worlds reading* (Everett, DeWitt, Graham), short: same list, all strings real, deviant strings have small total weight; branch-counting objection in two lines with the N = 4, p = 0.9 numbers. **How they relate:** identical predictions; the frequency argument is the law of large numbers written inside the formalism; its exponent is the norm's; Gleason, envariance, decision theory, pilot-wave typicality compared in one table (assumption / what it yields / main objection).

**Figures.** `a-weights-concentrate`: four panels, N = 4, 16, 64, 256, p = ¼: the weights w_m = C(N,m) p^m (1−p)^(N−m) against the reading m/N, dashed line at 0.25, each panel annotated with the spread √(p(1−p)/N) = 0.217, 0.108, 0.054, 0.027; caption: the weights total 1 in each panel and pile up ever more tightly round ¼; at N = 4 the reading ¼ exists but carries weight only 0.42. `a-deviant-length`: log–log, N from 10 to 10⁵, the exact squared length of the deviant part (|m/N − ¼| > ε) for ε = 0.05 and 0.1, with the Chebyshev bound p(1−p)/(Nε²) for each, and p(1−p)/N as a reference line; caption: everything falls to zero, the exact curves much faster than the bound; nothing here is a probability until a rule links length to chance.

**Opening sentence.** "Send photons one at a time through a polarizer set 30° from the axis of a polarizing splitter and count the reflected ones: after 100 photons the reflected share scatters by about 4 percentage points around 25%, after 10 000 by about 0.4, and it settles on the squared amplitude; the frequency-operator argument is the attempt to get that number, exponent and all, out of the N-photon state itself, with no probability rule put in by hand."

**Check yourself.** Polarizer at 60°, so the reflected share is |c₁|² = ¾: what scatter in the reflected fraction does the shortest-length formula give for N = 300 photons, and how many photons would halve it? (0.025; 1200.)

### Strategy B: N ions in one shot (projection noise, the deviant part, and which norm)

**Experiments.** E3 (Itano et al. 1993): ions prepared in a superposition with upper-level share p set by pulse length; fluorescence readout; the count in one level fluctuates with variance N p(1−p): zero at p = 0 and p = 1 (the certainty cases), largest at p = ½ (about ±5 ions in 100). E4: single-ion Rabi flopping, the fraction against pulse length with binomial error bars. E6 in one sentence as the original two-outcome spin sorter. Idealizations: perfect preparation and readout, independent ions, photon-counting noise removed as Itano et al. did.

**Classical candidates ruled out, and by what.** Each ion partly in each level and glowing a fixed share (a classical dipole picture): predicts a steady count with no N p(1−p) scatter; killed by the projection-noise measurement. Tickets: match any frequency by construction, not ruled out by counts alone; Bell for pairs. Classical coin with bias p: the template (law of large numbers), silent on p.

**Models.** *Model 1: the frequency operator first (Hartle; FGG), as "the counter is an observable".* Start from certainty: all-up ions give reading 1 with no scatter, all-down give 0; in the formalism these are the sure-bet lists (eigenvectors) of the counter with readings (eigenvalues) 1 and 0, and the certainty rule is the only probabilistic statement kept. Build the counter from the single-ion "is it up?" matrix diag(0,1); write it for N = 2 as a 4×4 diagonal matrix; readings m/N; the list Ψ_N; split it into the typical part (|m/N − p| ≤ ε) and the deviant part; compute what the squared length of the deviant part does (operator trick → p(1−p)/N → Chebyshev → 0); the p = 0.1, N = 4 example where the reading 0.1 does not even exist. Then the limit: FGG's infinite-copy space, the orthogonality-of-products calculation (30° vs 31°, 10⁵ copies, overlap 2.4×10⁻⁷), the eigenvector claim in the sense FGG define, and the certainty rule ⇒ frequency = |c₁|². Then the which-norm check (fact 6) as the key insight: the argument's exponent is the norm's exponent; connect to the earlier answers (the 2-norm is the one continuous lossless change conserves). Why this model: it is what "frequencies come from the formalism" means. ### Strategy C: Every branch of the build-up (Everett's measure and the maverick worlds)

**Experiments.** E5: the Hitachi build-up, electrons one at a time through a biprism, frames at 10, 100, 3000, 20 000, 70 000 electrons; the fraction landing in a chosen stripe region settles to a definite value (two-outcome reading: in the region or not); fewer than one electron in flight at a time; Bach et al. 2013 with masked slits. E6 in one sentence for the spin version that the many-worlds literature uses. Idealizations: identical preparation of each electron, perfect detection, a fixed region.

**Classical candidates ruled out, and by what.** Tiny balls through one slit each: no dark stripes (earlier answers); for the frequency question, a ticket per electron matches any frequencies by construction and is not ruled out by counts alone. Classical wave: no dots. Ordinary probability: the template.

**Models.** *Model 1: the many-worlds measure (Everett 1957; DeWitt 1970; Graham 1973).* Intuition: every arrival splits the world; after N electrons there are 2^N branches, one per string; draw the tree for N = 3 with amplitudes c₀^(3−m) c₁^m and weights (p = 0.8: 0.512, 0.128, 0.032, 0.008 per branch; by frequency 0.512, 0.384, 0.096, 0.008). Everett's derivation of the weight, every step (fact 8), with where the 2 enters marked. Then the maverick-branch weight ≤ p(1−p)/(Nε²) → 0 (operator trick shown compactly), and DeWitt's claim. Why this model: built to explain chance when every outcome occurs. Pros: the measure is forced among amplitude-functions by additivity; the tail argument is three lines. Cons (contested, labelled): branch counting gives frequency ≈ ½ for any p (N = 20, p = 0.8: count peaks at m = 10, weight at m = 16; N = 4, p = 0.9: 11 of 16 branches by count but weight 0.052 have frequency ≤ ½); maverick branches exist; measure is not obviously probability (Kent 1990; Barrett 1999); supporters moved to decision theory and envariance. *Model 2: the single-world frequency operator (Finkelstein, Hartle, FGG)*: the same tree, the same tail, read with the certainty rule instead of "all branches exist": the counter's eigenvector claim in the limit; Squires and Caves–Schack. *Model 3: the Born postulate* (and Gleason as why it is the only consistent one). **How they relate:** one algebra, three readings; the exponent in all three is the norm's (Everett's a′ = √Σ|a_i|², Hartle's squared lengths); relations table including envariance, decision theory, typicality.

**Figures.** `c-branch-tree`: the N = 3 tree for p = 0.8 (labelled "illustrative region"), eight leaves with string, amplitude and weight, grouped by frequency 0, ⅓, ⅔, 1 with group weights 0.008, 0.096, 0.384, 0.512; caption: all eight branches present; weights total 1; the frequency 0.8 is not available at N = 3. `c-count-vs-weight`: N = 20, p = 0.8; two bar charts against m/20: the share of branches by number, C(20,m)/2^20, peaking at ½, and the weight w_m, peaking at 0.8; caption: by count most worlds see a frequency near ½, by weight near 0.8; which "most" means is the dispute.

**Opening sentence.** "In the Hitachi experiment electrons arrived one at a time, 10, then 100, then 3000, 20 000, 70 000 of them, and the fraction landing in any chosen stripe settled on a definite number; in the many-worlds reading each arrival splits the world, so after N electrons there are 2^N branches, one for every sequence of in-the-stripe and outside, and the frequency-operator argument in its Everett–DeWitt–Graham form is the claim that the branches whose fraction disagrees with the squared amplitude carry a total weight that shrinks to nothing."
