Quantum mechanics is the physics that explains what ordinary matter is and why it holds together: why atoms exist at all, why every hydrogen atom in the universe is identical, and why each element glows in its own particular colors. It is the theory of electrons, atoms and light, and chemistry and modern technology rest on it. The best way in is the atom, where the older physics failed.

Experiments show an atom is a tiny, heavy, positive nucleus with much lighter, negative electrons around it. Classical physics, superb for everything from motors to the solar system, says the electrons must circle the nucleus like planets around the Sun, or they would fall straight in.

Then it predicts disaster. A charge that keeps changing direction radiates light (that's how radio antennas broadcast), so the circling electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in about a hundred-billionth of a second. And since orbits could be any size, no two atoms would match, and glowing gas would give off a smooth smear of all colors.

Nature disagrees on every count. Atoms don't collapse. All hydrogen atoms are identical. And glowing hydrogen gives off only a few sharp colors, like a barcode, not a rainbow.

Quantum mechanics replaces the orbit with a pattern of amplitudes: a number at every point around the nucleus, a bit like the height of a wave there. The size of that number, squared, gives the chance of finding the electron near that point if you look. That map of chances is the "electron cloud"; each look finds the whole electron at one spot.

The crucial rule: only certain patterns are steady, keeping their shape with one definite energy. Compare a guitar string. With its ends pinned, it rings steadily only in patterns that fit: one hump, two humps, three, each with its own note.

The comparison has limits: nothing material vibrates in an atom, and hydrogen's energies crowd together toward the top, while a string's frequencies are evenly spaced. But the nucleus's pull plays the part of the pinned ends: only certain steady patterns fit, and their energies form a ladder. Measure the electron's energy and you always get one of the rungs, never anything in between. Physics, not history, sets the ladder, so all hydrogen atoms are identical.

That explains the barcode. When the electron drops to a lower rung, the atom gives off one photon, a packet of light carrying away the energy difference. The one equation here, $E = hf$, says a photon's energy $E$ is a fixed number $h$ (Planck's constant) times its frequency $f$, which your eye sees as color. So each gap makes one sharp color, and bigger drops make bluer light: rung 3 to rung 2 gives hydrogen's red line (wavelength 656 nanometers), rung 4 to rung 2 its blue-green one. Each element has its own ladder and so its own barcode, which is how astronomers recognize hydrogen in stars.

So why doesn't the electron fall in? Squeezing the pattern closer to the nucleus would let the electron feel more pull, but a wave squeezed into less space wiggles more sharply, which in quantum mechanics means more energy of motion. The bottom rung is the best balance of the two; this trade-off is the uncertainty principle at work, a property of waves, not of clumsy measurement. An electron on a higher rung soon drops anyway, by giving off light, but from the bottom rung there is nowhere lower to go.

So the solar-system picture has to go. Quantum mechanics gives the electron no path: in hydrogen's lowest state the cloud is a round ball and nothing goes around. Yet the electron isn't at rest: measure its speed and you'd find it moving fast, because squeezing gives it energy of motion. (What it does between looks, if anything, is where interpretations of quantum mechanics differ.)

That is what quantum mechanics is about: matter described by patterns of amplitude rather than paths, energy ladders for anything trapped, and clouds of chance rather than certainties. Add one more quantum rule, that no two electrons can be in exactly the same state, so bigger atoms must fill their ladders upward, and you get the periodic table, chemical bonds, why solids are solid, and the transistors, LEDs and lasers of modern technology. Its predictions have been tested to extraordinary precision; what it means underneath is still debated.
