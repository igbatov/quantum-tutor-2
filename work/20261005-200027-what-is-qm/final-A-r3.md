Quantum mechanics is the theory of how matter and light behave; its effects are most obvious at the scale of atoms and smaller. A good way to see what it is about is one experiment: fire electrons, one at a time, at a wall with two narrow slits, and record where each one lands.

Behind the slits, a screen flashes where an electron hits. The beam is so faint that there is almost never more than one electron in flight at a time, so the pattern can't come from electrons bumping into each other. Before reading on, guess what pattern builds up.

If you pictured tiny balls, each going through one slit or the other, you probably expected a broad spread of hits with no stripes. Here is what real experiments find. Each electron does arrive whole, as one dot, at a spot nobody can predict; quantum mechanics gives only the chances. But as thousands of dots accumulate, stripes appear: bands where many electrons land, separated by bands where almost none do.

![Computer simulation of the ideal two-slit pattern building up, one dot per electron, after 10, 100, 1,000 and 10,000 electrons. Notice that each electron lands whole, as one dot at an unpredictable spot, and the stripes show up only in the pattern of many dots.](figures/a-buildup.png)

Now close one slit. The narrow stripes are replaced by one wide bright band, with much fainter bands further out. Comparing the two patterns reveals something strange: opening the second slit made some spots almost unreachable. Take a spot in the first dark stripe beside the centre. With one slit open, electrons land there often (in the set-up plotted below, 95% as often as at the wide band's peak); with both open, almost never. If each electron went through one slit and wasn't affected by whether the other was open, opening the second slit could only add hits there, never remove them.

![Calculated chance of landing at each spot, for an ideal set-up: identical slits spaced four slit-widths apart (centre to centre) and a distant screen. Top: grey is one slit open; dashed is the two one-slit chances simply added, which is also what an ideal which-slit detector gives; bold is both slits open with nothing recording the slit. At the dark stripes (arrows) the bold curve falls to zero in this ideal calculation, and to nearly zero in real experiments, though one slit alone gives 95% or 62% of its peak there (circles). At the bright stripes the bold curve rises above the dashed one (twice as high at the centre); over the whole screen the totals are equal, so what the dark stripes lose, the bright ones gain. Bottom: the one-slit and ideal which-slit curves over a wider range, magnified about 18 times: beyond the wide central band, dark gaps alternate with much fainter side bands, the brightest about 5% of the centre's height.](figures/a-one-vs-two.png)

The rule that fits: quantum mechanics gives every way an electron could reach a spot an *amplitude*, a number that, like the height of a wave, can be positive or negative (in the full theory, a complex number). Each slit has a width, so there are infinitely many ways through it. When no record tells the ways apart, you add up the amplitudes for all the ways through the left slit into one combined amplitude, do the same for the right slit, and add the two. That is the sum over every way, just sorted by slit (ignoring ways that weave back and forth through both slits, which change the result here only very slightly). The square of the total's size sets the chance of landing there. Amplitudes of +1 and −1 add to 0: no chance at all. Amplitudes of +1 and +1 add to 2, which squares to 4, twice the 1 + 1 = 2 you'd get by adding each slit's own chance. A way's amplitude depends on its length, and most spots are nearer one slit than the other, so at some spots the two combined amplitudes cancel (completely in an ideal set-up, almost completely in a real one): the dark stripes. Where they match, they reinforce: the bright stripes. This adding and cancelling is called interference.

The ways through one slit interfere with each other too. In the ideal set-up plotted above, their amplitudes add up to a large total near the middle of the screen, making the wide bright band, and cancel much more farther out: completely at the dark gaps, partly in the much fainter side bands (bottom panel).

Noise-cancelling headphones use the same arithmetic: they play a sound whose air pressure is high whenever the noise's is low, and low whenever it is high, so the two largely cancel and a steady hum, like an engine's, becomes much quieter. But sound is a wave in air; an amplitude is not a wave in any material, and no detector ever finds part of an electron.

Last twist: let a detector record which slit each electron uses. If the record reliably tells the slits apart, the narrow stripes disappear; a fuzzy record only fades them. With a reliable record, you add the two slits' chances instead of their combined amplitudes, so their contributions no longer cancel each other: in an ideal set-up, where the detector does nothing else to the electron, you get exactly the two one-slit patterns added together, much like the broad spread you probably first guessed. Even one stray photon bouncing off the electron can make such a record, and nobody ever has to read it. "Observing" here means any physical record, not a mind.

That one experiment shows much of what quantum mechanics is about:

- **Lumps.** Electrons, and light too, are detected only in whole units (quanta, hence the theory's name), even when they build a wave-like pattern.
- **Chances.** The theory predicts how likely each outcome is; unless one outcome is certain, it can't tell you which will happen.
- **Amplitudes.** Chances come from amplitudes that add and cancel. Applied to electrons in atoms, the same rules set atoms' allowed energies and how they bond: the basis of chemistry, transistors and lasers.
- **Records.** Possibilities can't interfere while there is a reliable record of which one happened. Air and light bouncing off big objects constantly record where they are, washing out their interference almost instantly; well-isolated molecules of about two thousand atoms still make stripes.

You'll often hear that the electron "goes through both slits" or "is in two places at once". That goes beyond the theory, which says only that both slits' combined amplitudes shape where the electron lands. Interpretations of the theory disagree about what the electron does in between, and about what measurement does to it. In one, it follows a single definite path, steered by amplitudes from both slits; in another, there is no fact about which slit it used unless something records it. All of them predict the same dots and stripes, the pattern experiments find.
