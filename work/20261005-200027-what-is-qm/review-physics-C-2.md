## Issues
- [major] "why every hydrogen atom in the universe is identical" (also line 9 "All hydrogen atoms are identical" and line 17 "so all hydrogen atoms are identical") — False as written. Deuterium and tritium atoms are hydrogen too, and their ladders are slightly shifted; that shift is how deuterium was discovered in 1932. Two hydrogen atoms on different rungs, or in different hyperfine states of the bottom rung, are also not in identical states. What is true, and what the argument needs, is that every atom of the same kind has the same ladder. Fix: line 3 "why every atom of ordinary hydrogen, anywhere in the universe, has exactly the same set of allowed energies"; line 9 "Every hydrogen atom has the same ladder of energies, wherever it is"; line 17 "Physics, not history, sets the ladder, so every ordinary hydrogen atom has the same one."
- [major] "Yet the electron isn't at rest: measure its speed and you'd find it moving fast" — Two problems. (1) "Isn't at rest" is a claim about what the electron does between looks, which the next sentence says is interpretation-dependent. In the de Broglie–Bohm interpretation, the electron in hydrogen's ground state is literally at rest. (2) "You'd find it moving fast" is an absolute. A speed measurement gives a spread of results, typically large but sometimes small. "Quantum mechanics gives the electron no path" has the same interpretation problem: standard quantum mechanics assigns no path, but Bohmian mechanics does. Fix: "Standard quantum mechanics gives the electron no path… Yet if you measure its speed you typically get a large value, around 1% of light speed, because squeezing gives it energy of motion. (What it does between looks, if anything, is where interpretations differ.)"
- [major] "Measure the electron's energy and you always get one of the rungs, never anything in between" — Two idealizations are left unnamed. (1) The claim holds only while the electron stays bound. Above zero ("electron set free", which C's own figure shows) any energy is allowed. (2) The −13.6/n² ladder is the simple model. In finer detail, most rungs split into very closely spaced sub-rungs (fine structure, Lamb shift, hyperfine), so "the rungs" are not the exact measured values. Fix: "As long as the electron stays bound, measuring its energy gives one of the rungs, never anything in between. (Looked at very closely, most rungs split into a few closely spaced sub-rungs, but the ladder stays a ladder.)"
- [major] "glowing hydrogen gives off only a few sharp colors" plus the figure strip "glowing hydrogen, as actually seen" with exactly four lines — Overstated, and the figure's window hides contrary features. Hydrogen's visible series does not stop at four lines. Fainter lines continue at 397, 389, 384 nm and so on, crowding toward about 365 nm, and further whole series lie in the ultraviolet and infrared. The 400–700 nm strip, captioned "as actually seen", implies the black background holds nothing else. Fix: text "glowing hydrogen gives off a few bright, sharp colors, like a barcode, not a rainbow"; strip title "glowing hydrogen: its four brightest visible lines (fainter ones continue into the violet and ultraviolet)". Optionally add a small arrow at the 400 nm edge labelled "more lines →UV".
- [major] "When the electron drops to a lower rung, the atom gives off one photon" — False as an absolute. The 2s→1s drop in hydrogen happens by emitting two photons, and in a gas an atom can also lose energy to collisions without emitting light. The same applies to line 23's "drops anyway, by giving off light". Relatedly, "So each gap makes one sharp color" is false for gaps whose light is ultraviolet or infrared, as C's own figure notes for rung 2→1. Fix: "When the electron drops to a lower rung by giving off light, the atom usually emits a single photon, a packet of light carrying away the energy difference… So each gap makes light of one sharp frequency, a color if it falls in the visible range."
- [major] "while a string's frequencies are evenly spaced" — This holds only for an ideal, perfectly flexible string. A real guitar string's stiffness makes its higher overtones slightly sharp, so the spacing is not exactly even. The idealization is unnamed. Fix: "while an ideal string's frequencies are evenly spaced (a real string's very nearly so)".
- [minor] "this trade-off is the uncertainty principle at work" — Right in spirit, and the standard heuristic. A rigorous proof that hydrogen is stable needs a related but stronger wave inequality than Heisenberg's relation. Fix: "this trade-off is closely related to the uncertainty principle: a property of waves, not of clumsy measurement."
- [minor] "how many different colors of light, at most, could it give off" — Some drops give invisible (ultraviolet) light, so "colors" can make a careful learner hesitate. The answer of 3 still stands. Fix: "how many different kinds of light (different frequencies, visible or not), at most…"

## Sound points worth keeping
- The numbers are correct: classical collapse time of about 10^-11 s, the −13.6/n² eV ladder, Balmer lines at 656.3/486.1/434.0/410.2 nm, and Lyman-alpha in the UV. The guitar analogy states its limits (nothing material vibrates; the rungs crowd together). The standing-wave figure is correct.
- The Born rule is stated plainly ("each look finds the whole electron"). Uncertainty is framed as a wave property, not as disturbance. The ground state is a round cloud with no orbit. Interpretations are kept out of the established physics, apart from the "isn't at rest" sentence flagged above.
